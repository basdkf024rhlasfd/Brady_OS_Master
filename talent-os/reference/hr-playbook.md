# Reference — HR & Talent Playbook

The frameworks and compliance lenses Hunter applies across skills. Brandon knows this material;
this file exists so the *agent* applies it consistently without being re-told each session.

---

## 1. Structured interviewing (the backbone of everything)

Structured interviews — same questions, same rubric, same order, scored independently — are the
best-validated predictor of job performance short of a work-sample, and they're the strongest
legal defense you have. Every interview guide this kit produces is structured.

**The rubric is anchored, not vibes.** Each competency scored 1–5 with *behavioral anchors*:

- **5 — Strong yes:** clear, specific evidence they exceed the bar (example given, outcome owned).
- **3 — Mixed / meets:** some evidence, some gaps; would need support.
- **1 — Strong no:** evidence they fall short, or no relevant evidence at all.

Score each competency *before* discussing the candidate as a whole (independent ratings first,
then calibrate). Never average a gut "vibe" into the number.

**Question types:**
- **Behavioral (STAR):** "Tell me about a time you…" → probe **S**ituation, **T**ask, **A**ction,
  **R**esult. Past behavior predicts future behavior.
- **Situational:** "What would you do if…" → useful when the candidate lacks direct experience
  (great for non-traditional hires).
- **Work-sample / role-play:** have them *do* a slice of the job (the sell-me-something video, a
  mock greeting). Highest signal of all.
- Avoid: brainteasers, trivia, "what's your greatest weakness," anything that tests interview
  polish rather than job ability.

## 2. Scorecard-first hiring

Define **what good looks like on paper and in person before you look at a single candidate.** A
scorecard lists 4–6 must-have competencies + the knockouts + the "nice-to-haves," each with how
it's assessed and by whom. This is what the retail team was missing — "every manager is
different" is a scorecard problem. The scorecard is the contract between recruiter, hiring
manager, and the AI screener: everyone sorts against the same definition.

## 3. The talent funnel & the metrics that matter

```
Source → Apply → Screen → Interview → Offer → Accept → Onboard → Ramp → Productive → Retain
```

Instrument these; they tell you *where* to intervene:

| Metric | What it exposes |
|---|---|
| **Time-to-fill / time-to-first-contact** | Follow-through speed; the #1 volume-hiring killer |
| **Source effectiveness** (apply→hire→retain by source) | Where to spend the sponsorship budget |
| **Screen→interview and interview→offer rates** | Whether the posting/screen sets right expectations |
| **Offer-accept rate** | Comp competitiveness / candidate experience |
| **Quality of hire** (ramp attainment, 90-day retention, performance) | Whether you're hiring the *right* people, not just filling seats |
| **Early attrition (0–90 / 0–180 day)** | Onboarding + expectation-setting + role reality |
| **Cost per hire** | Efficiency; sanity-check against sponsorship spend |

Rule: **diagnose the leak before prescribing.** More top-of-funnel never fixes a mid-funnel or
retention leak — it just adds cost.

## 4. Job descriptions that convert

A JD is a marketing asset with a compliance overlay, not a legal document. Good ones:

- **Lead with the candidate's win** (earnings, growth, what they'll get), not a company bio.
- **State the real terms up front** — schedule, comp model, location — so the wrong people
  self-select out *before* they consume interview time.
- **Sell honestly.** Concrete, believable earning ranges beat vague "competitive pay."
- **Strip the gates.** Remove degree/experience requirements that aren't truly job-required.
- **Are scannable, inclusive, and boring to lawyers** — no coded language, no protected-class
  proxies, ADA-compliant essential functions, EEO statement present.
- **Are optimized for the board** — searchable title (what candidates type, not internal
  jargon), keyworded, front-loaded.

## 5. EEOC / employment-law lenses (apply to every artifact)

Hunter is **not a lawyer** and flags material calls for review rather than resolving them. But it
applies these lenses automatically:

- **Protected classes** (federal floor): race, color, national origin, sex (incl. pregnancy,
  sexual orientation, gender identity), religion, age 40+, disability, genetic info. States/locals
  add more. Never screen on these or on their **proxies** (graduation year → age; "recent grad" →
  age; photos/names → race; health/absence history → disability; "culture fit" used loosely → bias).
- **Job-relatedness & consistency** are the twin defenses: any requirement, question, knockout, or
  score must be job-related and applied the same way to everyone.
- **Disparate impact:** a neutral rule (a test, a degree requirement, an assessment cutoff) that
  disproportionately screens out a protected group is unlawful unless job-related and validated.
  This is why we prefer work-samples and structured rubrics over generic tests and pedigree gates.
- **Interview do-not-ask:** age/DOB, marital/family/childcare status, pregnancy/plans, religion,
  national origin/citizenship (beyond work authorization), disability/health/workers-comp,
  arrest records (as distinct from convictions, which are restricted), genetic/family medical.
- **Automated screening (AI resume ranking, phone/video AI):** rising regulation (e.g. NYC Local
  Law 144-style bias-audit rules, EEOC guidance on AI, Illinois/Maryland video-interview consent
  laws, state ADMT rules). Requirements to bake in: **notice/consent, a bias audit or
  monitoring, a job-related basis, accommodation on request, and a human in every adverse
  decision.** Never let an automated knockout be a protected-class proxy.
- **Adverse action:** if a background check drives a rejection (FCRA), the pre-adverse /
  adverse-action notice sequence applies. Keep humans in that loop.

## 6. Onboarding & ramp (retention starts pre-day-one)

- **30/60/90** structure: 30 = learn (product, process, systems, first reps), 60 = do (own the
  motion with support), 90 = perform (hitting ramped targets). Each phase gets goals, enablement,
  and a check-in.
- **Ramp-to-productivity** is a curve, not a date. Design comp/support to cover the gap between
  start and self-sustaining earnings, or you lose people at the cliff.
- **Early wins** predict retention. Engineer a win in week 1–2.
- **Manager cadence** (weekly 1:1s through ramp) beats any orientation deck.

## 7. Total rewards

Comp is one lever in a bundle: **base/ramp + variable (commission/bonus/attach) + benefits +
schedule/flexibility + growth + recognition.** When comp can't move, the other levers can. For
variable-pay roles, always check whether the incentive is pointed at the behaviors you actually
want (volume vs. margin vs. attach vs. retention) — mis-pointed incentives are common and quietly
expensive.

## 8. Culture & engagement

Culture is what the schedule, the comp plan, the manager behavior, and the recognition system
*actually reward* — not the values on the wall. Read it from evidence (interviews, surveys,
review-site themes, attrition reasons), name the real tensions (e.g. "we say work-life balance,
we require every weekend"), and fix the *system*, not the poster. Engagement action plans work
when they change one or two real things and close the loop with employees, not when they add a
pizza party.
