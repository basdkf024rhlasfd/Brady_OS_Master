# Reference — Hiring for Commissioned Retail / Furniture Sales

Domain knowledge Hunter reasons from when the role is a frontline, commissioned retail sales
job (the RSA archetype). Genericized — no client specifics. Pair with `company-context.md` for
this engagement's particulars.

## The core truth: sort for drive, not for pedigree

High-performing commissioned salespeople are **made, not credentialed.** Across furniture and
big-ticket retail, the strongest predictors of success are behavioral, not biographical:

- **Money-motivation** — they *want* commission, they treat a slow week as a personal problem
  to solve, they ask about the earning ceiling in the interview.
- **Hunger / resilience** — rejection doesn't dent them; they re-approach.
- **Warmth + read** — they build rapport fast and read what a buyer actually needs.
- **Coachability** — willing to run the process (greeting, needs discovery, add-ons, financing,
  the close) even when it feels scripted.

What does **not** predict: a four-year degree, prior furniture experience, a spotless resume, or
a high score on a generic cognitive/personality test. The best hires routinely come from
bartending, food service, cosmetology, fitness, hospitality — jobs that select for exactly the
behavioral traits above. **Design every step to let those people through, not to filter them out.**

## The two real knockouts

Most commissioned-sales mis-hires trace to two expectations set too late:

1. **Schedule.** Weekends and holidays are the revenue. If a candidate can't or won't work them,
   nothing else matters — and finding that out at the in-person interview wastes everyone's time.
2. **Commission comfort.** "100% commission after ramp" excites the right people and terrifies
   the wrong ones. Surface it at the top of the funnel, framed as *upside*, not as a warning.

**Screen these two, honestly and early** — in the posting, at apply-time, and in a 3-minute
first-contact screen. They are legitimate, job-related, non-discriminatory knockouts. Everything
else should be a *signal*, not a gate.

## Sourcing patterns that work

- **The job board is a marketplace.** In dense metros your posting competes with hundreds or
  thousands of others. Organic reach dies past page one; **sponsorship / boosting is the price
  of visibility**, not a nice-to-have. Budget for it deliberately by location and urgency.
- **Hiring events** (a concentrated, sponsored, time-boxed push) reliably fill volume roles fast
  when a location falls several heads short. Run them proactively against forecasted gaps, not
  reactively after the floor is bleeding.
- **Work-sample at apply-time.** A short "**sell us something — record a 60-second video**"
  prompt is the highest-signal, lowest-cost screen in retail sales. It reveals presence, energy,
  and instinct in a way no resume can, and it self-selects for people willing to do the work.
  Treat a completed, strong video as a *fast-track*, not a checkbox.
- **Poach proven writers.** Competitors publicly celebrate their top writers (award posts, "top
  performer" shout-outs). Those are a warm, named sourcing list for direct outreach.
- **Referrals from current top performers** over-index — good writers know other good writers and
  understand the schedule reality.

## The funnel (and where retail sales funnels leak)

```
Post → Apply → [work-sample video] → First screen → Manager interview → Offer → Ramp → Productive → Retained
```

Most common leaks, in order of how often they're the real problem:

1. **Middle-of-funnel follow-through.** Applicants pile up; nobody sources through them, calls,
   and schedules fast. Speed-to-first-contact is decisive — the best candidates are gone in days.
   *This is usually the #1 leak and it's an ops/automation problem, not a marketing problem.*
2. **Expectation mismatch at the interview.** No first screen → managers interview people who
   bail on schedule/commission. Fix upstream.
3. **Weak posting.** Under-sells earnings, buries or hides the schedule, reads like a generic
   retail req. Fix: lead with the money, state the terms, screen for motivation.
4. **Ramp cliff.** People quit in weeks 6–12 as the hourly ramp ends before their book produces.
   Fix: ramp design + early-win coaching + transparent earnings trajectory.
5. **Schedule burnout / no path.** Retained-writer problem — culture, recognition, and a
   career/earnings ladder, not a hiring problem.

Diagnose *which* leak before prescribing. A great posting doesn't fix a follow-through gap; more
applicants make a follow-through gap worse.

## Comp & the "money left on the table" lever

Ramp-then-commission plans (flat hourly for N weeks → commission + tiered bonuses + attach
bonuses on financing/credit-apps and protection plans) create a hidden performance spread:
**two people writing identical volume can earn very differently based on add-on discipline.**
That gap is simultaneously:

- a **coaching** opportunity (scorecards that show "you left \$X on the table"),
- an **onboarding/training** opportunity (build attach habits during ramp), and
- a **total-rewards** design question (are the incentives pointed at the right behaviors?).

When you touch comp for these roles, always look at attach/add-on behavior, not just base rate.

## Assessment: use work-samples, avoid friction that doesn't predict

Long pre-hire psychometric tests tend to (a) add funnel friction and drop-off, (b) fail to
predict commissioned-sales performance, and (c) carry adverse-impact/validation risk. Prefer:

- **Work-samples** (the sell-me-something video; a mock greeting/needs-discovery role-play at
  interview) — job-relevant, high-signal, defensible.
- **Structured behavioral questions** scored on a rubric (see `hr-playbook.md`).
- If a validated assessment is ever used, it must be **job-analysis-validated** and monitored for
  adverse impact — otherwise it's risk with no return.

## Compliance notes specific to high-volume retail hiring

- **No education/experience gates** unless truly job-required (they rarely are here) — they
  screen out your best non-traditional talent *and* create disparate-impact exposure.
- **Ban-the-box / criminal-history:** many states/localities restrict when and how you can ask;
  individualized assessment beats blanket bars. Keep any background standard job-related and
  consistent.
- **Video applications:** apply the same structured rubric to every video; judge sales behaviors,
  not appearance, accent, or home setting. Document the criteria so it's defensible.
- **Consistency is the defense.** Same questions, same rubric, same knockouts, documented, for
  every candidate in a role. Structure isn't just better hiring — it's your EEOC insurance.
