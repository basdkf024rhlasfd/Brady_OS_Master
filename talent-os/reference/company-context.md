## The company

A **top-50 U.S. furniture retailer** — a large, fast-growing Ashley HomeStore licensee
operating ~30 showroom locations plus outlet stores across the Southeast (FL, GA, NC, AL,
MS, VA). Vertically integrated last-mile: multiple distribution centers (GA, AL, NC, FL) and
its own delivery operation, with a central HQ support/call center.

- **Ownership:** fourth-generation family business; recently installed its first non-family
  President and is professionalizing the leadership team.
- **Trajectory:** record sales and profit; **actively acquisition-driven** — integrating new
  stores and adding a large block of employees (order of ~200+) as it scales. Operating
  consistency across fast-acquired locations is a recurring challenge.
- **Tech/AI posture:** leadership is leaning hard into AI and analytics. There's an internal
  build capability and an outside data/AI vendor. The People function is expected to
  modernize with automation, not just add headcount.

Why it matters for People & Talent: **growth + acquisition + a thin People team + a mandate
to automate** = the exact conditions where a sharp, AI-native TA/HR operator creates outsized
value fast.

## The People/HR function today

- **Thin and stretched.** Essentially a very small People Operations team (on the order of
  one-to-two people) covering HR, recruiting oversight, onboarding, coaching/discipline, and
  compliance across the whole multi-state footprint. One member is transitioning out; the
  function is being restructured with a new leader expected to expand it (community/employer-brand
  work included).
- **Already AI-curious.** The team has started building point automations with Claude — a
  handbook-aware coaching-writeup generator, a roster-reconciliation helper, drafting for HR
  complaints. These are promising but half-finished and unsystematic. Opportunity: turn
  scattered experiments into a repeatable, owned pipeline.
- **Blocker to watch:** new-hire IT provisioning and tool access can be slow (ticket-gated).
  Any automation that touches internal systems (ATS API, roster feeds) will move only as fast
  as access is granted — sequence quick wins that don't depend on it first.

## The hiring stack

- **ATS:** **Workable**, tied to **Indeed**. Workable syndicates a posting to ~17–20 job
  boards; the large majority of applicant flow comes from Indeed. Priority boosting
  (low/med/high) and paid sponsorship control visibility.
- **Job-board reality:** in dense metros a role can sit behind 1,000+ competing listings —
  page-one placement requires sponsorship. **Hiring events** on Indeed (~several hundred
  dollars each) reliably fill roles fast when a location falls 5+ short. Monthly recruiting
  budget is finite (order of ~$10K/mo).
- **Video application:** applicants get an immediate prompt to **"sell us your favorite
  product" and upload a short video.** This has become the single best early signal —
  it surfaces personality, hunger, and sales instinct better than a resume, and has produced
  strong hires who a resume screen would have passed on. Not every applicant completes it.
- **Resume fit-ranking:** Workable auto-scores resumes against the JD (e.g. "40% fit / 90%
  fit"), but the logic is opaque and not trusted — no one can explain the rankings, so they're
  largely ignored. (A transparent, tunable ranker is a clear opportunity.)
- **Assessment:** a paid pre-hire assessment (Criteria) was used for years and **discontinued**
  — it slowed the funnel, applicants dropped off during the 20-minute test, and it never
  reliably predicted sales performance. Lesson: don't reintroduce friction that doesn't predict.

## The recruiting model (and where it breaks)

- **History:** central recruiters (two of them) used to source, do the first screen, and hand
  finalists to store managers for the in-person interview. When a recruiter role was lost,
  **sourcing was pushed down to store managers.**
- **The break:** store managers *can* review applicants but **don't consistently work the
  pipeline** — they don't source through applications, call, and schedule. The candidate pool
  is generally fine; the bottleneck is the **middle of the funnel**: nobody reliably filters
  applications, reaches out, and books interviews. Good candidates die in the follow-up gap.
- **A second gap — mismatched expectations reach the interview.** When there's no consistent
  first screen, managers waste time interviewing people who bail once they learn the role is
  100% commission after ramp and requires weekends/holidays. The fix lives upstream: a clear
  posting + a light structured screen that surfaces schedule fit and commission comfort before
  a manager's time is spent.

## The RSA economics (discovery-stage — verify)

Frontline sales comp is a **ramp-then-commission** model:

- **Ramp:** a flat hourly wage (~\$20/hr) for the first ~8 weeks, then transition to commission.
  (Enough runway to build a book, get deliveries completed, and start earning.)
- **Commission + bonus:** a base commission rate on written sales, plus tiered bonuses tied to
  volume thresholds and **credit-application** counts, plus a **protection-plan attach**
  bonus that steps up sharply at higher attach rates.
- **Earnings:** average writers land roughly \$60–65K; strong writers well into six figures;
  the very best write \$1M+ in sales. **Two people writing the same volume can earn very
  differently** based purely on add-on discipline (credit apps, protection attach) — this
  "money left on the table" is a real coaching and total-rewards lever.
- **Outlets** pay near local minimum and earn much less — a structural retention problem worth
  its own look.

## The turnover driver

The dominant retention issue is the **schedule**: Saturdays, Sundays, and blackout holidays
are mandatory because they're the biggest sales days. Historically associates had more schedule
latitude; tightening it drove out some tenured writers and shows up in employer-review sites as
"no work-life balance." New hires now arrive with the expectation set — so the leverage is
**setting that expectation honestly at the top of the funnel** and **selling the money that
the schedule makes possible**, not hiding it.

## The winning profile (this is the money insight)

The best writers **do not** come from an obvious resume. A former hairdresser became a
\$1M writer; people out of bartending and food service consistently sell well. What predicts
success is **personality, motivation, and hunger** — not furniture experience, not a degree,
not a clean job-history. Practical implications:

- **Don't over-filter on pedigree.** Education requirements and job-history gates screen out
  the exact non-traditional talent that wins here (and create EEOC risk for no upside).
- **Weight the video and the drive signals** over the resume.
- **Screen for money-motivation and schedule fit** — the two real knockouts — early and honestly.

## The shared vision (what "great" looks like here)

The client and Brady already sketched the target state: a **repeatable, Claude-Code-built
pipeline** — pull applicants from the ATS/Indeed → an agent that *knows what good looks like*
ranks them by need and potential (transparently, not a black box) → serves a daily **"here are
the ones to reach out to today"** list → hands the store manager a **structured interview
guide** → the manager owns the hire decision. Plus automation around the edges: handbook-aware
coaching write-ups, roster/staffing reconciliation, and JD optimization. Talent OS is built to
deliver exactly this, one shippable piece at a time.
