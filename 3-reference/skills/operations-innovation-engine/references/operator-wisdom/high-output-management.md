# High Output Management

**Source:** Andy Grove, *High Output Management* (1983; still the reference)
**Core principle:** A manager's output equals the output of their team plus the output
of teams under their influence. Management is an operating discipline, not a seniority
reward.

## When to apply

- Managers spend time on tasks that don't scale their team
- 1:1s don't happen, or happen without structure
- Meetings consume calendars but decisions lag
- Metrics measure activity, not leverage
- Organizations grow headcount faster than output

## The framework

**Manager's output equation:**

```
Output = Output of team + Output of teams under influence
```

Everything a manager does is evaluated against this. If an activity doesn't increase
either term, it's waste.

**Leverage:**

A high-leverage activity affects many outputs at once. A low-leverage activity affects
one output. Always shift toward high-leverage.

Examples of high-leverage:
- Hiring decisions (one hire affects dozens of outputs over years)
- Training (especially at scale)
- Process design (every output passes through a process)
- Setting standards and expectations (affects every judgment call)
- 1:1s with direct reports (multiplies their effectiveness)

Examples of low-leverage:
- Doing work that could be delegated
- Attending a meeting with no decision
- Reviewing work one-at-a-time when a checklist would do

**Four types of managerial activity** (Grove's matrix):

|  | High Leverage | Low Leverage |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | Training, hiring, delegating, decision forcing | Doing the work yourself |
| **Negative** | Meddling, second-guessing, hesitation | Trivial bottlenecks |

**Production principles applied to management:**

- The limiting step: find the slowest, most constrained activity; run the rest at its pace
- Indicators that lead, not lag: monitor inputs, not just outputs
- Quality at the source: catch problems at origin, not in reporting
- The 1:1 is the manager's kanban — a pull signal from direct report to manager

**Task-relevant maturity (TRM):**

Management style should match the direct report's maturity *for that task*:
- Low TRM → structured, close supervision (what + when + how)
- Medium TRM → coaching, two-way discussion (what + when, help with how)
- High TRM → monitoring, delegation (what + when, trust the how)

Wrong style is the most common management failure. Micro-manage a senior and they
leave; hands-off a junior and they fail.

**Meetings are operating leverage or operating drag:**

- **Mission-oriented meetings:** decision needed; one owner, specific outcome
- **Process-oriented meetings:** recurring, predictable; 1:1s, staff, ops review
- **Any meeting that's neither** is almost certainly waste

## Canonical example

Intel's culture (1970s-1990s) ran on HOM. One-on-ones were mandatory. Managers
graded on their team's output + their "shadow" across adjacent teams. Grove
personally conducted CEO staff 1:1s weekly. Intel's hypergrowth through the
microprocessor era is often credited to the operating discipline — quality wasn't
enforced with more QA headcount, it was baked into every manager's leverage portfolio.

## Applied to QSR / retail / ops

- **Store manager leverage:** Training a shift lead well (2 hours/week) multiplies
  across every shift they run. Doing one shift leader's job (8 hours) is the
  opposite of leverage.
- **DMs and ops leadership:** The 1:1 cadence with store GMs *is* the operating
  system. If it's sporadic, the whole field runs on exceptions.
- **Output = team + influence:** A regional VP's output includes peer teams they
  influence (supply chain, IT, HR) — not just their stores. Reward accordingly.
- **Indicators that lead:** In a restaurant, speed-of-service and mystery shop
  scores lead sales by weeks. Ops leaders who only look at sales are driving with
  the rear view.

## Common failure modes

- Confusing activity with output. Full calendar ≠ high output.
- Equal treatment instead of TRM-appropriate treatment. Not every report gets the
  same style; not every task gets the same depth.
- Skipping 1:1s during busy periods. Exactly when 1:1s are most needed.
- Building headcount to solve problems that process redesign would solve.
