# Bezos Shareholder Letters — Distilled

**Source:** Amazon annual shareholder letters 1997-2020; collected in *Invent and Wander* (2021)
**Core principle:** Stay on Day 1 forever. Decide fast on reversible decisions. Be
obsessed with customers, not competitors. Defend long-term focus against short-term
pressure.

## When to apply

- Leadership is drifting from customer focus to competitor-watching
- Decisions take weeks that should take hours
- Incentives reward quarter-over-quarter optics over long-term investment
- The company is confusing process with thinking
- "Proxies" (metrics, process, form) have replaced substance

## The framework

**Day 1 vs. Day 2:**

Day 1 is the mindset of a startup — customer obsession, experimentation speed, willingness
to be misunderstood. Day 2 is "stasis, followed by irrelevance, followed by excruciating
painful decline, followed by death."

Day 1 defenders:
- True customer obsession (not "customer centricity" — obsession)
- Resist proxies (form masquerading as substance)
- Embrace external trends early
- High-velocity decision making

**Type 1 vs. Type 2 decisions:**

- **Type 1 (one-way door):** Irreversible or very expensive to reverse. Methodical,
  careful, consultative.
- **Type 2 (two-way door):** Reversible. Decide fast. If wrong, walk back through.

Most decisions are Type 2. Most orgs treat them like Type 1, which slows everything
and burns capital.

**High-velocity decision making:**

1. Most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you wish you had.
   Waiting for 90% is too slow.
2. Disagree and commit. Say your view, lose, commit. Don't slow-roll a decision.
3. Recognize true misalignments early and escalate immediately. Don't let teams
   exhaust each other in alignment.
4. The question isn't "is this a good decision?" but "is this a good decision-making
   *process*?"

**Fixed-cost vs. variable-cost businesses:**

Amazon's retail bet is to turn fixed costs (infrastructure, fulfillment centers) into
operating leverage through volume. AWS's bet is similar — build once, serve everyone.
The strategic question: where can we take a fixed-cost position that pays back 10 years
from now?

**Long-term orientation as a strategic weapon:**

- Most competitors optimize next quarter
- Capital markets reward 5-10 year horizons if you commit and deliver
- "Gazelle Project" — source of the "willing to be misunderstood" phrase
- "Your margin is my opportunity" — Amazon took low margins on purpose to acquire
  customers who would compound over decades

**"It's always Day 1" operating habits:**

- Hire builders, not maintainers
- Write narratives, not decks
- Small teams, clear ownership
- Experiments as first-class work
- "We are stubborn on vision. We are flexible on details."

## Canonical example

Amazon Web Services was a Day 1 bet. Internal tools turned into a platform turned into
a $100B+ business. The decision to sell internal capabilities externally in 2006 was
a Type 1 decision (hard to reverse — once customers depend on it, it's a real business
forever). It was made methodically, over years.

The 1-Click patent was a Type 2 decision that turned out Type 1: worth defending.
Fire Phone was a Type 2 decision that failed cheaply; learnings rolled into Echo.

## Applied to QSR / retail / ops

- **Most ops pilots are Type 2.** They can be rolled back. Launch faster.
- **Fixed-cost position:** Modular restaurant construction. Commissary kitchens. AI
  site selection. All fixed-cost now, leverage at 500+ locations. Treat as such.
- **Customer obsession beats competitor-watching.** If Panda keeps watching CFA,
  Panda ends up building a worse CFA. If Panda obsesses over its own customer's job,
  Panda finds the moves CFA can't make.
- **Resist proxies.** "We ran 40 focus groups" is a proxy. "We watched 10 customers
  order and eat" is the actual thing.
- **Decide fast:** A pilot approval that takes 6 weeks is a signal the decision is
  being treated as Type 1 when it's Type 2.

## Common failure modes

- Copying tactics (memos, two-pizza teams) without the underlying principles.
- Using "long-term" to justify ignoring current problems.
- Mistaking boldness for recklessness. Type 1 decisions still deserve care.
- Confusing being "misunderstood" with being wrong. The phrase "willing to be
  misunderstood" doesn't apply to bad ideas.
