# Hoshin Kanri — Policy Deployment

**Source:** Toyota (1960s-); popularized in the West via Thomas Jackson, Pascal Dennis
**Core principle:** 3-5 year breakthrough objectives cascade through the organization
via an X-matrix, with monthly review cadence and catchball (two-way negotiation) at
every level.

## When to apply

- Strategy exists on paper but doesn't show up in daily work
- Teams set their own priorities, disconnected from corporate objectives
- Annual planning produces long lists that nobody can execute
- Leadership says "alignment" a lot; employees report the opposite
- 100-item OKR lists that blur every quarter

## The framework

**Four-level cascade:**

1. **Hoshin (breakthrough objective)** — 3-5 year, bold, transformational. E.g.,
   "Become #1 in customer satisfaction in every market we serve."
2. **Annual objective** — this year's contribution to the breakthrough. "Cut average
   order wait time from 8 minutes to 5 minutes."
3. **Improvement priorities** — specific processes to attack. "Redesign drive-thru
   to enable dual-lane ordering."
4. **Daily / weekly action** — what each team does each week to move the improvement.

Each level has fewer items than the level below, forcing prioritization. Typically:
3-5 hoshins → 5-8 annual objectives → 10-15 priorities → team-level actions.

**The X-matrix:**

A single-page document with four sides:

```
     [Annual Objectives]
           |
[Processes]—X—[Improvement Priorities]
           |
     [Measures / Targets]
```

Each corner shows the relationships between:
- Long-term strategy (top)
- Annual execution (right)
- Metrics (bottom)
- Processes / responsible teams (left)

One page forces ruthless prioritization.

**Catchball:**

Two-way negotiation between levels. Leadership proposes objectives; teams negotiate
what's feasible. Result: a committed plan, not an imposed one. Usually takes 4-6
weeks in the annual cycle.

**Review cadence:**

- **Monthly:** Each priority reviewed against targets. Red/yellow/green.
- **Quarterly:** Annual objectives reviewed. Countermeasures for red items.
- **Annual:** Hoshin reviewed. Adjust the 3-5 year arc if needed.

**PDCA at every level:**

Plan → Do → Check → Act. The check and act steps are what most companies skip.

## Canonical example

Toyota's quality hoshin (1980s-): reduce defects per 100 vehicles by 50%. Cascaded
into every plant, every line, every team. Measured monthly. Took a decade. Produced
the quality lead that enabled the Lexus launch and the Camry's dominance.

Hewlett-Packard, Xerox, and many US companies adopted Hoshin Kanri in the 1990s with
mixed results — mostly because they took the X-matrix without the catchball or the
review cadence.

## Applied to QSR / retail / ops

- **3-year hoshin for a chain:** "Cut cost-to-build a new store by 40% while
  increasing construction velocity by 2x." Everything flows from that.
- **Annual objectives:** Each year's piece — "Pilot modular construction," "Build
  permit playbook," "Standardize kitchen module" — with specific targets.
- **Monthly review:** Every priority rated red/yellow/green against target. No
  narrative, no story — just the numbers and countermeasures for red.
- **Catchball is load-bearing.** Without it, the plan is imposed. Teams don't commit.
  Execution lags.
- **X-matrix on the wall.** Literally. Print it, post it, update it. One page that
  every operator knows cold.

## Common failure modes

- Treating Hoshin as annual planning. It's a 3-5 year discipline with annual cycles.
- Skipping catchball in the name of speed. The commitment is what makes execution work.
- Too many priorities. 10-15 is a ceiling, not a floor.
- Monthly reviews that become status reports instead of countermeasure sessions. The
  point is to fix the reds, not admire the greens.
- Treating the X-matrix as a compliance artifact. It's an operating document.
