# Jobs to Be Done — Applied to Ops

**Source:** Clayton Christensen / Bob Moesta; extended by Tony Ulwick (Outcome-Driven
Innovation)
**Core principle:** Customers don't buy products; they hire them to do a job. The same
logic applies *inside* the organization — employees, suppliers, and internal
stakeholders are hiring your systems and processes to do jobs too.

## When to apply

- Internal process changes fail because operators "don't adopt" them
- Tech investments (POS, KDS, scheduling tools) don't deliver the promised benefits
- Suppliers behave in ways that work against your interests
- "User experience" is treated as an external concept only
- Change management is framed as "overcoming resistance" instead of solving a job

## The framework

**Classic JTBD (for external customers):**

A "job" is the progress someone is trying to make in a specific circumstance.
- Functional dimension (speed, cost, convenience)
- Social dimension (how others perceive me)
- Emotional dimension (how I feel about myself)

**The question:** "When [circumstance], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."

Example: "When I'm driving home after a long day, I want a fast meal that doesn't
require decisions, so I can get home to my kids faster." — Panda Express competes
with every other QSR on this job; it also competes with HelloFresh, meal prep, and
takeout from a non-chain.

**JTBD for internal stakeholders:**

Same framework, different hirer:

- **Crew member's job:** "When I start a shift, I want to know exactly what's
  expected, so I can perform without stress and get through the shift successfully."
- **GM's job:** "When I'm running a store during a rush, I want real-time visibility
  into labor, throughput, and queue length, so I can move one person without killing
  another station."
- **Supplier's job:** "When you issue a PO, I want a predictable forecast 2-4 weeks
  out, so I can optimize my production runs and avoid emergency premiums."
- **DM's job:** "When I visit a store, I want to know in 10 minutes whether it's on
  track, so I can spend the rest of my visit on problems instead of scanning."
- **Construction PM's job:** "When a new store enters the pipeline, I want
  standardized specs and clear permit playbooks, so I can run 10 builds in parallel
  instead of 3."

**Outcome metrics (Ulwick):**

For each job, identify the "desired outcomes" — specific, measurable, stable over
time. Then rate each outcome on importance and satisfaction today. The gap (high
importance, low satisfaction) is the innovation target.

## Canonical example

Christensen / Moesta's milkshake study (McDonald's, early 2000s): the job was "help
me survive my morning commute" — not "nutritious breakfast." The competition was
bagels and bananas, not other beverages. The insight reshaped the product (thicker,
lasting longer, harder to spill).

A hospital that reframed nurse scheduling around the nurse's JTBD (predictability,
family planning, career growth) instead of hospital capacity. Retention doubled;
agency costs dropped 40%.

## Applied to QSR / retail / ops

- **Crew JTBD in scheduling:** Nurses, retail workers, and QSR crew increasingly
  optimize for predictability and voice. Scheduling tech that just "optimizes labor
  cost" fails on the crew's actual JTBD.
- **Supplier JTBD in procurement:** Forecast sharing, ordering cadence, payment
  terms — these all address the supplier's job. Work against the JTBD and suppliers
  build in buffers that cost you.
- **Delivery driver JTBD:** Fast pickup, clear order, labeled bag, dedicated
  staging area. Optimize for the driver's job → driver satisfaction rises → DoorDash
  algorithm sends more orders your way.
- **Franchisee JTBD:** Unit economics, brand support, flexibility. Franchise
  agreements that misread the JTBD produce slow growth and bad relationships.
- **Internal tool adoption:** A POS that asks operators to type 12 characters for
  a mod is working against the operator's JTBD during a rush. Tools that don't
  solve the actual job get bypassed.

## Common failure modes

- Confusing "persona" with "job." Job is contextual; persona is demographic.
- Treating JTBD as a marketing exercise only. It's an operating lens.
- Doing JTBD interviews without the "switch" question ("when did you last switch
  from X to Y, and what triggered it?"). The switch is where the job is visible.
- Using JTBD to justify what you were already going to build.
