# Thorndike — The Outsiders / Capital Allocation

**Source:** William Thorndike, *The Outsiders* (2012). Eight CEOs: Henry Singleton,
Tom Murphy, Warren Buffett, Bill Anders, John Malone, Katharine Graham, Bill Stiritz,
Dick Smith
**Core principle:** The CEO's primary job is capital allocation. Judge them by
returns on deployed capital and cost of capital raised, not by charisma, tenure, or
growth narratives.

## When to apply

- Portfolio decisions (buy, build, divest) dominate strategy
- CapEx spending is defended by narrative rather than IRR
- Leadership optimizes for short-term earnings at the expense of long-term compound
  returns
- The company is considering share buybacks, M&A, or major CapEx
- The operator is evaluating a multi-unit holding company or portfolio

## The framework

**Five sources of capital:**

1. Operating cash flow
2. Debt
3. Equity issuance
4. Asset sales / divestitures
5. Returns on previously deployed capital (compounding)

**Five uses of capital:**

1. Invest in existing operations
2. Acquire other businesses
3. Pay dividends
4. Repay debt
5. Repurchase shares

**The CEO's job:** Allocate among these with a lens on long-term, tax-adjusted,
per-share value. Not growth. Not size. Per-share value.

**Common traits of the eight "outsiders" CEOs:**

- **Decentralized operating, centralized capital allocation.** Operators run the
  business; CEO decides where the cash flows.
- **Strong cost discipline.** Low corporate overhead. No trophy headquarters.
- **Willingness to concentrate capital.** When an opportunity was obviously good,
  they bet big. Not 80 tiny bets.
- **Countercyclical behavior.** Bought when others were selling; sold when others
  were buying. Repurchased shares heavily at low valuations.
- **Patience.** Multi-decade horizons. Comfortable with periods of visible
  inactivity.
- **Low public profile.** Didn't chase press or conferences.
- **Numeracy.** Understood the math of compounding, return on invested capital, cost
  of capital raised.

**The share buyback as a signal:**

Several of the eight (Singleton, Buffett, Malone) used buybacks aggressively when the
stock was cheap. Key principle: buybacks are only good if the price is below intrinsic
value. Above it, they destroy shareholder value.

**M&A discipline:**

- Most M&A destroys value
- The eight were exceptions — their M&A compounded
- They paid low multiples, often bought smaller targets, and integrated
  operationally (not just financially)

## Canonical example

Henry Singleton at Teledyne (1960-1986): grew earnings per share by ~20%/year for 25
years through disciplined acquisition in the 60s, heavy buybacks (repurchased 90% of
outstanding shares over his tenure) in the 70s-80s, and operating discipline throughout.
Buffett called him the best capital allocator of the era.

Tom Murphy at Capital Cities (1966-1995): acquired ABC for $3.5B in 1985 (then the
largest corporate acquisition ever). Focused on two metrics: cash flow and ROIC. Sold
Capital Cities to Disney for $19B.

John Malone at TCI/Liberty: decentralized operating, centralized financing, used debt
aggressively when interest rates were high, created tracking stocks, minimized taxes
through spinoffs. Compounded shareholder returns at ~30% for decades.

## Applied to QSR / retail / ops

- **Multi-unit holding companies** (Sycamore Lane Holdings, Yum Brands, Inspire Brands,
  Roark Capital): same capital allocation lens — which brand gets the next dollar?
- **CapEx decisions (new format rollout, modular construction, automation):** Frame
  as ROIC vs. alternative uses (dividends, buybacks, acquisitions). What's the
  hurdle rate?
- **Franchise vs. company-owned:** Capital allocation question. Franchise = asset-
  light, lower returns on operations but lower capital deployment. Company-owned =
  higher returns if you can execute but tied-up capital.
- **Portfolio pruning:** Thorndike-style CEOs divest aggressively. Most QSR portfolios
  are over-indexed on legacy brands; pruning is under-used.
- **Countercyclical moves:** Build during downturns when construction costs drop and
  real estate is cheaper. Most chains build when they have cash (pro-cyclical) and
  pay a premium.

## Common failure modes

- Treating capital allocation as finance's job, not the CEO's
- Optimizing for next quarter's EPS instead of long-term per-share value
- Diversifying acquisitions for the sake of growth, without operational fit
- Buybacks above intrinsic value (common — CEOs buy back at peaks to "signal
  confidence")
- Centralized operating + centralized financing = bureaucracy. The eight did it
  the other way: decentralized operations, centralized finance.
