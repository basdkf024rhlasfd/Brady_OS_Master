## 1. Executive Summary — Five Org-Design Signals

1. **Justin is hedging on a strategic co-leader.** Hiring Brady (CFSO) and Alyson (CTrO) simultaneously, with Justin's own admission of "some overlap," is not a confident org build. It's a founder unsure which seat will carry the strategic load — so he's filling both. **This is the single biggest fact in the email.** It either resolves with Brady absorbing scope (good) or Alyson absorbing scope (bad). The first 90 days decide.

2. **Frank's COO elevation is the trust currency in the room.** Frank is internal, executes day-to-day, and Justin is giving him "operational integration of acquired stores" — the post-close M&A work. That's a meaningful slice of what an external CFSO with M&A architecture credentials could reasonably claim. Brady walks in as the financial architect; Frank walks in as Justin's existing operator. The CEO-CFO-COO triangle has a built-in tilt toward the COO unless Brady acts deliberately.

3. **Brady's title is "Chief Finance & Strategy Officer" but his scope reads as 60% finance.** Of five bullets, two are pure finance (capital structure + finance oversight), one is M&A (deal sourcing/modeling/execution), one is structural (entity design), and one is technology (AI roadmap). The "Strategy" word is in the title but mostly absent from the scope. **This is the gap to close on the call.**

4. **The Georgia Tech MBA Director was hired by Justin, not Brady — and reports to Brady within 30 days.** That's an inherited direct report on Day 1, picked by someone else. Combined with the existing CFO being exited, Brady walks into a finance function with one departure and one Justin-installed lieutenant. Political risk: the Director becomes Justin's eyes inside Brady's house.

5. **Russell to Chairman is governance-clean but the personal guarantee question is unanswered.** Justin saying "no on the Russell part" (Russell is not a hiring gatekeeper) is a strong CEO-empowerment signal. But Russell still owns the company, and as long as he personally guarantees debt, Brady's lender posture is constrained — banks negotiate with the guarantor, not the CFO. Need to know the guarantee structure before Day 1.

## 3. The Alyson Question — Additive or Threatening?

**Justin's exact words: "I see some overlap here with your role but I also think there's enough work coming that you can justify two minds on this tackling slightly different aspects of the work. For discussion."**

That sentence is not a casual aside. It's Justin saying out loud that he hasn't decided how the strategic seat is split. He's inviting Brady to weigh in. **Treat it as the most important sentence in the email.**

### What Justin's hiring philosophy reveals

Hiring two senior strategic execs at $185M revenue (scaling to $285M) is not standard. The pattern fits one of three founder archetypes:

1. **Hedger** — can't decide which seat carries weight, hires both, lets them shake out
2. **Maximalist** — believes "two minds are better than one," doesn't see the overlap as a problem
3. **Hire-the-people-then-write-the-jobs** — hired Alyson first because GE pedigree + "transformation" sounded right, then realized the CFSO scope swallowed half her job description

Justin's "for discussion" framing rules out #2. A maximalist wouldn't pre-flag the overlap. The actual answer is some mix of #1 and #3 — and the timing matters: **Alyson appears to have been recruited before Brady was scoped.** Justin built the org around her shape and is now retrofitting Brady around what's left. Brady's negotiating job is to reverse that.

### GE pattern reading

Alyson trained at GE during the "Session C / general manager rotation" era. GE historically ran overlapping general manager scopes deliberately — to create internal tension, force collaboration, and surface the next CEO. **That model presumes the founder/CEO has the time and skill to referee daily.** Justin is running an $185M operating company, closing AGR, launching ERP, and doing two senior hires at once. He will not have referee bandwidth.

When the referee can't referee, the person with **information advantage** wins overlap fights. Today that's Alyson — she's running the AGR integration as her launch deliverable. Brady starts after AGR closes (Aug 1) and inherits the financial cleanup of a deal he didn't structure.

### Failure mode

The downside scenario plays out like this:

1. Alyson starts in May/June; Brady starts Aug 1 (or later)
2. AGR closes Aug 1; Alyson runs the integration playbook she's been building
3. By the time Brady is on the ground, Alyson has the integration model, the synergy targets, and the relationship with the AGR finance team
4. Future M&A — Zeidan, MS, Beaumont — uses Alyson's playbook by default
5. Brady becomes the finance person who closes deals Alyson architects
6. "Strategy Officer" becomes ceremonial

That's not paranoia. That's the predictable outcome of starting second with a softer scope.

### The counterfactual question

**"What does the org look like without the CTrO role?"**

Strip Alyson out and redistribute:
- AGR integration → Brady (Finance) + Frank (Ops) — already the natural split
- Future M&A playbook → Brady (deal architecture) + Frank (operational integration)
- Org design + talent strategy → CEO + CPO (where it lives at most $200M companies)
- Store of the future → CMO (concept/brand) + COO (execution) — concept-belongs-with-merch in retail
- New lines of business (SSB) → CMO (commercial) + Brady (entity/economics)
- Cross-functional transformation → CEO + Brady's strategy hat

Every Alyson bullet has a natural home elsewhere. **The CTrO role is not structurally necessary at this revenue scale.** Justin is hedging or honoring a hire decision he already made.

### Is Alyson additive or threatening?

**Threatening, with a path to additive.** The path requires Brady to:

1. Get explicit ownership of M&A deal architecture (pre-close + economics + capital), with Alyson supporting integration sequencing
2. Get the words "operating model" and "transformation" out of Alyson's bullets where they touch capital, IT, or financial reporting
3. Establish a steering structure where Brady chairs the bodies that approve transformation spend
4. Watch the start-date gap — every week Alyson is in the building before Brady is a week of unreversed scope creep

If Brady can't get all four, this org structure becomes a slow squeeze.

### Two questions Brady must ask Justin in the call

1. **"When did you sign Alyson, and what was the original scope before my CFSO role was on the table?"** (Tells you whether Brady's scope is what's left over after Alyson, or vice versa.)
2. **"In a year, if Alyson and I have an overlap dispute on M&A integration or operating model, how do you imagine that gets resolved?"** (Forces Justin to name the tiebreak rule. Default tiebreak = whoever has been in the building longer wins.)

## 4. CEO-COO-CFO Triangle Dynamics

### Standard $200-300M private retail dynamic

In a typical scaling private retailer at this size, CFO and COO sit at parity under the CEO. CFO owns the balance sheet, capital, and the numbers; COO owns the operation. They referee together; the CEO breaks ties on strategic direction. **Brady's title (CFSO) explicitly elevates him into the strategy seat — but his scope doesn't yet match the title.**

### Frank's structural advantages

- **Tenure / trust banked** — Frank is internal. Justin's calculation on Frank is already made.
- **Bullet #5 — operational integration of acquired stores.** This is the M&A bullet on the COO line. It says Frank owns the ground truth of post-close execution. That's where the economics of the deal show up or don't.
- **OWN IT model.** Frank has a named operating system already. Brady's "applied AI roadmap" is newer and less proven inside the company.
- **Distribution reports here.** That's a $23-25M cost line (per SFDR-015) and a real lever. Frank has direct ops control.

### Brady's structural advantages (the moat)

- **Capital structure and lender relationships** — Frank can't do this. Justin can do parts but not the syndicate management. This is Brady-only.
- **Entity design across opco / RE / aviation / new ventures** — touches Russell's personal balance sheet, requires CPA/legal sophistication, only Brady has it.
- **Deal sourcing, modeling, execution** — Brady has the financial architecture chops; Frank doesn't. But (see overlap #1) Alyson contests this.
- **Applied AI roadmap** — softer mandate, but Brady's only territory if he holds it.
- **Booth + retail + foodservice + strategic ops** — pedigree no one else on the team has.

### The risk

If Justin defaults to **"Frank knows the business,"** the strategy work routes through Frank by gravity. Brady becomes the back-office finance head despite the title. The "Strategy" in CFSO needs a load-bearing deliverable in the first 90 days.

### Brady's first-90-day deliverable that locks the strategic seat

Pick one and own it visibly:

1. **The 3-year capital + entity plan** — a written, board-quality document covering opco capital structure, RE strategy (sale-leaseback or hold), aviation entity, family trust integration, and the M&A funding model for the next $100M of revenue. This is uniquely Brady's. Frank can't write it; Alyson won't write it; Justin needs it for banks. Deliver by Day 60.

2. **The post-AGR financial integration model + Zeidan deal architecture** — show up Aug 1 with the AGR financial integration plan already drafted (not Alyson's plan, Brady's), and a Zeidan deal model ready to share with banks. Forces Brady into the M&A seat structurally, not aspirationally.

3. **The applied AI / management OS one-pager that goes to Russell** — the artifact that demonstrates Brady is the strategist on the team. Russell sees it; Justin can't write it; nobody else can.

**Recommended:** #1, with #2 in flight by Day 90. That sequencing locks the strategic seat in writing before Alyson's transformation deliverable lands.

## 5. Russell as Chairman — Governance Read

### What "Chairman & Owner" actually means here

Russell is moving from operating to Chairman while still owning the company. Three possible flavors:

- **Honorary** — emeritus, attends quarterly, signs paperwork
- **Board-only** — meaningfully engaged on strategic direction quarterly, owns capital decisions and big-bet approvals
- **Deeply engaged** — Chairman in title but day-touching on capital, M&A approvals, and family-wealth decisions

Justin's "no on the Russell part" comment told Brady that Russell is **not** a hiring gatekeeper. That's CEO-empowered. But it's a weak signal on the broader Russell role — hiring approval is one specific thing; capital, M&A, and major spend approval are different things.

### The personal guarantee question

This is the load-bearing question Brady hasn't asked yet. If Russell personally guarantees the senior debt:

- The lender's counterparty is Russell, not the company
- Loan covenants likely include personal financial reporting from Russell
- Brady's negotiating posture with banks is constrained — banks negotiate with the guarantor's wealth manager, not the CFO
- Major capital decisions (refinancing, sale-leaseback, acquisition financing) require Russell's signature, not just Justin's
- "Chairman" becomes a meaningful operating role for capital matters

If Russell's PGs are being released as part of the Chairman transition (a clean PE-style governance evolution would do this), then Brady's role is straightforwardly the company's CFSO and his bank relationships are real.

### Brady's exposure if Russell's risk tolerance differs from Justin's

In the conservative scenario:
- Russell vetoes a deal Justin wants (Brady's M&A pipeline stalls)
- Russell wants conservative leverage; Justin wants aggressive growth — Brady gets caught
- In a downturn, Russell pulls capital to protect family wealth; Brady has to manage covenant compliance with reduced runway

In the aggressive scenario:
- Russell wants more deals than Brady's models support
- Brady becomes the no-person on family-driven deals (worst position for an external CFO)

**Key ask of Justin:** Walk me through how Russell engages on capital, M&A approval, and big-spend decisions. What's the formal vs. informal cadence?

### What good looks like

- Russell signs off on major capital actions through a board cadence (quarterly)
- Justin owns operating decisions and M&A approval up to a defined threshold (e.g., deals under $30M)
- Brady has direct CFSO access to Russell on capital matters but does not need Russell's daily approval
- Personal guarantees released or capped by a date certain
- A Chairman's letter or governance doc memorializes the model

If none of this exists, Brady should offer to draft it in the first 90 days. (That offer is also the "strategic seat" deliverable.)

## 6. The Georgia Tech MBA Director — Political Read

### What we know

- Hired by Justin, starts in a week (early May)
- Reports to Justin "because of the current CFO problem" — interim arrangement
- Plan is to move under Brady once Brady starts
- Justin describes him as "pretty strong"
- Long-term plan: Director "drives most of the finance function" so Brady can do strategy
- Will be the main person on ERP launch (June 1) — which is Brady's IT scope without Brady in the building

### Why this is good for Brady (the surface read)

- Brady doesn't have to do controllership, FP&A, or treasury day-to-day
- Day 1 Brady has a functioning lieutenant
- The Director absorbs the existing CFO's exit while Brady is still on Panda
- ERP cutover is owned by the Director, not by Brady's first weeks

### Why this is risky for Brady (the political read)

- **Justin picked the Director, not Brady.** This is the single most important fact. Loyalty defaults to who hired you.
- The Director will spend 8-12 weeks (depending on Brady's start date) reporting directly to Justin. He builds working rhythm with Justin, learns what Justin wants, sees the company through Justin's eyes — all before Brady arrives.
- He'll own the ERP launch — a six-figure technology decision Brady should be involved in but won't be (because he's at Panda)
- He becomes the institutional memory of the CFO transition — what was broken, why the previous CFO left, what Justin wants from finance
- When Brady arrives, the Director isn't reporting up; he's reporting **across** to Brady — already loyal to Justin
- If Brady disagrees with a Justin decision, the Director is the natural backchannel — a soft second source for Justin

### The Walmart pattern

This is exactly the dynamic Brady would have warned a junior leader about at Walmart: **never inherit a direct report you didn't pick.** Especially not one picked by your boss who continues to have a relationship with that report.

### What Brady should ask for

1. **Interview the Director before Day 1.** Phone or in-person. Not to veto, but to establish the relationship as Brady's, not Justin's.
2. **Explicit confirmation that the Director reports to Brady from Day 1, with no dotted line back to Justin.** Dotted lines kill clarity and create the exact backchannel risk above.
3. **Veto right within 90 days.** If the Director isn't a fit, Brady has the unilateral authority to move him out of finance. This is the correct CFO posture and a normal ask. If Justin pushes back, that's a signal about how much real authority Brady has.
4. **Be in the room (or on the phone) for ERP launch decisions.** Brady doesn't need to be in Bentonville to weigh in on architecture decisions. June 1 is during Brady's Panda engagement — but a 30-min weekly check-in is the right cadence.
5. **Reorganize finance after 6 months.** Brady reserves the right to reorg the function, including the Director's scope, after 6 months. This is the soft ask that creates room for the harder one if needed.

## 7. Comp Implications of the Org Weight

### Headcount math at $185-285M revenue

| Role | Estimated Base | Estimated TDC |
|---|---|---|
| Russell (Chairman) | $0-300K (depends on whether he draws cash) | varies |
| Justin (CEO) | $500-700K | $800K-$1.2M |
| Brady (CFSO) | $400-450K | $700-850K target |
| Frank (COO) | $300-400K | $450-600K |
| Alyson (CTrO) | $300-350K | $450-550K |
| Amanda (CMO) | $250-325K | $375-500K |
| Leah (CPO) | $225-275K | $300-400K |
| **Total exec base** | **$1.78-2.50M** | **$3.07-4.10M+** |

### Sanity check vs. revenue

- **Today ($185M):** Total exec TDC at $3.5M = **1.9% of revenue.** High end of typical range (1.5-2.5%) for this scale.
- **Post-AGR ($254M):** Total exec TDC at $3.7M = **1.5% of revenue.** Comfortable.
- **48 stores in 18 months ($285M):** Total exec TDC at $4M = **1.4% of revenue.** Comfortable.

Justin's bet: revenue grows fast enough to dilute exec headcount cost. The AGR close in Aug + the 2 LOI'd stores + the FL opening + the future tuck-ins make the math work. But the cap table feels the weight in years 1-2 if anything slips.

### What this means for Brady's bonus headroom

EBITDA pool drives the bonus pie, and the bonus pie has to support **five execs** at meaningful targets:

- 2025 EBITDA $25M; 2026 likely $25-28M; 2027 with full AGR ~$35M; 2028 with tuck-ins ~$40M
- If exec bonus pool is 8-10% of EBITDA (typical), that's $2-2.5M today, growing to $3-4M by 2028
- Brady at 1% of EBITDA (target) = $250K Y1 → $400K Y3 — consistent with Justin's $700K post-acquisition reference for Brady all-in
- But: if Alyson is at 0.7%, Frank at 0.7%, Amanda at 0.5%, Leah at 0.4% — that's ~3.3% of EBITDA just on five execs. Add Justin's owner take + Russell's Chairman comp and the 8-10% cap is already binding by year 2.

**Implication:** Brady's bonus % needs to be defined explicitly, in writing, and not subject to "we'll see how the pool develops." Otherwise the bonus number Justin floated ($700K post-acquisition) gets squeezed by the rest of the comp pool.

### LTI structure — the question to ask

Justin said: "issued annually, vest 5-year." That's a vehicle, not a structure. Possibilities:

- **Phantom equity / appreciation rights** — paper that pays out at exit or annual valuation event. Family-owned company without exit plan = paper that may never monetize. **IVFH risk.**
- **Cash-deferred bonus** — booked annually, paid out at vest. Real cash, but illiquid 5 years.
- **Profit interest in opco LLC** — actual equity participation; tax-efficient if structured as profits interest. Best vehicle if available.
- **Real common units** — direct equity ownership; very rare in family-owned companies.

The vehicle determines the entire risk profile. Brady cannot evaluate "$X LTI per year" without knowing the vehicle.

### Comp recommendation given the org weight

- Don't optimize base above $450K — it triggers parity arguments with Frank that Brady doesn't want to fight
- Optimize EBITDA bonus formula and floor instead — that's where Brady's leverage lives
- Make LTI a live conversation, not a "we'll figure it out" — get the vehicle named in writing
