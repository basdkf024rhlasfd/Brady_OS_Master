# Why the Clarks Became Mormons: A Whitepaper on the 1911 Conversion of Joseph Anderson Clark and the Monacan/"Brown People" of Irish Creek, Virginia

**For:** Brady Smallwood
**Subject:** Great-great-grandfather Joseph Anderson Clark — the first LDS convert in your line
**Date:** 2026-05-06
**Purpose:** Definitive narrative of how a Monacan man on Irish Creek in Rockbridge County, Virginia, ended up baptized into a Utah-headquartered church in 1911 — and how that single decision routes through Plecker, the priesthood ban, a 1951 genealogical rescue, and the Buena Vista and Waynesboro wards your family still anchors today.

## Executive Summary

Joseph Anderson Clark was not a random convert. He was the most theologically logical convert the Southern States Mission could have hoped for in 1911, and at the same time the most racially impossible one — a contradiction that shaped four decades of his family's relationship with the Church and explains every weird gap and cluster in the baptism record you grew up hearing about.

The compressed version:

1. **The Mormons came looking for him.** LDS theology, codified in the Book of Mormon (1830), holds that Native Americans are literal descendants of the House of Israel. Missionaries on the Pedlar Creek / Irish Creek circuit explicitly identified the dark-skinned uplanders of the Blue Ridge as "Lamanites." J. Golden Kimball himself described Peter Mason on Pedlar Creek as having "Indian descent, his skin being almost as dark as an Indian's." (Source: Olson, Crossroads, 2004 — credibility 4)

2. **Joseph Clark went looking for them.** Per family genealogy (and corroborated in Ted Olson's Crossroads chapter), Joseph Anderson Clark believed "people with the blood of Israel had special responsibilities to prepare for the second coming of the Messiah." That is not a generic conversion narrative. That is a man who heard Lamanite doctrine and recognized himself in it. (Credibility 4)

3. **The Episcopals offered an alternative — and lost.** F.W. Neve and later Arthur P. Gray Jr. founded St. Paul's Bear Mountain Episcopal Mission in Amherst County in 1908. The Bear Mountain Monacans went Episcopal. The Irish Creek Clarks, 25 miles north and across the Blue Ridge, went Mormon. The split is geographical and theological — Bear Mountain was in the heart of Plecker country and got a school; Irish Creek got missionaries who told them they were chosen people. (Credibility 4)

4. **Plecker then trapped them.** The 1924 Racial Integrity Act reclassified Virginia Indians as "colored." Joseph Clark and Mary Susan Clark had been married "as colored" in Amherst County records in 1906. Under LDS doctrine as it stood from Brigham Young (1852) through the 1978 revelation, anyone "of any known African descent" could not hold the priesthood. The Clarks were now caught — Mormon by faith, "colored" on paper, ineligible by policy. (Credibility 5)

5. **The 1951 rescue.** When 12-year-old Claude Clark was denied priesthood ordination, Joseph Anderson Clark — then in his 70s — worked with LDS genealogical missionaries to prove Native, not Black, ancestry. The key precedent was the 1925 Atha Sorrells case in Rockbridge County, where Sorrells had successfully demonstrated Indian ancestry under the "one-sixteenth" Pocahontas exception. Branch President Eugene Larsen accepted the proof. Claude was ordained. In 1957 he became the first full-time missionary from the Waynesboro Branch. (Credibility 4)

This is not "always mysterious." This is a Monacan man named Joseph Anderson Clark making a perfectly rational theological choice in 1911, and then spending the rest of his life paying the price for the gap between Mormon doctrine (Lamanites are chosen) and Mormon racial policy (anyone "colored" is excluded).

## 1. Why Were LDS Missionaries in Southern Appalachian Virginia in 1911?

**Short answer:** They had been there for 36 years, riding a circuit that ran from Tennessee through the Blue Ridge, and they were specifically hunting for what they read as "Lamanite" populations — dark-skinned, mixed-ancestry mountain communities they believed were literal Israelites.

### The Southern States Mission (1875–)

Brigham Young formally organized the Southern States Mission at the LDS October 1875 General Conference. Eight men were called: Henry G. Boyle, George Teasdale, D.P. Rainey, Joseph Standing, John Morgan, John D.H. McCallister, David H. Perry, and John Winder. By summer 1876, the mission covered Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Arkansas, Tennessee, and Virginia. (Source: Heather Seferovich, *History of the LDS Southern States Mission, 1875–1898*, BYU dissertation — credibility 5)

Between 1875 and 1898, **1,760 elders baptized 3,839 people across the South.** Notable Southern States missionaries included John Morgan, B.H. Roberts, LeGrand Richards, George Albert Smith, and J. Golden Kimball — five future apostles or general authorities. (Credibility 5)

The Mormons were not seen as Christians by southern Protestants. They were seen as polygamists, federal subversives, and cultists — "spiritual carpetbaggers," as one historian phrased it. Reconstruction-era hostility was raw, and the missionaries arrived in homespun suits with Books of Mormon in their saddlebags. (Credibility 4)

### The Cane Creek Massacre, August 10, 1884

The single defining moment of the early Southern States Mission was the **Cane Creek Massacre** in Lewis County, Tennessee, where five people died: missionaries John H. Gibbs and William S. Berry, local LDS members Martin Conder and J.R. Hutson, and the mob's own leader David Hinson. The trigger was the Salt Lake Tribune's "Red Hot Address" — a fabricated sermon that called for war against "Gentiles" and the assassination of Utah's governor — combined with anti-polygamy sentiment whipped up by Baptist preacher John Clayborn Vandiver. **No trial was ever held.** (Source: Wikipedia / BYU Studies / Bruce Crow, *A Land of Strangers* — credibility 5)

J. Golden Kimball was working in the Chattanooga mission office as mission secretary when news of Cane Creek arrived. He developed malaria during this same mission. (Credibility 5)

### J. Golden Kimball as Mission President (1891–1894)

Kimball returned to lead the Southern States Mission as president from 1891 to 1894, called by President Wilford Woodruff. He was simultaneously serving as a member of the First Council of the Seventy — a sitting general authority running a mission in the violent post-Reconstruction South. His tenure professionalized the missionary corps, expanded the circuit into the southern Appalachian uplands, and explicitly emphasized identifying "Lamanite" populations. (Credibility 5)

Kimball's own field journals describe Peter Mason of Pedlar Creek (just over the Blue Ridge from Irish Creek) as having "Indian descent, his skin being almost as dark as an Indians. His hair was long and black." That is not casual ethnography — that is a mission president flagging a target population. (Source: Olson, *Crossroads* 2004, citing Kimball journals — credibility 4)

### The Mountain Circuit by 1911

By 1911, the Southern States Mission had evolved a regular Blue Ridge circuit: Pedlar Creek (Amherst County) → Irish Creek (Rockbridge County) → Buena Vista boom town → up to Cornwall and Long Hollow. The Pedlar Creek "Mason neighborhood" had been a mission headquarters since the 1880s. Records exist for Buena Vista, Cornwall (Irish Creek), and Oronoco (Pedlar Creek) in LDS membership rolls from this period. (Credibility 4)

The 1887 Josiah Borrows "Conference in Virginia" piece in *Deseret Evening News* — which I could not directly retrieve in this research pass but which is referenced in Olson — appears to document an early conference held in or near these communities, predating Joseph Clark's baptism by 24 years. (Credibility 2 — I could not independently verify this primary source)

## 2. Why Did the Monacan/"Brown People" of Irish Creek Convert?

This is where the doctrinal mechanics become decisive.

### The Lamanite Doctrine

In Joseph Smith's 1830 Book of Mormon, the family of Lehi — described as a wealthy Hebrew prophet — left Jerusalem around 600 BCE and sailed to the Americas. Their descendants split into two main peoples: the Nephites (righteous, white) and the Lamanites (rebellious, "cursed" with dark skin). After centuries of war, the Lamanites destroyed the Nephites in roughly 421 CE.

For 19th-century Mormons, the Lamanites were not metaphor. **They were Native Americans.** Brigham Young taught explicitly that Indians were the "precious remnant" of the House of Israel and that converting and intermarrying with them would fulfill prophecy. The Church's first organized mission, in October 1830, was the "Lamanite Mission" — Oliver Cowdery, Parley P. Pratt, Peter Whitmer Jr., and Ziba Peterson sent west to preach to the Delaware, Seneca, Wyandot, and Shawnee. (Source: ChurchofJesusChrist.org "Lamanite Identity" + Wikipedia "Native American people and Mormonism" — credibility 5)

In 1855, Brigham Young called 160 missionaries to American Indian communities from the West Coast to the Mississippi. Wilford Woodruff personally adopted a Paiute boy named Moroni Bosnel that same year. (Credibility 5)

This is the theological water Joseph Anderson Clark stepped into in 1911. The missionaries who knocked on his cabin door were not generic evangelists. They were operating under a doctrine that said *he* — a man classified as "colored" by Virginia bureaucrats but understood by his family as Indian — was a literal descendant of the House of Israel with a special role in preparing for the Second Coming of Christ.

### Joseph Anderson Clark's Stated Reason

Per Ted Olson's *Crossroads* chapter (Mercer Univ. Press, 2004, the foundational scholarly source on this community): **Joseph Anderson Clark "became a Mormon in 1911 because he believed that people with the blood of Israel had special responsibilities to prepare for the second coming of the Messiah."** (Credibility 4)

That sentence is theologically precise. It is not "the missionaries were nice" or "I felt the spirit." It is a man articulating Lamanite doctrine in his own voice. He understood what was being offered, and he accepted the identity claim — Israelite, chosen, end-times preparer — that the LDS Church specifically extended to Native Americans and that *no other denomination active in southern Appalachia in 1911 was offering.*

### The Episcopal Alternative — Bear Mountain vs. Irish Creek

This is the comparative move that locks in the analysis.

**Bear Mountain (Amherst County):** The Reverend F.W. Neve — Episcopal Archdeacon of the Blue Ridge — wrote "Some Mountain Missions in Virginia" in 1901, surveying Episcopal outreach to the Monacan/Brown People community. In 1908, the Reverend Arthur P. Gray Jr. formally established **St. Paul's Mission and the Bear Mountain Indian Mission School** on Monacan land in Amherst County. Because Virginia's segregation laws excluded Monacans from public schooling, the Episcopal mission provided seventh-grade education to Bear Mountain children from 1908 until integration in 1964. The school is on the National Register of Historic Places. The relationship between the Monacan Nation and the Episcopal Church continues today. (Source: Encyclopedia Virginia + Diocese of Southwestern Virginia — credibility 5)

**Irish Creek (Rockbridge County):** Same population — descendants of the same pre-contact Monacan/Tutelo people, identified in Library of Congress records as "Rockbridge County Brown People," numbering 300+. But geographically separated from Bear Mountain by the Blue Ridge, and on the wrong side of the watershed from the Episcopal circuit. The Mormons got there first and stayed.

**The split is structural:**
- The Episcopals offered: schooling, Anglican respectability, integration with white Virginia's mainline religious establishment.
- The Mormons offered: chosen-people identity, literal Israelite ancestry, an end-times role, and a doctrine that *elevated* Native identity rather than treating it as a deficit.

For a community already being told by Walter Plecker that they were "colored" and inadmissible, the Episcopal mission was charity. The Mormon mission was election. (Credibility 3 — this is my synthesis, but it is consistent with Olson and the Monacan twentieth-century literature)

### Did the Plecker Crisis Drive the Clarks Toward LDS?

**Partially, yes — but the timing matters.** Joseph Clark was baptized in 1911. The Racial Integrity Act passed in 1924. Plecker did not become Registrar of Vital Statistics until 1912 (one year after Clark's baptism). So Clark's *initial* conversion predates Plecker's institutional crackdown.

What Plecker did was **trap the Clarks inside their LDS commitment** — once they were Mormon, the Plecker-era reclassification of them as "colored" collided directly with the LDS priesthood ban, creating the 40-year crisis that culminated in the 1951 genealogy rescue. (Credibility 4)

So the cleaner causal chain is:
1. 1911: Clark converts because LDS doctrine elevates Native identity — pre-Plecker.
2. 1924–46: Plecker reclassifies Virginia Indians as Black, voiding the racial logic that made the conversion theologically coherent.
3. 1951: Joseph Clark fights to prove the original Native classification, restoring LDS standing for his sons and grandsons.

## 3. The 1911–1932 Clark Family Baptisms

Per the family genealogy you sent, the baptism timeline is:

- **1911:** Joseph Anderson Clark (head of household)
- **1911:** Mary Susan Clark (wife)
- **1912:** Walter (son)
- **1917:** James (son), Fannie (daughter)
- **1918:** Levi (son)
- **1932:** Elijah, Mark, Paul, Silas (sons)

### Why the Gaps?

**There were no resident priesthood holders in Irish Creek.** Baptisms required a Melchizedek or Aaronic priesthood holder, which under church policy meant ordained white men from outside the community. The Southern States Mission ran a circuit, not a parish — missionaries swept through, baptized whoever was ready, then moved on. The 1911 → 1912 → 1917 → 1918 cluster matches the documented circuit cycles of Pedlar/Irish Creek/Buena Vista. (Credibility 4)

**Then Church activity ended in and near Buena Vista in 1918.** Per Olson and the Shaybo synthesis: official LDS records for Buena Vista, Cornwall, and Oronoco disappear from the rolls after 1918. World War I disrupted the missionary corps; the 1918 influenza pandemic disrupted everything; and the Southern States Mission shifted resources toward urban centers. (Credibility 4)

### The 14-Year Dormancy (1918–1932)

This is where the family genealogy becomes most interesting. **Without resident priesthood, the Clark family could not partake of the sacrament, could not be confirmed if not already, and could not have new male members ordained.** They had baptism (an Aaronic ordinance), but the higher ordinances were inaccessible.

What they appear to have done instead is preserve practice in a vernacular form. Per Olson's account, members held meetings in homes and under trees, reading scripture together. Myrtle Wilhelm Coleman, born 1923, told Olson she "had always belonged" to the church but "didn't know anything about elders" growing up. (Credibility 4)

Theologically, this is functionally pre-priesthood Christianity — a baptized lay community without ordained leadership, dependent on personal scripture reading and oral transmission of doctrine. Closer to early Christian house-church practice than to standard 20th-century Mormonism.

### The 1932 Reactivation: Alvin Pocock

In 1932, **Elder Alvin Pocock** arrived in Buena Vista and conducted intensive baptismal work, reactivating the dormant community. Pocock served two consecutive missions in the same location (unusual in LDS practice, where rotation is the norm), with mission program covers documented from 1930 and 1934. He performed full-immersion baptisms in the South River near Cornwall and in "the blue hole." The 1932 baptisms of Elijah, Mark, Paul, and Silas Clark are part of this Pocock reactivation. (Credibility 4)

A church building was constructed in Long Hollow by 1937, with Elder Reid Tippitts recording the dedication on May 30, 1937, with 130 attendees. (Credibility 3)

## 4. The 1849–1978 Priesthood and Temple Ban on Black Members

### Origins

There is no scriptural foundation for the ban in Joseph Smith's lifetime — Smith ordained at least a few Black men, including Elijah Abel and Walker Lewis. The ban is institutionalized under **Brigham Young, beginning roughly 1849–1852**, and formalized in his February 5, 1852 address to the Utah Territorial Legislature. Young's stated rationale combined biblical curse-of-Cain theology with antebellum racial pseudoscience. (Source: Race and the Priesthood Gospel Topics essay, December 2013 — credibility 5)

### Scope: "Any Known African Descent"

The ban applied to anyone with **"any known African descent"** — a one-drop rule that exactly mirrored the Plecker-era Virginia statute. This is the collision point that defines your family's story.

For most of the ban's history (1852–1978):
- Black men: no priesthood, no temple endowment, no temple sealing.
- Black women: no temple endowment, no temple sealing.
- Native Americans: priesthood eligible (Lamanite doctrine).
- Native Americans reclassified as "colored": ineligible (one-drop rule).

The Clarks lived in the seam of this contradiction for 27 years (1924–1951). (Credibility 5)

### David O. McKay Era

President David O. McKay (served 1951–1970) is widely documented as personally uncomfortable with the ban and as having considered lifting it in the late 1950s. He is quoted as saying it was "policy, not doctrine" — language that anticipated the eventual 1978 revelation. McKay also softened the lineage standard for borderline cases in Brazil and South Africa. (Credibility 4)

### The 1978 Revelation

On **June 1, 1978**, President Spencer W. Kimball assembled the First Presidency and Quorum of the Twelve in the Salt Lake Temple. At 2:45 p.m., they formed a prayer circle around the altar, and Kimball announced he had received revelation that the priesthood ban was lifted. The official declaration was issued on June 8, 1978, and canonized at General Conference on September 30, 1978, as **Official Declaration 2** in the Doctrine and Covenants. (Source: Edward L. Kimball, *BYU Studies* — credibility 5)

The 1978 revelation came too late for Joseph Anderson Clark, who died in or around the early 1950s based on the genealogy timeline. He never lived to see the ban lifted institutionally, but he had already engineered its lifting *for his own family* in 1951 by proving Native, not Black, ancestry.

### The 2013 "Race and the Priesthood" Essay

On December 6, 2013, the LDS Church posted "Race and the Priesthood" on its official website. The essay explicitly **disavows** the historical theological justifications for the ban: "The Church disavows the theories advanced in the past that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse, or that it reflects actions in a premortal life; that mixed-race marriages are a sin; or that blacks or people of any other race or ethnicity are inferior in any way to anyone else." (Credibility 5)

This is the closest the Church has come to an institutional reckoning with the ban. It does not rescind the ban (the 1978 revelation already did that). It rescinds the *theology* used to justify the ban for 126 years.

## 5. The 1951 Joseph Anderson Clark Genealogy Rescue

This is the climactic moment in your family's LDS history and deserves its own careful reconstruction.

### The Trigger: 12-Year-Old Claude Clark

In 1950, Joseph Anderson Clark's grandson Claude Clark — 12 years old, the standard age for ordination to the Aaronic Priesthood as a deacon — was denied ordination when LDS officials checking courthouse records discovered his family classified as "colored." (Credibility 4)

### The Branch President: Eugene Larsen

The Buena Vista branch president at the time was **Eugene Larsen**, who appears to have been sympathetic but bound by policy. Larsen's response was to write to LDS Church headquarters requesting a genealogical review. (Credibility 4)

### The Genealogical Missionaries

Per the Olson/Shaybo synthesis:

- **Elder William S. Tanner** responded to Larsen's inquiry on May 1, 1951, with courthouse marriage records confirming that Joseph Anderson Clark and Mary Susan Clark had been married "as colored" in Amherst County in 1906. This was the bad news.
- **Two unnamed LDS genealogical missionaries from Utah** then traveled to Rockbridge County and worked with Joseph Anderson Clark to assemble counter-evidence. (Credibility 3 — names of the Utah missionaries are not in the public record I could access)

### The Key Precedent: Atha Sorrells, 1925

The legal hinge was the **Atha Sorrells case (1925, Rockbridge County)**. Sorrells, a Rockbridge woman classified as "colored" under the 1924 Racial Integrity Act, had been denied a marriage license to marry a white man. She sued, and the Rockbridge County clerk's denial was overturned when she successfully proved descent from Indian (specifically Pocahontas-line) ancestry, falling within the Pocahontas Exception of the 1924 Act (one-sixteenth Indian blood). The Sorrells case established legal precedent in the same county where the Clarks lived that "colored" did not necessarily mean "Negro." (Source: Encyclopedia Virginia + JMU thesis on Monacan twentieth-century history — credibility 5)

Plecker himself was so alarmed by the Sorrells decision that he distributed John Powell's brochure "The Breach in the Dike" to county clerks across Virginia, warning of the danger of "intermarriage of Whites with So-Called Indians." (Credibility 4)

### Joseph Clark's Evidence

Joseph Clark and the genealogical missionaries appear to have used a combination of:

1. The Sorrells case as legal precedent.
2. Cross-family evidence showing that Claude Clark's parents shared ancestors with families classified as "white" *and* "Indian," not "Black."
3. Antebellum free-people-of-color records that showed Indian, not African, lineage.
4. Microfilm copies of Rockbridge County court records assembled with help from the genealogical missionaries.

The dossier was reviewed by Branch President Larsen and accepted. (Credibility 3)

### The 1951 Ordination

Larsen ordained **Claude Clark** that same year, along with **Alvin Coleman** and **Garvis Wheeler** — direct descendants of Peter and Diannah Mason of Pedlar Creek, who Olson notes Larsen referred to as "Lamonite \[sic\] brothers." (Credibility 4)

### Claude Clark's First Mission, 1957

In **1957, Elder Claude Edward Clark served as the first full-time missionary from the Waynesboro Branch of the LDS Church** — the symbolic capstone of the 1951 genealogy fight. (Credibility 4)

### The Madsen Reference (Page 85)

You asked about Brigham D. Madsen's *Against the Grain: Memoirs of a Western Historian* (Signature Books, 1998). I could not retrieve page 85 directly in this research pass, but Madsen's papers (held at the University of Utah) include extensive Native American research files, and his Sunstone interview confirms that he worked on Indian genealogy projects in the postwar period. The reference in your family genealogy presumably places Madsen as one of the Utah-side researchers connected to the 1951 Clark effort, but I cannot independently confirm without retrieving page 85 of his memoir. **Action item:** request scan of *Against the Grain* p. 85 from a research library if you want the direct quote. (Credibility 2 on the specific Clark connection)

- **June 15, 1918:** 500 people attended an LDS meeting in Buena Vista — likely the high water mark of the early branch.
- **July 13, 1918:** Members subscribed over $400 for a church building (about $9,000 in 2026 dollars).

- **1918:** Official LDS records for the area then go dormant for 14 years.
- **1932:** Pocock reactivation begins.
- **May 30, 1937:** Long Hollow chapel dedicated.

(Credibility 4)

### Stake Organization

The **Waynesboro Virginia Stake** was formally organized on **May 28, 1978** — eleven days *before* the June 8, 1978 priesthood revelation announcement. This is striking timing. The stake went live with full priesthood structure within weeks of the ban being lifted, which would have meaningfully expanded ordinance access for any remaining Clark descendants whose paperwork had been ambiguous. (Source: Wikipedia "Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in Virginia" — credibility 5)

The town of Buena Vista now hosts **Southern Virginia University**, acquired by LDS interests in 1996 as the only liberal arts university east of the Mississippi staffed and oriented around LDS values (positioned as an SVU/BYU-East alternative). There is documented friction in the broader Buena Vista community between the LDS population and longer-tenured townspeople — the 2008 *By Common Consent* "Call for Study: the Buena Vista Social Feud" post discusses meetings staged by some residents to push back against LDS demographic concentration. ## 7. The Broader Pattern: Mormon-Native American Conversion in Appalachia

The Clark conversion was **not unique** but was part of a documented LDS strategy to evangelize Appalachian Native and mixed-ancestry communities under Lamanite doctrine. The closest analog is the Catawba.

### The Catawba Indian Nation, South Carolina

LDS missionaries first contacted the Catawba in approximately 1883. A formal branch was established **July 31, 1885**. The mass conversion was extraordinary: by **1950, 97% of the Catawba Indian Nation belonged to the LDS Church.** As late as the 1950s, the majority of the tribe still affiliated. (Source: BYU dissertation + South Carolina Encyclopedia — credibility 5)

Persecution mirrored what happened in Tennessee and Virginia: missionaries hid in the woods, subsisted on what Catawbas smuggled to them, held secret night baptisms. **Samuel Taylor Blue** — who later became Catawba chief in the early 1930s and president of the Catawba Branch — physically carried missionaries across the river on his back to protect them from mobs. Some Catawbas migrated west, founding the Mormon towns of Fox Creek, La Jara, and Sanford in southern Colorado.

### Comparative Picture

| Community | Location | First contact | Peak conversion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Catawba | Rock Hill, SC | c. 1883 | 97% by 1950 |
| Pedlar Creek (Mason family) | Amherst Co., VA | c. 1883–84 | Multi-generational LDS to present |
| Irish Creek (Clark family) | Rockbridge Co., VA | by 1888 (Tate letter) | Multi-generational LDS to present |
| Cherokee (Eastern Band) | NC | Sporadic 1880s+ | Limited |
| Lumbee | NC | Sporadic | Limited; Lumbee predominantly Methodist/Holiness |

The Clark conversion fits the Catawba template almost perfectly, just on a smaller scale — a tight-knit Native or Native-descended community in the upland Southeast, contacted by Southern States Mission elders riding circuit, persuaded by Lamanite doctrine, and held together across multi-generational dormancy by oral transmission and family ties. (Credibility 4)

### What the Pattern Means

LDS Appalachian missions in 1875–1920 were *not* primarily aimed at Scots-Irish or English-descended white mountaineers. They were aimed disproportionately at communities the missionaries identified as racially "Indian" — the populations most likely to read as "Lamanite remnant." This is a deliberate doctrinal strategy, not opportunism. (Credibility 3 — this is my synthesis from the Catawba and Virginia evidence; would need a comparative dissertation to nail down quantitatively)

## 8. Theological Tension Today

### The 2013 Reckoning

The "Race and the Priesthood" essay (December 6, 2013) is the formal institutional disavowal of the theological framework that trapped your family from 1924 to 1951. It rescinds the curse-of-Cain theology, the premortal-life-disfavor theology, and the inferiority-of-mixed-race-marriage teaching. It does not, however, formally apologize for the ban — that step has not happened. (Credibility 5)

### The DNA Problem

In **2002–2003**, Mormon scientists Thomas Murphy and Simon Southerton published genetic studies arguing that Native American ancestry traces overwhelmingly to East Asian migrations across Beringia, with no detectable Hebrew/Middle Eastern signal. This is the scientific consensus today. (Credibility 5)

The Church responded in **2006** by quietly amending the Book of Mormon's introductory page from describing Lamanites as "the principal ancestors of the American Indians" to "**among** the ancestors of the American Indians." A 2007 *Deseret News* article confirmed this was understood as a response to the DNA evidence. The 2013 Lamanite Identity Gospel Topics essay further softened the position: the Church now "does not take a position on the specific geography of the Book of Mormon or claim complete knowledge about the origins of any specific modern group in the Americas or the Pacific." (Credibility 5)

This creates a quiet theological problem for descendants of communities like the Clarks who *converted specifically because of Lamanite doctrine*. The doctrine that brought Joseph Anderson Clark to the font in 1911 has been functionally retired in its literal form. What remains is a softer covenantal claim — that all members are part of the "covenant house of Israel" by descent or adoption — without the specific Native American election that animated the 19th-century mission to Pedlar Creek and Irish Creek.

### Monacan-Specific LDS Scholarship

I found no Monacan-specific LDS scholarship beyond the Olson chapter and the broader Catawba/Virginia historiography. The Monacan Indian Nation received **federal recognition in 2018** under the Thomasina E. Jordan Indian Tribes of Virginia Federal Recognition Act, after decades of effort to undo Plecker's damage. The Bear Mountain Episcopal community has been central to that recognition fight. The Irish Creek/Buena Vista LDS community has been more scattered and less politically organized as a Monacan-tribal-identity bloc — partly because LDS theology absorbs them into a pan-Israelite identity rather than reinforcing tribal-specific identity, and partly because Plecker's records did the most damage to the people who were classified as "colored" rather than maintaining clear tribal rolls. (Credibility 3)

### What This Means for the Clark Descendants

You inherit a genuinely complicated theological position:

1. Your great-great-grandfather converted because he was told he was an Israelite chosen for the Second Coming.
2. The genetic evidence the Church now lives with says he wasn't, in any literal sense.
3. The institutional ban that nearly excluded your family was disavowed in 2013.
4. The doctrine that pulled your family in has been softened to the point of near-metaphor.
5. And yet the Buena Vista and Waynesboro wards remain — built by Clarks, peopled by Clarks, anchored in a community that exists *because* a Monacan man on Irish Creek in 1911 said yes to a doctrine that no longer exists in the form he heard it.

That is the actual mystery, and it is not a mystery of "what happened" — the historical record is clear. It is a mystery of how a family-scale theological commitment survives the disavowal of the doctrine that produced it. The Catawba Branch faced the same question. So did the Mason descendants on Pedlar Creek. The pattern in all three cases is that the community persists *as a community* even as the original theological frame attenuates — what the missionaries called Lamanite, and what the 2013 essays now treat as a more diffuse covenantal belonging.
