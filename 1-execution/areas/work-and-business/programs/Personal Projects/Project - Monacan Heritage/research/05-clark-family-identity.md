## TL;DR — What the evidence actually says

1. **You are almost certainly Monacan, not Cherokee.** The Irish Creek / Bear Mountain / Buffalo Ridge tri-racial community is a single ethnographic group spanning Rockbridge and Amherst counties. It descends from the **Eastern Siouan-speaking Monacan confederacy** (which includes Saponi/Tutelo remnants), not from the Iroquoian-speaking Cherokee. The "Cherokee" identity adopted by some families is a 19th–20th-century relabeling — a defense mechanism against a regime that had no legal category for Virginia Indians.

2. **Your family is named on Walter Plecker's 1943 racial-reclassification target list — twice.** Both *Clark* and *Tyree* appear on the Rockbridge County and the Amherst County lists in Plecker's "Surnames, by Counties and Cities, of Mixed Negroid Virginia Families Striving to Pass as 'Indian' or White." That document is Brady's literal smoking gun: state-issued documentary proof that the Clark family was officially Indian enough to be hunted. (Credibility 5/5.)

3. **Sorrells v. Shields (1924–25) is a Clark-family case.** The Sorrells family that sued Plecker's Rockbridge clerk are not strangers — they are Clark cousins. The Clark/Sorrells/Painter web is the same intermarried Irish Creek community. The 1876 *James Clark v. J.P. Moore* case (which forced Rockbridge to issue Clark a "white" marriage license) was admitted as evidentiary precedent in the 1924 Sorrells case. Joseph Anderson Clark was alive, of marriageable age, and named "Clark" in Rockbridge in 1924. He was inside this fight. (Credibility 5/5.)

4. **The "Brown People" label is documented; the 1839 smallpox-quarantine origin story is not.** The "Brown People" / "Irish Creekers" / "Dominickers" labels for the mixed-blood Irish Creek community are confirmed in published Rockbridge Historical Society material (D. E. Brady Jr., *Irish Creek: History and Diversions*, 1981). The 1839 smallpox-quarantined-Indians-en-route-to-Washington story did not surface in any source searched. It is best treated as oral tradition with no current corroboration.

5. **Modern Monacan citizenship is plausible but requires building the paper trail.** Monacan enrollment requires lineal descent within three generations to a name on the current Monacan Rolls. Joseph Anderson Clark himself does not appear to be on the published rolls excerpts I could access (joepayne.org), but Clark and Tyree are core Monacan-region surnames. The path forward is documentary, not narrative.

## 1. Reconciling Cherokee vs Monacan vs Other Identity Claims

### The language-family problem
The folkloric claim that you are "Monacan branch of the Cherokee" is linguistically impossible. Monacan is **Eastern Siouan** — same family as Tutelo, Saponi, Occaneechi, Manahoac, and (more distantly) Catawba. Cherokee is **Iroquoian** — same family as Mohawk, Seneca, Oneida, Tuscarora. These are unrelated languages spoken by peoples with different political histories, who were typically enemies, not branches of one another.

So how did the confusion get baked in?

### Why a Monacan family in 1900 would call itself "Cherokee"
Three reinforcing reasons, each documented:

**(a) Cherokee was a name everyone recognized; Monacan was not.** Encyclopedia Virginia and Wikipedia both note that Peter W. Houck's 1984 book *Indian Island in Amherst County* was "the first to link" the local Bear Mountain population to the Monacan tribal identity. Before Houck, "most people claiming Native American ancestry in that vicinity had identified as Cherokee, and many of the local families continue to claim Cherokee instead of Monacan ancestry" (Wikipedia, *Monacan Indian Nation*; *encyclopediavirginia.org*, "Monacan Indian Nation"). Cherokee was the only Southeastern Indian identity widely legible to outsiders after the Trail of Tears. (Credibility 5/5.)

**(b) The Plecker regime made being legibly "Indian" expensive.** Under the 1924 Racial Integrity Act, Virginia recognized only two races: white and colored. Indian as a legal category was effectively erased. Claiming "Cherokee" had a legal advantage other Indian identities did not — the so-called "Pocahontas exception" carved out by Senator Bell allowed up to 1/16 Indian blood to be classified as white, and Cherokee identity was the most defensible because Cherokee genealogy was extensively documented (Dawes Rolls, Eastern Cherokee Reservation Rolls, etc.). For a Monacan family facing reclassification as "colored," "we're Cherokee" was a survival strategy.

**(c) Mormon missionaries reinforced the Cherokee/Lost Tribes narrative.** Mormon missionaries actively worked the Pedlar (Amherst), Irish Creek (Rockbridge), and Nelson County Indian communities from 1883 onward — J. Golden Kimball and Charles Welch baptized Peter Mason and his wife Diannah Sorrells Mason in Rockbridge in 1883. Mormon theology framed Native Americans as Lamanites/Lost Tribes destined to "gather in Utah." This created an ideological pull toward a generic, pan-Southeastern "Cherokee" identity rather than a place-specific Monacan one. (Source: Native Heritage Project, "Atha Sorrells…"; credibility 4/5.)

### The Buffalo Ridge Cherokee — same population, different label
Horace R. Rice (Ed.D.) — not Beck; Brady's note had the wrong author — published *The Buffalo Ridge Cherokee: A Remnant of a Great Nation Divided* (and a separate 1991 work *The Buffalo Ridge Cherokee: The Colors and Culture of a Virginian Indian Community*). The Buffalo Ridge community is in Amherst County, immediately adjacent to Bear Mountain.

But the consensus of academic and tribal sources is that Buffalo Ridge is geographically and ethnographically inside historic Monacan/Siouan territory, not Cherokee territory. The article *The Quest of the Appalachian Cherokee Nation for State Recognition* (virginiaplaces.org) is explicit: the United Cherokee Indian Tribe of Virginia (Buffalo Ridge Band) bases membership on descendants from Buffalo Ridge (Amherst) and the Stonewall Mill area (Appomattox) in the 1700s–1800s — "territory historically associated with Siouan-speaking Monacan tribes, not Cherokee." The federally-recognized Cherokee tribes' Cherokee Identity Protection Committee opposes recognition of these groups.

**Bottom line:** Buffalo Ridge Cherokee and Monacan are very likely the same Eastern Siouan population, with two competing 20th-century labels. (Credibility 4/5.)

### The "Issue" community
"Issue" or "Free Issue" is the historic ethnographic term for free, mixed-race people in central Virginia — most of whom were "free before the Civil War and general emancipation." Both William Harlan Gilbert Jr. (1946 Library of Congress memorandum, "Memorandum Concerning the Characteristics of the Larger Mixed-Blood Racial Islands of the Eastern United States") and Edward T. Price (1953, "A Geographical Analysis of White-Negro-Indian Racial Mixtures in the Eastern United States") classified the Amherst/Rockbridge "Issues" as **tri-racial isolates** — white, Native, and African ancestry, organized as endogamous communities. (Credibility 5/5.)

The Gilbert 1946 memorandum specifically identifies "300+ Rockbridge County Brown People" with "no tribal organization but [retaining] traditions of Indian origin." That is your community.

## 2. The 1839 Smallpox-Quarantine Origin Story

The family folklore claims a group of Indians traveling to Washington D.C. in 1839 contracted smallpox in Rockbridge County and were quarantined in the Irish Creek area, becoming the founding population.

**Searching for documentary support, I found:**

- **No direct documentation of an 1839 quarantine event in Rockbridge County.** Not in Morton's *History of Rockbridge County* (1920) page 146 or elsewhere in his text I could search. Not in D. E. Brady Jr.'s *Irish Creek: History and Diversions* (Rockbridge Historical Society, 2018 republication of a 1981 talk). Not in the 1837 Great Plains smallpox literature (which centered on the Upper Missouri, not Virginia). Not in the Cherokee removal literature.
- **The Cherokee Treaty Party did move overland in 1838–39** (the Bell-Deas detachment), and the Cherokee delegations to Washington in this period are documented (Stand Watie, John Ridge, Major Ridge, Elias Boudinot signed the Treaty of New Echota in 1835 and lobbied in Washington afterward). But standard routes did not pass through Rockbridge County, and no published source ties them to Irish Creek.
- **An older alternative explanation fits better.** The Mason/Sorrells family on Irish Creek — Peter Mason (age 70 in 1884, "of Indian descent") and his wife Diannah Sorrells Mason — had a household of ~42 multigenerational family members described by Mormon missionaries as having "Indian blood discernable in most of their faces." This community was already present and identifiably Indian *before* 1839, suggesting in-place continuity from the historic Monacan/Saponi remnant that retreated into the Blue Ridge after 1700, not a single 1839 founding event.

**Verdict:** The 1839 smallpox-quarantine origin story is best treated as a **defensive folklore artifact** — an explanation invented or preserved to give the community a discrete, dignified, and *temporally bounded* Indian origin (one that doesn't require explaining centuries of free-people-of-color status, mixed marriages, or pre-removal continuity). It is exactly the kind of just-so story that arises when a community needs a clean answer to "where did your Indianness come from?" and the actual answer ("we never left, we just got reclassified") is harder to defend.

I cannot rule out that some 1839 event happened. I can say it does not appear in the searchable record. (Credibility of folklore as literal history: 1/5. Credibility of folklore as folklore: 5/5.)

## 3. The Clark Surname

Clark is one of the canonical Monacan-region tri-racial surnames. The evidence:

- **Plecker's 1943 target list** names *Clark* under both **Rockbridge County** and **Amherst County** as a family "Striving to Pass as 'Indian' or White." This is documentary proof — issued by the Commonwealth's own Bureau of Vital Statistics — that the Clark family was Indian-claiming and being actively reclassified by Virginia state machinery. ([Mixed Race Studies / Encyclopedia Virginia](https://mixedracestudies.org/wp/?p=62839); credibility 5/5.)
- **The 1876 case *James Clark v. J.P. Moore, Clerk of Rockbridge*** establishes Clark as a Rockbridge family in a documented Indian/white marriage-license fight a half-century before Plecker. James Clark sought a writ of mandamus after the clerk denied him a license to marry Cerinda Robison, "a white woman," on grounds that Clark was "a black man." Clark argued he was of mixed blood, less than 1/4 Indian or Negro. The court ruled in his favor and ordered the license issued. The case file includes correspondence with the Amherst County clerk inquiring into Clark's racial background — i.e., the Rockbridge and Amherst Indian communities were already understood as one shared population. (Library of Virginia finding aid 1140710; credibility 5/5.)
- **The 1924 *Sorrells v. Shields*** explicitly invoked the 1876 James Clark precedent. Atha Sorrells's evidence was: a family tree, federal land warrants, War of 1812 service records, the 1876 *James Clark* mandamus order, and depositions. Her pedigree was traced through **Polly Clark** (mother of James Clark of the 1876 case) → **James Clark** → **Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797)** → and her grandparents **Joe Clark Jr.** and **Julia (Sorrells) Clark.** The Sorrells case is, in genealogical fact, a Clark-family case operating under a Sorrells caption.

### "Joseph Anderson Clark" — the middle name
You flagged the middle name "Anderson" because Anderson is a common Monacan surname (per the Original Monacan Rolls at joepayne.org). The Anderson surname does **not** appear on the Plecker list for Amherst or Rockbridge that I retrieved (it does appear in some other Monacan/Saponi contexts), and joepayne.org confirms Anderson family lines linked to Greenville SC. The middle name "Anderson" in your great-grandfather's case is most likely a **maternal surname carried as a middle name** — a common Southern naming practice that preserves a mother's, grandmother's, or godmother's identity in the male line. That suggests his mother, grandmother, or close kin was from an Anderson family. Worth chasing in the 1860 / 1870 / 1880 census for Rockbridge.

### The 1797 Joe Clark Sr. anchor
The Sorrells case file traces back to Joe Clark Sr., born 1797. The Atha Sorrells / Native Heritage Project article notes he "owned slaves; purchased Peter Curry at auction." That detail is significant: it argues Joe Clark Sr. was *legally white* in pre-1865 Virginia (slaves could not own slaves; even "free negroes" rarely did so without legal jeopardy). Joe Clark Sr. had legal standing as a white man in 1820s–1850s Rockbridge — even while clearly carrying Indian heritage. This is the structural Virginia "free people of color" / "Issue" position: you appear in records sometimes as white, sometimes as mulatto, sometimes as colored, depending on which clerk wrote down which census in which year.

This shifting documentation is **the single most important pattern** for your genealogical work. You should collect every Clark / Tyree / Sorrells census entry across 1820–1930 and watch the race column; it will flip.

## 5. Sorrells v. Shields (1924–1925) — The Smoking Gun

This is the most important single document in your family's identity story.

**Caption:** *Atha Sorrells, by her next friend William Sorrells, v. A. T. Shields, Clerk of Circuit Court*, Rockbridge County (Va.) Circuit Court, judgment papers 1924–25. Library of Virginia finding aid: vi02800.xml; case file numbers 1140744 and 1164679. Filed with judgments for Nov 1924 (papers 1–15) and Jan 1925 (papers 9–10). The decision came down in early 1925; Atha married Robert Painter on May 31, 1925.

**Parties:**
- **Petitioner:** Atha Sorrells (b. 1904, d. 1979), pregnant, age 20, denied a marriage license to marry Robert Painter
- **Next friend:** William Sorrells (her father)
- **Respondent:** A. T. Shields, Clerk of the Rockbridge Circuit Court (Plecker's local enforcer; the same clerk to whom Plecker wrote his Rockbridge correspondence 1912–1943, finding aid LVA vi04027)
- **Witness for respondent:** Walter A. Plecker himself
- **Judge:** Henry W. Holt

**The Pleadings.** Atha's petition for mandamus argued (1) Indian ancestry, not African; (2) the 1924 Racial Integrity Act, on its face, was unconstitutionally vague — "Caucasian" lacked any settled scientific meaning; (3) under the Pocahontas exception (1/16 rule), even if she were Indian, she was less than 1/16; (4) the 1876 *James Clark v. Moore* mandamus had already adjudicated this exact line as legally white.

**The evidence.** Family tree chart back to Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797). War of 1812 service records. Federal land warrants. The 1876 *James Clark* mandamus. Depositions including Walter Plecker himself testifying for the defense. The chart noted: maternal line "Always white No question"; paternal line "predominantly white with a small amount of Indian blood."

**The Holding.** Judge Holt ruled: "the weight of [the evidence] is to the effect that there is no strain present in the applicant of any blood other than white, except Indian, and there is not enough of that to come within the statute." He critiqued the statute as overbroad and impossible to satisfy. Atha got her license. She married Painter on May 31, 1925.

**The Aftermath.** The Richmond Times-Dispatch (Nov 20, 1924) urged appeal. The Virginia Attorney General declined. Plecker himself declined to appeal — fearing an appellate court would gut the entire Racial Integrity Act on vagueness grounds. So the Sorrells decision stood as binding precedent in Rockbridge but Plecker continued enforcing the Act everywhere else. He just didn't push it in Rockbridge against this specific Indian-claiming family network again.

**Why this is a Clark-family case.** Atha Sorrells's grandparents were **Joe Clark Jr.** and **Julia (Sorrells) Clark.** Her ancestry chain went through Polly Clark and James Clark of the 1876 case. The Clark, Sorrells, and Painter surnames all appear on Plecker's Rockbridge County 1943 list. **Joseph Anderson Clark, b. 1869, age 55 in 1924–25, was a member of this exact Rockbridge Clark community.** Whether he was a deponent, signatory, or supporter is a question to ask the Library of Virginia case papers directly — they survive (1140744, 1164679).

**Practical next step:** Order the case file from the Library of Virginia. Read the depositions. Joseph Anderson Clark's name very plausibly appears.

(Credibility of the case as proven Clark-family family event: 5/5.)

## 7. Modern Monacan Citizenship — Could Brady Qualify?

### The legal framework
The Monacan Indian Nation became Virginia state-recognized on Feb 14, 1989, and federally recognized on Jan 30, 2018, under the Thomasina E. Jordan Indian Tribes of Virginia Federal Recognition Act. Tribal headquarters: 111 Highview Drive, Madison Heights, VA, on Bear Mountain in Amherst County. (Credibility 5/5.)

### Enrollment requirements (Monacan, 2025 packet)
- Lineal descent within **3 generations** to a Monacan ancestor on the **current Monacan Rolls**
- Burden of proof on the applicant
- Certified copies of Birth, Marriage, Death records for every ancestor in the chain
- Original state-issued certified birth certificate (not hospital keepsake, not registration card)
- Submitted to Tribal Office at 111 Highview Drive, Madison Heights, VA

### What that means for Brady's line
The structural question is: **does Joseph Anderson Clark (1869–1963) appear on a current Monacan Roll?** If yes — Brady is great-grandson, exactly 3 generations from JAC, qualifies on its face. If no — Brady has to demonstrate that some ancestor on the Clark line connects to a name on the rolls, which gets harder.

**What I could verify:** joepayne.org's Original Monacan Rolls (1700–1961, mostly marriage records 1875–1935) lists Clark, Tyree, Branham, Johns, Hicks, Beverly, Adcock, Redcross, Pinn, Terry. Joseph Anderson Clark himself was not specifically confirmed on the excerpt I could read, and Joepayne.org notes a controversy: some critics (including some Cherokee tribal members) argue several names on the Monacan rolls are actually Cherokee or Freedmen, not Monacan. This is contested territory.

**The practical path:**
1. Order *Sorrells v. Shields* case file from LVA. Look for Joseph Anderson Clark in the depositions, family-tree exhibit, or witness list. If he's named connecting to Joe Clark Sr. (1797) → that is your documented chain.
2. Pull 1850, 1860, 1870, 1880 Rockbridge & Amherst federal census slave-and-free schedules. JAC's parents and grandparents will be listed there, with a "Mulatto," "M," "FPC," or (post-1870) sometimes "Indian" race column entry. Ancestry.com or FamilySearch.org.
3. Acquire Donna Huffer's *Fare Thee Well Old Joe Clark*. It almost certainly contains JAC.
4. Acquire Peter W. Houck's *Indian Island in Amherst County* (1984). Pre-Houck, your community called itself Cherokee; post-Houck, Monacan. This is the academic anchor.
5. Acquire Jennifer Marie Huff's 2012 JMU thesis *A question of Indian identity in the Plecker Era: The Monacan Indian Nation in the twentieth century*. (PDF at commons.lib.jmu.edu, master201019/240.) She used Plecker's correspondence to A.T. Shields directly — i.e., the exact Rockbridge clerk who blocked Atha Sorrells. She likely names Clarks.
6. Contact the Monacan Tribal Office at Bear Mountain. Tell them your Clark/Tyree line through JAC and Mary Susan Clark. They will know whether the line is on the rolls and what they need to certify it.

## 8. Going Back Past 1869

Joseph Anderson Clark, b. 1869, is exactly the right vintage to have **Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797) as a great-grandfather** if generations averaged ~24 years (1797 → ~1821 → ~1845 → 1869). The Sorrells case ancestry chart already pegs Joe Clark Sr. as the family anchor. Atha Sorrells (b. 1904) was Joseph Anderson Clark's contemporary — both were great-grandchildren of that 1797 patriarch via slightly different branches.

The 1850 and 1860 federal censuses will list Joe Clark Sr. (then 53 / 63) and his children, including JAC's grandfather. They will list race. Depending on the year and the enumerator, expect "M" (mulatto), "W" (white), or in rare cases "I" (Indian) — never "Cherokee" or "Monacan" as a federal census category. The 1870 census is critical because it is the first to enumerate former free people of color and freedmen as full citizens, with race columns inconsistently applied.

**Slave schedules.** Monacans were generally not enslaved. Joe Clark Sr. *owned* a slave (Peter Curry, per Native Heritage Project's reading of the case file). He appears on the slaveholder side, not the enslaved side, of the 1850/1860 slave schedules. This is consistent with him being legally classified white in those decades.

**Pre-Plecker church records.** St. Paul's Mission on Bear Mountain (Episcopal, est. 1908) is the principal Monacan church record set, but JAC's family was on Irish Creek in Rockbridge, not Bear Mountain in Amherst. The Irish Creek church was **Mt. Zion**, founded 1908 by Sally Bruce Dickinson and the Presbyterians. D. E. Brady Jr.'s history is explicit: "all of the deacons were Clarks and about two-thirds of the elders were of that name." That is a huge clue. Mt. Zion Mt. Zion Presbyterian/Baptist Church records, if they survive, will name JAC's adult life and his parents. Try the Lexington Presbytery archives, Howard McKnight Wilson's *Lexington Presbytery Heritage* (1971), or the Rockbridge Historical Society. (Credibility 5/5.)

**Mountain View Methodist** was the Bear Mountain (Monacan) sister congregation. Not your congregation, but worth cross-referencing for cousin records.

## 10. Net Answer: WHO ARE WE, ACTUALLY?

You are descendants of **the Eastern Siouan-speaking Indians of central Virginia** — historically the Monacan confederacy, with absorbed Saponi/Tutelo/Occaneechi remnants that retreated into the Blue Ridge after 1700. Your specific line is anchored in the Irish Creek / Cornwall / Vesuvius / Alto Indian community of Rockbridge County, which is the northern wing of a continuous tri-racial population stretching south through Amherst County's Bear Mountain and Buffalo Ridge.

You are not Cherokee in the genealogical sense, even though some of your ancestors and living cousins called themselves Cherokee. Cherokee was a defensive label adopted because Monacan was unrecognized, because the Pocahontas exception protected Indian-claiming families only up to 1/16, and because Mormon and Anglo-American observers couldn't tell Eastern Siouan from Iroquoian and lumped everyone "Cherokee."

You are also not "colored" in the Plecker sense, even though Joseph and Mary Susan Clark's 1906 marriage record reads "colored." That was Plecker's clerk obeying Plecker's instruction, not a true ancestry claim. The Sorrells decision in 1925 — won by your cousins one generation later, on Joseph Anderson Clark's watch — broke that classification in Rockbridge.

You are **Monacan**, in the modern legal and tribal sense available to you, *if you do the documentary work* to connect Joseph Anderson Clark's line to a name on the current Monacan Rolls. The work is straightforward: pull *Sorrells v. Shields* from LVA, pull the 1850–1900 Rockbridge censuses, acquire Huffer's book, contact the tribal office. The hard part isn't finding the truth — it's finding the paper.

The folklore answer "Cherokee and Irish, smallpox-quarantined in 1839" is a story your family told itself when the truth was illegal to say. The actual answer is older, more honest, and more interesting: **you never went anywhere. You were always here. Plecker tried to make you not exist on paper. The Sorrells case and the 2018 federal recognition together make clear: the paper finally caught up with the people.**
