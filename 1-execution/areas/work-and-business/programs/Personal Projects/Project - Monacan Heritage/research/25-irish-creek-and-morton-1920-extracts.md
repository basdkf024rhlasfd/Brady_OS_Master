## Part A — D.E. Brady Jr., *Irish Creek: History and Diversions* (1981 talk / 2018 reprint)

### Bibliographic confirmation

- **Full title:** *Irish Creek: History and Diversions*
- **Author:** D. E. Brady Jr. (Douglas E. "Pat" Brady; b. 1916 — confirmed via internal footnote "When I was a boy I visited the site")
- **Author bio (PDF byline footnote, p. 1):** "Douglas E. 'Pat' Brady was mayor of Lexington from 1965 to 1971 and a town and city council member before that. He was a leader of Historic Lexington Foundation, Rockbridge Historical Society, the Stonewall Jackson House and Hospital and the Red Cross. Owner during his lifetime of Buffalo Forge, a National Historic District, he died in 2001."
- **Origin:** paper delivered to The Fortnightly Club, Lexington, Virginia, November 1981
- **Reprinted as:** *Rockbridge Epilogues* No. 16 — Autumn 2018, Historic Rockbridge / Rockbridge Historical Society
- **Length:** ~6 printed pages including U.S. Geological Survey map of Irish Creek, "Old Joe Clark" lyric box, photo of Mt. Zion Baptist Church, photo of the Alexander plaque
- **Brady's own stated sources (closing line, p. 5):** "Oral accounts; survey notes; 'Wildcatting on the Mountain' by Benjamin F. G. Kline Jr. (1970); 'Rockbridge County, Virginia: An Informal History,' by Dr. Edmund Pendleton Tompkins (1952); 'The Lexington Presbytery Heritage,' by Howard McKnight Wilson (1971)." Cites Morton 1920 p. 170 by footnote for the Daniel Dougherty forge.

**Surname-coincidence flag:** D.E. "Pat" Brady (1916–2001) is unrelated to Brady Smallwood. Master-sources A8 already noted this.

### Verbatim extract: the "mixed blood" passage — load-bearing

Page 2, column right, top-to-mid:

> "By the early 1920s, Irish Creek had a reputation of backwardness and lawlessness. The people were spoken of as 'Irish Creekers' or, worse, 'Dominickers' by non-mountain people. Usually the references were to a number of families of mixed blood — white, Indian, and possibly Negro. There are few of these people left there; they seem to have been among the first to migrate to the towns.
>
> "It may be due to my limited contacts and this may be an unfair generalization, but I believe that the present people are eager to be friendly and are outgoing to strangers, while often being suspicious and unforgiving of their own people."

(Brady's footnote ii on the second sentence: "This observation goes against the stereotype that they are insular and suspicious of outsiders.")

Source: D.E. Brady Jr., *Irish Creek: History and Diversions* (Rockbridge Epilogues No. 16, Autumn 2018), p. 2.

**Why it matters:** Brady — Lexington mayor, Rockbridge insider, peer-audience Fortnightly Club talk in 1981 — describes the Irish Creek community in print, by name, in the same paragraph as the "Dominickers" slur. Corroborates Morton 1920's "brown people" passage (Part B) and Gilbert 1948 (master-sources E6b), bracketing the community across **at least three independent published sources spanning 1920–1981**. Brady's date-stamp ("By the early 1920s") and his out-migration line ("among the first to migrate to the towns") confirms the documented Plecker-era → mid-century migration that explains the community's thinness on Irish Creek itself by 1981. His "possibly Negro" hedge is exactly the racial-classification ambiguity Sorrells (1924) and the Nelson Harrison Clark 1860 mulatto census entry (file 12) settled empirically.

### Verbatim extract: Mt. Zion church + Clarks as deacons and elders

Pages 2–3:

> "Religion and education came to Irish Creek about the same time. In 1908, Miss Sally Bruce Dickinson founded the Irish Creek Mission at a settlement about eight miles up the creek from South River. The site was described by Dr. Howard Wilson as 'an isolated, spiritually underprivileged mountain community.' Soon enough, a drunken element burned the mission school. The people of the area then furnished the materials to rebuild it and then to erect a new chapel. In 1912, after an appeal to the governor, teachers paid by the state came to the school. These Christian women taught small children in the morning, youth in the afternoon, and adults at night. Soon a Sunday School was started and preaching services were held, with the Presbyterians supplying the ministers. The church, called Mt. Zion, was formally opened in November 1912. **During its existence as a Presbyterian Church, all of the deacons were Clarks and about two-thirds of the elders were of that name.**"

Source: D.E. Brady Jr., *Irish Creek: History and Diversions* (Rockbridge Epilogues No. 16, Autumn 2018), pp. 2–3 (emphasis added).

**Why it matters:** The single most concrete printed statement of Clark dominance in the Irish Creek religious community in the secondary literature. Brady states, with no qualifiers, that **all** Mt. Zion deacons (1912 forward, until dissolution as Presbyterian mission in 1962) were Clarks, and two-thirds of the elders were Clarks. **JAC was 43 in 1912 — he is in the document set this sentence describes.** The Presbyterian Mt. Zion does not contradict the 1911 LDS conversion (master-sources A4 / Olson) — Clarks were the spine of *both* the Presbyterian and LDS branches simultaneously, suggesting the family was the religious-leadership family of the entire community across denominational lines.

**Photo caption verbatim (page 3 sidebar):** "Mount Zion Baptist Church on Irish Creek Road — descended from Miss Sally Dickinson's 1908 Irish Creek Mission (later, Mount Zion Presbyterian Church)."

The church survives as **Mount Zion Baptist** today, on Irish Creek Road, descended from the 1908 Mission. Its cemetery and member rolls are likely the best surviving body of post-1962 Clark family records still in private community hands.

### Verbatim extract: Tyree's grist mill

Page 4, column left, bottom:

> "Tyree's grist mill operated near the old furnace site. To date, I have not been able to locate the remains."

Source: D.E. Brady Jr., *Irish Creek: History and Diversions* (Rockbridge Epilogues No. 16, Autumn 2018), p. 4.

**Why it matters:** Brady's only Tyree mention in the entire 6-page paper — unambiguously placed at Irish Creek, "near the old furnace site" (the Daniel Dougherty 1779 forge at the mouth of Irish Creek that Brady cites Morton p. 170 for). Anchors the Tyree family in the same micro-geography as the Clarks; corroborates the joepayne.org Tyree Monacan-rolls footprint (master-sources D22) and supports Tier B2 (Tyree as co-equal Monacan branch). That Brady — a meticulous local historian — could not locate the physical remains by 1981 makes the Tyree mill site an archaeology-grade field-survey target, not an extant landmark.

### Verbatim extract: "Old Joe Clark" / Sam Downey

Pages 4–5:

> "The area also made its contribution to the world of music. One Sam Downey, an illiterate teamster, had music in his soul. As he drove along he would make up jingles and tunes and sing them over and over. One of his songs, 'Ole Joe Clark,' came to be included in books of folk songs and was much played by so-called hillbilly bands. I expect that some of the verses used by the bands never passed the lips of Sam Downey. Dr. Tompkins tells in his book of a patient who had known Sam.
>
> "'Yes, I knowed him; when he got too old to work he came to live with his married daughter near our house. One day he took a bucket of swill to the hogpen, he walked up on a ramp and poured the swill into the hog trough, as he straightened up he fell over backward, and when someone got to him he was plumb dead.' [footnote viii: Edmund Pendleton Tompkins, *Rockbridge County, Virginia* (Richmond, 1952), pp. 91–93.]"

Sidebar lyric box (page 4):

> "**Old Joe Clark** — Old Joe Clark had a mule / His name was Morgan Brown / And every tooth in that mule's head, / Was sixteen inches around. // Old Joe Clark had a cow, / She was muley born, / It takes a jay bird a week and a half, / To fly from horn to horn."

Source: D.E. Brady Jr., *Irish Creek: History and Diversions* (Rockbridge Epilogues No. 16, Autumn 2018), pp. 4–5.

**Why it matters (and what it does NOT prove):** Brady — writing in 1981, before the Penick (1956) letter and Seeger (1983) oral tradition were synthesized — locates the *Old Joe Clark* song's authorship at **Sam Downey, an illiterate Irish Creek teamster**, not at Joseph Carter Clark Sr. as the 1956 Penick letter claims. Brady's source is Tompkins 1952 (a book Brady knew personally and cites). Per master-sources G1, the Kentucky claim (Joseph Clark of Clay County KY, b. 1839) has stronger external documentation than the Virginia / Irish Creek claim. **In Brady's reading, the Joe Clark of the song is the song's subject; Sam Downey is its documented author** — and Sam Downey himself is a real local figure who died on a hogpen ramp at his married daughter's home. Future drafts must phrase the Old Joe Clark connection at G1's corrected level only.

### Other Irish Creek context surfaced (no new genealogical content)

- **Geography (p. 1):** "the main stream has a rather gentle fall for its entire length, taking fifteen miles to drop from its origin near Montebello, at an elevation of 2,800 feet, to the South River at Cornwall." — Brady places Irish Creek mouth at Cornwall, headwaters at Montebello, exactly the corridor where the joepayne.org Clark/Tyree marriages cluster.
- **Hessian settlement (p. 1):** "After the Revolutionary War, some Hessians had settled along the Nettle Creek part of the watershed, and in 1866 there was an attempt to form a settlement of Polish people in the Irish Gap area, but the settlement did not succeed." — Hessian settlement is documented surname source for the Painter / Pultz / German-pattern surnames in master-sources D35.
- **Norvell Flats Black hermit (p. 2):** "Norvell Flats is named for a black man who had a cabin there." — distinguished by Brady from the larger mixed-blood Clark/Tyree settlement upcreek; a separate household.
- **Furnace and Daniel Dougherty (p. 3):** "About one-half mile into the gorge are the very faint remains of an iron furnace. The early-20th-century historian Oren Morton says that it was built in 1760 by one Daniel Dougherty." — Brady cites Morton 1920 p. 170 by footnote. (Morton actually says "1779" not 1760 — Brady's slip; verbatim Morton text in Part B below.)

### What is NOT in Brady 1981 — the surname silence

Complete sweep against the convention #11 surname list:

- **Clark — twice (deacon dominance + song subject)**
- **Tyree — once (grist mill)**
- **Norvell, Dougherty, Downey — each once**
- **Curry, Sorrells, Cash (as a name), Floyd, Hartless, Mason, Branham, Johns, Pultz, Painter — none. Zero hits.**

Brady's silence on the other Plecker-list surnames is a finding: a Lexington insider in 1981 named only **Clark, Tyree, Norvell, Dougherty, Downey** — five surnames in 6 pages, with Clark and Tyree co-located and dominant. This should not be read as evidence those families weren't there; it should be read as evidence Brady's lens was Mt. Zion + Tyree mill, and that the Sorrells / Cash / Hartless / Floyd / Pultz / Painter component had largely migrated out by 1981 — exactly the pattern Brady himself describes ("among the first to migrate to the towns").

### Verbatim extract: page 146 — "Brown People" of Irish Creek (Chapter XVI: The Negro Element)

Page 146 header confirms via djvu line 7755: `146 A HISTORY OF ROCKBRIDGE COUNTY, VIRGINIA`. Brown People passage falls a few sentences later, technically straddling p. 146 into 147 (page 148 header at line 7845). Verbatim, lightly OCR-corrected by visual inspection of the page-image viewer:

> "In 1910 the negroes of this county were sixteen per cent of the population, and paid taxes on land and personalty assessed at $237,505. The gregariousness of the race is indicated in the fact that of the aforesaid amount, $155,653 belonged in Lexington town and district.
>
> "It is worthy of mention that Amy Timberlake, daughter of a negress brought from Africa, lived to a greater age than any other resident of Rockbridge, so far as our information goes. She died in 1897 at the age of 107.
>
> "**In the middle course of Irish Creek is a considerable community sometimes known as the 'brown people.' They live the simple life in their little log cabins which dot the valley and the bordering hillsides. In the veins of many of them is the blood of the Indian as well as that of the African, but the Caucasian type is dominant.**"

Source: Oren F. Morton, *A History of Rockbridge County, Virginia* (Staunton: McClure Co., 1920), pp. 146–147 (Chapter XVI: The Negro Element). Verbatim from djvu lines 7782–7795, page-aligned against `archive.org/details/historyofrockbri00mortrich?page=146`.

**Why it matters:** **The single most-cited but never-verbatim-pulled passage in the v1 research stack.** A 1920 published paragraph — four years before the 1924 Racial Integrity Act — in which a mainstream Virginia county historian states for the record that the Irish Creek community is (1) Indian-descended, (2) African-descended, (3) Caucasian-dominant in appearance. **All three components.** The community is named ("brown people") and geographically pinned ("middle course of Irish Creek"). Direct external corroboration of Brady 1920s memory ("mixed blood — white, Indian, and possibly Negro") writing 60 years later. Morton's text is published openly under the McClure Co. imprint in Staunton in 1920; four years later the same community appears in court records, school correspondence, and Plecker's circulars only under euphemisms ("free issue," "mongrel," "negro"), eventually surname-list erasure. **Future v2 drafts should put Morton p. 146 and Plecker's 1943 surname circular on facing pages.**

**Taxonomic note (significant for Tier B6):** The "brown people" passage sits under **Chapter XVI: The Negro Element**, NOT in any Indian chapter. Morton 1920 was already operating in the binary white/colored frame the 1924 Racial Integrity Act would harden into law. Plecker's "either white or colored, no third category" rule was not Plecker's invention — it was already the working frame in Morton's text. Foreground evidence for the v2 Demise + Divergence chapter.

### Verbatim extract: page 170 — Daniel Dougherty's 1779 forge at the mouth of Irish Creek

Page 170 header confirms via djvu line 8881. Brady's footnote iii (Part A) cited "Morton 1920, p. 170" — that page reads:

> "When we come to the iron industry there is a larger story to tell. The smelting of iron began west of the Blue Ridge in 1700, and the beginning seems to have been in Rockbridge. By 1779 we know that Daniel Dougherty was operating a forge near the mouth of Irish Creek. It is said that cannonballs made here were fired at the British in Yorktown in 1781. In 1799, there is mention of the forges of McCluer and Nicholas Vanstavern. The former seems to have been near the site of Buena Vista."

Source: Morton 1920, p. 170. Date is **1779**, not 1760 — Brady misremembered.

## Synthesis

1. **Two independent mainstream-published Rockbridge sources spanning 60 years** (Morton 1920, Brady 1981) describe the same Irish Creek mixed-blood community, name Clarks and Tyrees by surname, locate them in the same micro-geography, and confirm Indian + African + Caucasian admixture before Plecker had legal authority to suppress documentation.
2. **Mt. Zion-as-Clark-stronghold is now verbatim-confirmed.** Brady 1981: "all of the deacons were Clarks and about two-thirds of the elders were of that name." JAC was 43 at Mt. Zion's 1912 opening — he is in this document set. Mount Zion Baptist on Irish Creek Road (the modern descendant) is a live action target for cemetery + member-roll work.
3. **"Old Joe Clark" framing is corrected.** Brady 1981 → Tompkins 1952 names **Sam Downey, an illiterate Irish Creek teamster**, as the song's documented author — Joe Clark is the subject, Sam Downey is the author. Future drafts must phrase at master-sources G1's corrected level only.
4. **Morton 1920 p. 387–391 is the index page** for Tier B1 (Clark line 1700–1869) and Tier B7 (the First Clark). South River District alone has 43 Plecker-list-surname households on the 1917 tax list — every documented surname for this community alive and on the tax books in one district before Plecker's apparatus deployed.
5. **Morton 1920 p. 441+ WWI roster is the named-individual matching primary source** for Tier B1. Ten Clark draftees (two at Irish Creek, two at Cornwall) plus five Tyrees, five Hartlesses, two Floyds, two Painters — federally registered, racially designated on draft cards, retrievable free.
