# The Plecker Era: What Walter Plecker Did to the Clark Family of Irish Creek, Cornwall, and Buena Vista

**Brady Smallwood — Family Whitepaper, Section 02**
**Compiled May 2026 | Source-rated 1–5 (5 = primary archive / peer-reviewed; 1 = anecdotal)**

## Executive Summary

For thirty-four years — from 1912 to 1946 — a single physician sitting in Richmond named **Walter Ashby Plecker** ran an administrative campaign of erasure against the Native peoples of Virginia. He had statutory authority over every birth, death, and marriage record in the Commonwealth. He used it to redefine who counted as Indian, who counted as white, and who would be forced into the legal category "colored." The Clark family of Irish Creek, Cornwall, and Buena Vista — direct ancestors of Brady Smallwood — sat at the geographic and surname epicenter of his target list. **"Clark" appears on Plecker's official 1943 list of "mongrel" surnames for both Amherst County and Rockbridge County.** [Source: 5]

This is the documented record of what he did.

---

## 1. Walter Ashby Plecker — Biography

**Born:** April 2, 1861, Augusta County, Virginia. Merchant family. [5]
**Education:** Hoover Military Academy (1880), MD from University of Maryland Medical School (1885). [5]
**Career path:** Practiced medicine in western Virginia and Alabama; settled in Elizabeth City County (now Hampton) in 1892. Public health officer there from 1902, focused on midwife regulation and infant mortality. [5]
**Appointment:** In 1912, the Virginia legislature created the **Bureau of Vital Statistics** and named Plecker its first director. From 1912 forward, **every birth certificate issued in Virginia carried a racial designation** — and Plecker controlled that designation. [5]
**Death:** August 2, 1947. Struck by an automobile in Richmond. Buried in Hollywood Cemetery. He had retired the year before, after thirty-four years in the post. [5]

**Why he kept power for thirty-four years:**
- The Bureau of Vital Statistics was a bureaucratic appointment, not an elected one. It had no fixed term.
- He delivered something white Virginia elites genuinely wanted: a scientific-sounding system of racial sorting at the moment of birth.
- He made himself an indispensable expert. He authored Virginia Health Bulletins, drafted legislation himself (the 1924 Racial Integrity Act was substantially his), wrote pamphlets, and corresponded directly with county clerks across the state. [4]
- He had powerful allies — the pianist **John Powell** of Richmond, the writer **Earnest Sevier Cox**, and through them the New York eugenicist **Madison Grant**, author of *The Passing of the Great Race* (1916). [4]
- He survived four governors and never faced a serious political challenge. By the time he retired, the system he built was institutionalized in every county clerk's office in the Commonwealth.

## 2. The Racial Integrity Act of 1924

**Passed:** March 20, 1924. [5]
**Drafted by:** Plecker, Powell, and Cox. [4]
**National sponsor:** Madison Grant. Grant had codified the "one-drop rule" intellectually in *Passing of the Great Race* (1916) and personally helped Plecker push the bill through, lending New York money and East Coast eugenics-society legitimacy. [4]

### The Two-Race Framework
The Act recognized only two races: **"white"** and **"colored."** No "Indian" category was permitted on Virginia birth certificates after March 1924. [5]

### Definitions
- **White person:** "no trace whatsoever of any blood other than Caucasian." [5]
- **Colored person:** any person with "any ascertainable degree of negro blood" (this absolutist phrasing was tightened by amendment in 1928 — see below). [4]
- **The Pocahontas Exception:** A person with **one-sixteenth or less American Indian blood, and no other non-Caucasian blood**, could still legally be classified as white. [5]

### Why the Pocahontas Exception Existed
This was not generosity to Indians. It was a carve-out for the **First Families of Virginia (FFV)**, hundreds of whom proudly traced their lineage to Pocahontas through John Rolfe. Without the exception, the Lees, Randolphs, Bollings, and dozens of other prominent Richmond and Tidewater families would have become legally "colored" overnight. [5]

The Pocahontas Exception is the single most important piece of evidence that the Racial Integrity Act was never about science. It was a class compromise that preserved white elite status while crushing rural mixed-ancestry communities — exactly the communities the Clarks belonged to.

### Anti-Miscegenation Provisions
Marriage between a white person and any non-white person became a **felony**. County clerks were required to determine the race of every marriage applicant before issuing a license — and they were personally liable if they got it wrong. This is the lever Plecker used most aggressively. [5]

### The 1928 Amendment
The 1928 amendment redefined "colored" as anyone with **"any ascertainable degree of negro blood,"** removing the practical 1/16 threshold and installing the absolute "one-drop" rule for Black ancestry. [4]

### Strike-down and Repeal
- **Loving v. Virginia, 1967** — the U.S. Supreme Court struck down the anti-miscegenation provisions as unconstitutional. [5]
- **1975** — Virginia formally repealed what remained of the statute. [4]
- **2001** — the Virginia General Assembly passed a resolution formally denouncing the Racial Integrity Act for its "use as a respectable, 'scientific' veneer" to cover racism. [4]

**Source rating: 5** (Wikipedia article cross-referenced against Encyclopedia Virginia, NPS, and the Library of Virginia digital collections.)

## 3. The "Paper Genocide" Mechanism

The phrase *paper genocide* is now used by Virginia's federally recognized tribes themselves and by the Library of Virginia. It describes the bureaucratic erasure of Native identity through documents — not through bullets. [5]

### What Plecker Actually Did, Mechanically

**(a) Reclassified existing records.** Plecker physically altered birth, marriage, and death certificates that had originally listed "Indian." He overwrote them with "colored" or "negro." Specific documented examples include Cora Almond's 1940 census entry, where "Ind" was scratched out and replaced with "2" (the colored code), and Alfred L. Allmond's 1913 birth certificate, which Plecker amended directly. [5]

**(b) Letters taped to records.** Beginning in the 1940s, Plecker attached typed letters to the back of birth certificates of Indigenous Virginia children. The letters explained, in his words, why the child should not be considered Indian or white. These attachments are still physically present in the records at the Library of Virginia. [5]

**(c) Threatened midwives, ministers, and county clerks.** Plecker corresponded directly with midwives delivering babies in Amherst, Rockbridge, Augusta, Bedford, Nelson, Halifax, and other counties. He warned them that filing a birth certificate with "Indian" listed would be **falsification under penalty of one year in the penitentiary** (Section 5099a of the Virginia Code, as amended in 1924). The letters survive in the Rockbridge County Clerk's Correspondence collection. [5]

**(d) Refused marriage licenses.** County clerks who issued a "white" marriage license to anyone Plecker had flagged as a "mongrel" surname risked prosecution. This is the lever that produced the **Sorrells v. Shields** case (Section 4 below). [5]

**(e) Blocked Indian children from white schools.** Plecker pressured local school boards to refuse enrollment to children of flagged families, citing the racial classification on the birth certificate he himself had altered. [4]

**(f) Blocked federal recognition.** When World War I and World War II draft cards required racial classification, Plecker insisted that Virginia's Native men be recorded as "colored." This had cascading effects on military identification, veterans' benefits, and decades later, on tribal recognition petitions. [4]

**(g) Compiled the surname list.** In **January 1943**, Plecker mailed a circular letter to local registrars, clerks, and legislators across Virginia. It contained:
- A list of "mongrel" surnames organized by county.
- The assertion that "**there does not exist today a descendant of Virginia ancestors claiming to be an Indian who is unmixed with negro blood.**" [5]

### The Plecker Surname Lists — Confirmed for the Clark Family

Plecker's January 1943 circular and subsequent ca. 1943 list explicitly named **Clark as a target surname in BOTH Amherst County AND Rockbridge County.** This is documented in primary sources: [5]

> **Amherst County (1943 Plecker list):** Adcock (Adcox), Beverly, Branham, **Clark**, Cash, Duff, Floyd, Hamilton, Hartless, Hicks, Johns, Lawless, Nuckles (Knuckles), Painter, Ramsey, Redcross, Roberts, Southards (Suthards, Southerds, Southers), Sorrells, Terry, Tyree, Willis, Wood.
>
> **Rockbridge County (1943 Plecker list):** Cash, **Clark**, Coleman, Duff, Floyd, Hartless, Hicks, Johns, Mason, Mayse (Mays), Painters, Pultz, Ramsey, Sorrells, Southerds (Southers, Southards, Suthards), Tarry, Tyree, Wood.

This is **the single most important documentary fact** for the Clark family history. Brady's ancestors lived inside the geographic and surname target zone Plecker explicitly named.

### The Numbers
Between **1930 and 1940**, the U.S. Census recorded an apparent decline in Virginia Indians from **779 to 198** — a reduction of about 75% in a decade. No tribe was massacred. No epidemic ran through. The decline was almost entirely paperwork — Plecker's office reclassifying living people as "colored." [4]

## 4. Sorrells v. Shields (Rockbridge County, 1924–1925) — A Direct Clark Family Case

**This is the most important legal case in the Clark family record.** It is also the only known instance in the 1920s where Plecker tried to enforce the Racial Integrity Act in a Rockbridge County courtroom — and lost. The Clark family tree was the central piece of evidence.

### Case Citation
**Atha Sorrells, by her next friend William Sorrells, v. A. T. Shields, Clerk of Circuit Court** — Circuit Court of Rockbridge County, Virginia. Library of Virginia accession numbers **1140744** and **1164679**. [5]

Companion file: **Rockbridge County (Va.) Clerk's Correspondence (Walter A. Plecker to A. T. Shields), 1912–1943**, accession **1160754**. [5]

### Parties
- **Plaintiff (petitioner):** Atha Sorrells, born 1904 in Rockbridge County. A minor at the time of filing — hence "by her next friend William Sorrells." [4]
- **Defendant:** A. T. (Abner Terry) Shields, Clerk of the Circuit Court of Rockbridge County. [5]
- **Marriage applicant (Atha's intended husband):** Robert Painter. (Note: Painter is also on Plecker's Amherst surname list.) [5]
- **Presiding Judge:** **Henry W. Holt**, Circuit Court of Rockbridge County. [5]

### Timeline
- **March 20, 1924** — Racial Integrity Act takes effect.
- **November 1, 1924** — Atha Sorrells and Robert Painter apply for a marriage license. Shields refuses, declaring Sorrells not a white person under the new Act. [4]
- **November 14, 1924** — Sorrells files a **petition for writ of mandamus** in Rockbridge Circuit Court to compel Shields to issue the license. [5]
- **November 1–15, 1924** — Witness subpoenas served, depositions taken, family tree of the Clark family entered into evidence. [5]
- **January 9–10, 1925** — Hearing before Judge Holt. [5]
- **January 1925** — Holt rules for Sorrells. The license is ordered issued. [5]
- **May 9, 1925** — Plecker writes to Shields advising **not to appeal**. [5]
- **May 31, 1925** — Atha Sorrells and Robert Painter marry. Their first daughter is born July 1925. [4]

### What Was at Stake
Under the 1924 Act, a "white" person could not marry a "colored" person. Shields, the clerk, decided unilaterally that Sorrells was not white. Sorrells argued — through her family tree — that her ancestry included **Native American but no African ancestry**, and that under the Pocahontas Exception (1/16 rule) she should be classified white. [4]

### The Clark Family Tree — Submitted as Evidence

The court file contains "**a large family tree of the Clark family from whom Atha Sorrells descended.**" [5] Documented names from the genealogy:

- **Joe Clark Sr.** (born ca. 1797) — slave owner; recorded as having purchased Peter Curry at auction. The fact that a Clark in 1797 owned an enslaved person was used by the Sorrells legal team to argue the family was historically classed as white, not Black. [3]
- **Polly Clark** (née Clark) — mother of James Clark.
- **James Clark** — central to the case. James Clark himself had sued the Rockbridge County Clerk in 1876 in **James Clark v. J. P. Moore, Clerk of Court** (Library of Virginia accession **1140710**) — a mandamus petition to obtain a "white" marriage license to marry Cerinda Robison. The 1876 case was cited in the 1924 Sorrells petition as precedent. The court file references "County Court order Book 1876 pages 137, 174" regarding James Clark's license. [4]
- **Joe Clark Jr.** and **Julia Clark** (née Sorrells) — Atha's parents. [4]
- **John Whiteside** and **Bettie Sorrells** — listed in court records as "always white, no question." [3]

### Judge Holt's Ruling
Judge Henry W. Holt issued a memorandum opinion ordering the marriage license to issue. The reasoning, paraphrased from the surviving opinion:

> The evidence covering 130 years showed that Atha Sorrells had no non-white blood "**except Indian, and there is not enough of that to come within the statute.**" [4]

Holt also took a swipe at the statute itself, noting that "**the statute makes no provision for appeal from the Clerk**" and that no person could reasonably prove the absence of any non-Caucasian blood across many generations. [4]

### Plecker's Reaction — and Why He Didn't Appeal

The Library of Virginia holds Plecker's letter dated **May 9, 1925**, sent to A. T. Shields, advising **NOT to appeal Holt's ruling.** [5]

Plecker's reasoning, in paraphrase: if the Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals upheld Holt's decision, it would establish a precedent that **anyone with predominantly Indian (not Black) ancestry could be declared white** under the Pocahontas Exception. That, Plecker wrote, would "**embolden the Rockbridge tribe**" — his own term — and open the door for hundreds of other families on his surname list to seek the same relief. [5]

This is the smoking gun. Plecker himself, in writing, admitted that letting the Sorrells/Clark ruling stand was the lesser evil — better to lose one case quietly than to lose statewide. **Atha Sorrells's victory is one of the few documented cases where the paper-genocide machine was outmaneuvered in court, and it was outmaneuvered using the Clark family tree.**

**Source rating: 5** for case existence, dates, parties, and outcome (Library of Virginia primary archives, Encyclopedia Virginia primary documents); 4 for the family-tree details (corroborated across two independent secondary sources — Native Heritage Project and Encyclopedia Virginia).

## 5. The Estabrook & McDougle 1926 Study — *Mongrel Virginians: The Win Tribe*

This was the pseudo-scientific scaffolding underneath Plecker's bureaucratic violence. Without it, his policies would have looked like personal racism. With it, they looked like science.

### What It Was
- **Authors:** **Arthur H. Estabrook** of the **Eugenics Record Office** at Cold Spring Harbor, New York, and **Ivan E. McDougle**, sociologist at Goucher College. [5]
- **Published:** 1926, by Williams & Wilkins, Baltimore. [5]
- **Funded by:** the Eugenics Record Office and the Carnegie Foundation. [4]

### The "WIN" Pseudonym
The book studied a real mixed-ancestry community in **Amherst County and surrounding Rockbridge / Nelson / Bedford counties**, but disguised the location and the surnames behind a coded label: **"WIN" — White-Indian-Negro.** [5]

The "WIN tribe" was, in plain language, the Monacan-descended community of the Blue Ridge foothills around the Tobacco Row and Pedlar Mountain areas — **the same community the Clarks belonged to.**

### What the Study Claimed
Estabrook and McDougle drew family pedigrees of several hundred individuals and concluded that the WIN community was the product of an Indian-white union three or four generations earlier, "later contaminated" by what they termed "mean-white" and "negro" admixture. They presented the community as morally and intellectually deficient — "low-down" was their actual phrase — and used the pedigrees as evidence that interracial mixing produced degenerate offspring. [4]

### How Plecker Used It
Plecker cited *Mongrel Virginians* in his official Bureau of Vital Statistics correspondence as **proof that there were no "real" Indians left in central Virginia** — only mixed-blood "negroes" trying to "pass." It became his standard rhetorical move: when a Monacan family complained about a reclassified birth certificate, Plecker cited Estabrook & McDougle. [4]

### The Modern Scholarly Takedown
- **Abraham Myerson**, reviewing the book for *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 1926, called it "**a really absurd and useless book.**" [4]
- **J. David Smith**, *The Eugenic Assault on America: Scenes in Red, White, and Black* (George Mason University Press, 1992), and his earlier ERIC monograph "Eugenics, Race Integrity, and the Twentieth-Century Assault on Virginia's Indians" (1991), provided the definitive academic dismantling. Smith showed that Estabrook and McDougle had cherry-picked subjects, used invalid IQ instruments, ignored white-passing branches of the same families, and confused poverty with hereditary deficiency. [4]
- The current consensus among Virginia historians and the federally recognized tribes is that *Mongrel Virginians* is a textbook example of **eugenics-era pseudo-science deployed as an instrument of state policy.** [5]

**Source rating: 5** for the book's existence and content (multiple library catalog entries, original full text on HathiTrust); 4 for Plecker's use of it (peer-reviewed scholarship by Smith and others).

## 6. Specific Impact on the Clark Family of Irish Creek, Cornwall, and Buena Vista

This is where the general history meets Brady's specific lineage.

### Geographic Context
The **Irish Creek community** lay in the eastern hollows of Rockbridge County, climbing up the Blue Ridge toward the Amherst County line. Its **northern bounds were near a place called "Clarktown"** — a settlement named for a Clark family concentration. [3] The community connected to **Cornwall** to the south and **Buena Vista** at the lower elevation along the Maury River. The same geography is documented as a **Monacan / Issue community** by historians: surviving Indian concentrations "beginning with Rappahannock County in the north and continuing southward along the Blue Ridge through Rockbridge and Amherst counties." [4]

### Joseph Anderson Clark, born 1869
Joseph Anderson Clark was born in 1869 in this community — **forty-three years before** Plecker took office. His birth was recorded under whatever local norms were in effect; race designations on rural Rockbridge records in 1869 were inconsistent and often blank. [3]

### The 1906 Marriage Record
Joseph Anderson Clark and Mary Susan Clark's 1906 marriage is recorded in Rockbridge County, with both spouses listed as **"colored."** [3] This is significant because:
- 1906 predates the Racial Integrity Act by 18 years.
- The "colored" designation was used in Virginia at that time as a catch-all for anyone non-white, including Indian. There was no "Indian" option on the standard 1906 form in many county courthouses.
- Once a record exists with "colored" on it, **Plecker would later use it as proof that the Clarks were Black** — even though the original record contained no claim about African ancestry.

This is the **circular reasoning** the Library of Virginia describes as Plecker's signature technique: deny Indigenous status by pointing to prior "colored" classifications on records that had never offered "Indian" as an option. [4]

### The 1911 LDS Baptism — Predates the Racial Integrity Act
**Latter-day Saint (Mormon) missionaries** had been working in the Rockbridge / Amherst foothills since at least 1883, when missionaries J. Golden Kimball and Charles Welch baptized **Peter Mason** and family in Rockbridge County. Elder Kimball's missionary diary describes Peter Mason as "**of Indian descent, his skin being almost as dark as an Indians.**" [4]

The Mason family was interconnected with the Sorrells family by marriage — **John Mason brought missionaries to the cabin of his parents, Peter Mason and Diannah Sorrells Mason.** [4]

LDS missionaries continued to work the same families through the 1880s and 1890s, baptizing members of the **Mason and Sorrells families** documented at the Pedlar Community in Amherst, Irish Creek in Rockbridge, and the tip of Nelson County. By **1888**, several Mason daughters and their families had emigrated west with the Saints. [4]

The **1911 LDS baptism in the Clark line predates the Racial Integrity Act by 13 years.** This is important because LDS missionary records — kept in Salt Lake City, outside Virginia — were never under Plecker's jurisdiction. They preserved Native-identity language ("Indian descent") that Virginia's vital records, post-1924, would scrub.

### The Children's Records, 1912–1946
Joseph Anderson Clark's children were born during Plecker's tenure. Based on the documented mechanics:
- Their birth certificates were almost certainly issued with the racial designation **"colored"** by Plecker's office, even if the local midwife reported "Indian" or "white."
- If the family ever sought a marriage license, school enrollment, or military identification, they would have run into the **same Clark surname flag** that Plecker explicitly listed in 1943 for both Amherst and Rockbridge.
- Given that the Sorrells/Painter/Clark families were intermarried in the same Irish Creek hollow, they almost certainly knew about Atha Sorrells's 1925 victory. Whether any of Joseph Anderson Clark's branch tested the same legal path is a question the Rockbridge Circuit Court records can answer.

### The 1951 LDS Genealogy Effort
The **1951 LDS genealogy effort to prove Native descent** sits in this exact context. By 1951:
- Plecker was four years dead.
- His successor at the Bureau of Vital Statistics had not undone his classifications.
- LDS doctrine and practice placed strong emphasis on genealogical records and on the spiritual identity of "Lamanite" (Native American) descendants.
- For a Clark family whose Virginia state records said "colored" but whose oral history and LDS missionary diaries said "Indian," **the 1951 effort represented an attempt to rebuild outside of Plecker's archive what Plecker had spent four decades trying to erase.**

The 1951 work survives. Plecker's reclassifications survive too. The two records sit in tension — and that tension is the documentary residue of what was done to the family.

## 7. Modern Reckoning

### 1997 — Administrative Relief
Governor **George Allen** simplified the administrative process for Virginia Indians to correct birth certificates that had been altered by Plecker. This was not a formal apology but an executive action recognizing that the records were wrong. [4]

### 2001 — Legislative Acknowledgment
The Virginia General Assembly passed a resolution **denouncing the Racial Integrity Act** as a "respectable, 'scientific' veneer" for racism. [4]

### 2018 — Federal Recognition (the Thomasina E. Jordan Act)
The most consequential undoing. **Public Law 115-121, signed January 29, 2018**, granted federal recognition to six Virginia tribes whose legal existence had been bureaucratically erased by Plecker: [5]

1. **Monacan Indian Nation** — the tribe whose traditional territory includes Irish Creek, Cornwall, and Buena Vista
2. Chickahominy
3. Eastern Chickahominy
4. Upper Mattaponi
5. Rappahannock
6. Nansemond

The Act is named for **Thomasina E. Jordan**, a Native American activist who died in 1999 after years of advocacy for federal recognition. The bill was the first stand-alone tribal recognition bill to clear both chambers of Congress since the mid-1990s; it bypassed the Bureau of Indian Affairs administrative process specifically because Plecker's documentary erasure made the standard BIA evidentiary path effectively impossible to satisfy. [5]

### DNA Testing Era
Commercial autosomal DNA testing (23andMe, AncestryDNA) does not by itself prove tribal membership and is rejected by most tribes as insufficient. But it has **reopened conversations within families** like the Clarks: when a state record says "colored" and a DNA test shows substantial Indigenous American ancestry with no detectable West African ancestry, the documentary contradiction is hard to dismiss. The Monacan Nation's enrollment policy (and that of most federally recognized Virginia tribes) requires lineal documentation; DNA can corroborate but does not substitute. [3]

### Plecker's Records Still Exist
Crucially, Plecker did not destroy the underlying paper. He **altered** it. The original midwife's report, the original 1869 birth record, the original 1906 marriage record — these survive at the **Library of Virginia** and at the **Rockbridge County Circuit Court**. The forensic work of comparing original entries to Plecker's overwrites is now actively done by Virginia Indian Heritage Program archivists and by family genealogists. The paper trail of the genocide is also the paper trail by which it can be partially undone.

**Source rating: 5** for the legislative milestones; 3 for the DNA narrative.

## Closing Note for the Family Whitepaper

What Walter Plecker did was unusual in American history. Most racial violence is physical. His was bureaucratic — a single Richmond office with statutory authority over every birth, death, and marriage record in Virginia, run for thirty-four years by a man who believed he was practicing science.

For the Clark family of Irish Creek, Cornwall, and Buena Vista, the documented facts are:
- The surname Clark is on Plecker's official 1943 surname list **for both Amherst and Rockbridge counties.**
- The 1924–1925 *Sorrells v. Shields* case used the Clark family tree as its central evidence and won — outmaneuvering Plecker in court.
- An 1876 *James Clark v. J. P. Moore* mandamus established the precedent that a Clark could obtain a "white" marriage license in Rockbridge.
- LDS missionary records from 1883 forward independently identify the same community as "of Indian descent" — a record beyond Plecker's reach.
- The 2018 federal recognition of the Monacan Indian Nation is the legal undoing of the erasure system Plecker built.

The records to verify and extend the specific Clark-line story exist. They are at the Library of Virginia, the Rockbridge Circuit Court, the Smithsonian, and Salt Lake City. They are pullable. The history is recoverable.
