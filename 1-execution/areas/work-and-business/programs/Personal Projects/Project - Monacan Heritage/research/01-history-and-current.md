# Monacan Indian Nation — History and Current State

**Research brief for the Smallwood family whitepaper**
Compiled: 2026-05-06
Audience: Brady's family, descended from the Clark family of Irish Creek / Buena Vista, Rockbridge County, Virginia, with Monacan ancestry

Source credibility scale: **5** = primary tribal source / peer-reviewed scholarship; **4** = state government, NPS, Encyclopedia Virginia, major newspaper of record; **3** = local newspaper, university press release, museum site; **2** = general reference (Wikipedia summaries cross-checked); **1** = uncorroborated personal site or hobbyist genealogy.

## 1. Monacan origins (precontact)

### The single biggest correction this whitepaper needs to make

**The Monacan are Siouan-speaking — not Algonquian (like Powhatan) and not Iroquoian (like Cherokee).** This is the most common error in family lore about Virginia Indian ancestry, and it matters because the linguistic family tree determines almost everything else: kinship, archaeology, ceremonial life, and which living tribes the Monacan are still connected to today. (Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, *Monacan Indian Nation*. Credibility: **5**.)

The Monacan belonged to the **Eastern Siouan** branch, the same family as the Tutelo, Saponi, Occaneechi, and Mannahoac. Linguists believe the broader Siouan family originated in the Ohio River Valley thousands of years ago and split as groups migrated east and west. The eastern branch settled across what is now Virginia, the Carolinas, and West Virginia. The western branch became the better-known Plains Sioux (Lakota, Dakota, Nakota). The Monacan and the Lakota are distant cousins on the same family tree — separated by perhaps three thousand years of migration. (Sources: Virginia Department of Education *First People*; Wikipedia *Tutelo language*. Credibility: **4**.)

### Pre-contact territory

At European contact in 1607, the Monacan and their allied tribes controlled **more than half of present-day Virginia**. The official tribal history places their range "from the Roanoke River Valley to the Potomac River, and from the Fall Line at Richmond and Fredericksburg west through the Blue Ridge Mountains" — the entire Piedmont and Blue Ridge of central Virginia, with the James River as the spine of the homeland. The Monacan held the territory above the falls of the James (modern Richmond); the Powhatan Confederacy held everything east of the falls down to the Chesapeake. (Source: Monacan Indian Nation official history, *monacannation.gov/our-history*. Credibility: **5**.)

The mountain anchor of the homeland was — and remains — **Bear Mountain in Amherst County**, west of Lynchburg. This is the spiritual center of the Monacan world and the continuous reference point of their identity for "hundreds of years" by their own account, and likely much longer based on archaeology. (Source: Encyclopedia Virginia. Credibility: **5**.)

The Clark family's origin point — Irish Creek and Buena Vista in Rockbridge County — sits squarely inside this pre-contact homeland, in the Blue Ridge foothills less than 40 miles west of Bear Mountain. Family ancestors there were not on the periphery of Monacan country; they were inside its core mountain redoubt. (Geographic confirmation: Wikipedia *Rockbridge County*, multiple sources on Bear Mountain location. Credibility: **3** — the family link is documented elsewhere; this brief only confirms the geography is consistent.)

### Population and society

The pre-contact Monacan numbered **at least 10,000 people**, and possibly as many as 15,000 across the alliance, organized into towns under regional chiefs. They practiced **mound burial**: bodies were placed in the open, then bones were ceremonially reinterred in earthen mounds raised over centuries. Thomas Jefferson famously excavated one of these Monacan burial mounds near the Rivanna River in the 1780s — described in *Notes on the State of Virginia* — and is sometimes credited as performing the first scientific archaeological excavation in North America. The mound he opened was Monacan. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; *Rassawek* Wikipedia; Monacan official history. Credibility: **5**.)

### Relationship to the Powhatan Confederacy — rivals, not subjects

This is the second major correction. Brady's family, like most Virginians, has probably absorbed the Powhatan / Pocahontas / Jamestown story as the Virginia Indian story. **The Monacan were the Powhatan's rivals.** Powhatan ruled the Algonquian-speaking Tidewater. The Monacan ruled the Siouan-speaking Piedmont. The two confederacies were "traditional enemies," and Powhatan repeatedly warned the English away from Monacan country in the early Jamestown years — partly because he wanted to monopolize trade with the colonists, and partly because the rivalry over hunting territory and copper trade routes ran deep. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; *virginiaplaces.org*. Credibility: **5**.)

When Brady's family talks about being descended from "Virginia Indians," the instinct is to picture Powhatan / Pocahontas. The truer picture is the *opposite* people: the upland Siouan rivals, fewer in number on the colonial record because they kept the English at arm's length and retreated into the mountains rather than treat with them.

### Capital town: Rassawek

The Monacan capital was **Rassawek**, located at the confluence of the Rivanna and James Rivers — what is now called Point of Fork, in Fluvanna County, about 45 miles west of Richmond. At the time of Jamestown's founding, Rassawek had roughly 1,500 residents, a 60-foot longhouse, and at least a dozen roundhouses. It was the political and ceremonial center of the entire Monacan alliance and a major regional trade hub. Archaeological evidence indicates continuous human habitation of the site for over 5,000 years — back to roughly 3000 BCE. (Sources: Wikipedia *Rassawek*; *virginiaplaces.org*. Credibility: **4**.)

### 1607 John Smith encounter

In **September 1608**, Captain Christopher Newport led an expedition up the James River from Jamestown into Monacan country — the first sustained English contact. Newport visited two Monacan towns, Mowhemicho and Massanack, and mapped three more: Rassawek, Monasukapanough, and Monahassanugh. The English never actually reached Rassawek itself. Captain John Smith published his famous 1612 *Map of Virginia* including these five Monacan towns based on Newport's expedition and accounts from Powhatan informants. The Monacan kept their distance and their secrets — there is no archaeological or documentary evidence of any European ever standing inside Rassawek. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; Wikipedia *Rassawek*. Credibility: **5**.)

This early standoffish posture is part of why the Monacan survived as a community — see Section 2.

## 2. Colonial period through the 19th century

### Disease, displacement, and dispersal (1607-1750)

European epidemics — smallpox, measles, influenza — devastated the Monacan population *before* sustained colonial settlement reached the Piedmont, just as they did for nearly every Eastern Woodland tribe. By the official tribal account, "European diseases devastated the population" before the English even pushed inland. Between 1607 and 1720, the Monacan gradually moved westward into the mountains. Some bands relocated north to Pennsylvania and ultimately to Canada, where Tutelo/Saponi descendants merged with the Cayuga Nation and survive in Ontario today. Others remained in the ancestral Amherst County mountain country. (Source: Monacan official history. Credibility: **5**.)

Rassawek itself was abandoned by the early 1700s. The capital simply emptied as the people withdrew westward. (Source: Wikipedia *Rassawek*. Credibility: **4**.)

### Retreat into the mountain hollows

Unlike the Powhatan tribes of the Tidewater, who negotiated treaties and reservations with the English (the 1646 and 1677 treaties created the Pamunkey and Mattaponi reservations that exist to this day), **the Monacan did not negotiate, did not treat, did not concentrate**. They dispersed. Families withdrew into the Blue Ridge hollows — the upland creek drainages of Amherst, Rockbridge, Nelson, and surrounding counties — and survived as small kinship clusters in country the colonists considered marginal mountain land.

This is the key mechanism by which the Monacan persisted as a community when other Virginia tribes were declared extinct. They were never on a reservation. They were never on a treaty. They were never enumerated as a tribe. They were classified by colonial and antebellum Virginia as "free people of color" — a category that was deliberately ambiguous and that Monacan families used to remain together while staying off the radar of state machinery. (Source: Houck, *Indian Island in Amherst County* (1984/1993), as summarized in Lynch's Ferry Magazine and Encyclopedia Virginia. Credibility: **4**.)

### The Bear Mountain settlement (1831-1833)

The continuous-community story has a specific founding event. In **1831–1833**, **William Johns** — a free man of color, age 63, who had married Molly Evans in 1790 — purchased over 400 acres on Bear Mountain from Landon Cabell for $400. He bought the land specifically as a refuge for his extended Indian family, "to establish a place where he and his family could reside apart from the prejudices of the rest of Amherst County." (Sources: *Lynch's Ferry Magazine*, "William Johns"; Monacan official history. Credibility: **4**.)

By the 1850 census, **29 large, interrelated Indian families** were living on or around the Bear Mountain settlement. This is the demographic core from which the modern Monacan Indian Nation descends — and the documentary chain (deeds, censuses, church records) anchored to William Johns is the spine of contemporary Monacan enrollment proof.

If your family's Clark line connects to Monacan ancestry through the Irish Creek / Rockbridge area, the linkage almost certainly runs through one of these Bear Mountain–era kinship networks — the Johns, Branham, Hicks, Hamilton, Terry, Willis, Redcross, Gibson, and Clark surnames recur across Monacan rolls. (Confidence on Clark specifically: **2** — surname appears in the regional record but specific lineage requires Monacan enrollment-grade documentation.)

### St. Paul's Episcopal Mission and the Bear Mountain Indian Mission School (1908)

Virginia's segregation laws barred Monacan children from white public schools, and the Monacan refused to attend "colored" schools because doing so meant signing on to a non-Indian classification. Around 1868 a small log cabin on Bear Mountain was used for occasional church services. In **1908**, the Episcopal minister **Arthur P. Gray Jr.** formally established **St. Paul's Mission** at the site and added classrooms to the cabin, creating what became known as the **Bear Mountain Indian Mission School**. (Sources: Encyclopedia Virginia; *episcopalassetmap.org*; Wikipedia *Bear Mountain Indian Mission School*. Credibility: **5**.)

A 1930 fire destroyed everything except the schoolhouse. From the early 1900s through **1964**, when Brown-era integration finally opened public schools to Monacan children, the mission school provided up to a seventh-grade education — the only formal schooling available to a Monacan child in Amherst County. It produced several hundred Monacan graduates over six decades and served as the social and ceremonial anchor of the community.

In **1995**, the Episcopal Diocese of Southwestern Virginia formally returned 7.5 acres of mission land to the tribe. In **1997**, the original log mission school was added to the National Register of Historic Places. It is now the **Monacan Indian Nation Ancestral Museum**. (Source: Monacan official history; Virginia DHR. Credibility: **5**.)

### Why the Monacan survived as a community

Pulling the threads together, the Monacan survived as a recognizable people while many other Virginia tribes were declared extinct because of an unusual stack of factors:

1. **Geographic refuge.** They retreated into terrain (Blue Ridge hollows around Bear Mountain) the colonists did not want.
2. **Endogamous kinship.** A small number of interrelated family lines — anchored to William Johns's 1833 land purchase — held together for five-plus generations.
3. **A church-school institutional anchor (1908-1964).** St. Paul's Mission gave the community a physical center, an educational pipeline, and a written record (baptisms, marriages, attendance) that later supported the recognition push.
4. **Documentary survivors.** Despite the Plecker era's deliberate "paper genocide" (Section 3), enough pre-1924 records survived — deeds, census entries, mission rolls, family Bibles — to rebuild the lineage chain.
5. **Refusal to assimilate downward.** Monacan families consistently refused the "colored" classification when it would have erased their Indian identity, even at the cost of education and mobility.

(Synthesis from: Houck, *Indian Island*; Monacan official history; University of Lynchburg student scholarship on the Racial Integrity Act. Credibility: **4**.)

## 3. The Plecker era (brief — covered in depth elsewhere)

The Virginia Bureau of Vital Statistics under registrar **Walter Ashby Plecker** (1912-1946) used the **Racial Integrity Act of 1924** to systematically reclassify nearly every Virginia Indian as "colored" on birth, marriage, and death records — a campaign Chickahominy Chief Stephen Adkins later called **"paper genocide."** The effect was statistical erasure: the 1930 Virginia census recorded 779 Indians; by 1940 the count had been reduced to 198. In 1942, several Monacan tribal members challenged Plecker directly, forcing him to admit he had no evidence supporting his reclassifications. (Sources: National Park Service, *The Racial Integrity Act, 1924: An Attack on Indigenous Identity*; Encyclopedia Virginia *Racial Integrity Laws*. Credibility: **5**.) **A separate brief in this whitepaper covers Plecker's bureaucratic mechanics, his correspondence campaign, and the human cost.**

## 4. The federal recognition push (1980s–2018)

### The road to recognition

The modern recognition campaign began with the 1988 incorporation of the Monacan Tribal Association as a Virginia non-profit, followed by **state recognition by the Virginia General Assembly on February 14, 1989**. Peter Houck's *Indian Island in Amherst County* (1984), which explicitly identified the Amherst County Indian community as Monacan, gave the recognition movement scholarly cover and external visibility it had lacked. (Source: Monacan official history; Encyclopedia Virginia. Credibility: **5**.)

For nearly two decades after that, the Monacan and five other state-recognized Virginia tribes (Chickahominy, Eastern Chickahominy, Upper Mattaponi, Rappahannock, Nansemond) lobbied Congress for federal recognition. The Bureau of Indian Affairs administrative path was effectively closed to them because the Plecker-era record destruction made the standard "continuous tribal community" documentation requirement nearly impossible to satisfy — the federal process required precisely the records the Commonwealth had spent decades destroying. So the tribes pursued a **legislative path** instead, repeatedly introducing recognition bills through Virginia's congressional delegation.

### The Thomasina E. Jordan Act, January 2018

The **Thomasina E. Jordan Indian Tribes of Virginia Federal Recognition Act of 2017** (H.R. 984, 115th Congress) passed both chambers of Congress and was **signed into law by President Trump on January 29, 2018** as Public Law 115-121. (Sources: Congress.gov; *Indianz.com*. Credibility: **5**.)

The bill is named for **Thomasina E. Jordan**, a longtime advocate for Virginia Indian recognition who died before seeing the law passed.

The Act federally recognized **six Virginia tribes**:

1. **Chickahominy Indian Tribe**
2. **Chickahominy Indian Tribe – Eastern Division**
3. **Upper Mattaponi Tribe**
4. **Rappahannock Tribe**
5. **Monacan Indian Nation**
6. **Nansemond Indian Nation**

(Note on the seventh Virginia tribe: The **Pamunkey Indian Tribe** was federally recognized **earlier**, in **2015**, through the standard BIA administrative process — making it the only Virginia tribe to take that route.)

### What federal recognition gives the Monacan — and what it takes away

Federal recognition makes Monacan citizens eligible for the full menu of federal Indian services and programs administered by the Bureau of Indian Affairs and other agencies: health (Indian Health Service), education (BIE programs), housing (HUD ICDBG), environmental (EPA tribal grants), and cultural protection (NAGPRA standing). The Act also requires the BIA to take certain pre-2007 lands into federal trust for the Monacan, but only in specified counties.

**Critical caveat:** the Act contains an **explicit, airtight prohibition on tribal gaming** for all six tribes — a concession the Monacan and the others accepted to clear the political path through Congress. There will be no Monacan casino. (Sources: *Indianz.com*; Public Law 115-121. Credibility: **5**.)

This is unusual in federal recognition law and worth flagging in family conversation: the Monacan path was about *survival, dignity, and services* — not the gaming-economics path that has dominated the public image of recognized tribes since the 1988 Indian Gaming Regulatory Act.

## 5. The current Monacan Indian Nation — institutional state

### Headquarters and footprint

Tribal headquarters are in **Madison Heights, Amherst County, Virginia**, on Bear Mountain. The tribe's land base now includes the original mission property returned in 1995, plus 110 additional acres on Bear Mountain purchased by the tribe in **1993**, plus subsequent acquisitions. The Bear Mountain Indian Mission School log cabin is now the **Monacan Indian Nation Ancestral Museum**, open to the public. (Source: Monacan official history; *monacannation.gov*; Wikipedia. Credibility: **5**.)

### Tribal government structure

The Nation is governed by:

- **Chief** and **Assistant Chief** (executive leadership, four-year terms)
- **Tribal Council** of seven elected citizens, serving as the link between the executive office and the broader tribal community
- **Tribal Administrator** plus a staff of approximately ten administrative employees running enrollment, environmental services, family services, housing, food bank, museum operations, and federal grant management.

(Source: *monacannation.com/government*; Bureau of Indian Affairs tribal leaders directory. Credibility: **5**.)

**Recent leadership history (important for family context):**

- **Sharon Bryant** — first female Chief in tribal history, elected 2011, died of cancer June 23, 2015. (Source: Wikipedia *Sharon Bryant*; *newsadvance.com*.)
- **Kenneth Branham** — Chief through the federal recognition victory of 2018 and the Rassawek victory of 2022. Re-elected to additional term(s).
- A 2022 internal governance dispute and federal lawsuit ("fake elections" allegations) signaled significant internal tension; election held June 17, 2023, with Chief sworn in June 24, 2023. As of 2024 reporting, the institutional structure (Chief, Assistant Chief, 7-member Council) remains intact.

(Source: *wset.com* July 2022 reporting; *ictnews.org*. Credibility: **3** — local TV reporting on intra-tribal politics; treat with appropriate care.)

### Membership

**Approximately 2,000–2,100 enrolled citizens** as of the most recent reporting — the largest of the six tribes recognized in the 2018 Act. Satellite communities exist in West Virginia, Maryland, Tennessee, and Ohio, reflecting the migration patterns of Monacan families through the 20th century, but Amherst County remains the demographic and ceremonial center. (Source: *Indianz.com* 2018; Wikipedia. Credibility: **4**.)

### Enrollment criteria (the Clark family question)

Monacan enrollment is based on **lineal descent**, not blood quantum. From the Monacan Nation's published guidelines:

> "The burden of proof of Monacan lineage is on the applicant, who must provide clear quality copies of acceptable documentation proving their lineage within 3 generations to a MIN ancestor/citizen on the current Monacan Rolls."

(Source: Monacan Indian Nation enrollment policy materials, summarized via search engine indexing of the official enrollment PDF; the PDF itself was binary-corrupted on direct fetch and could not be read line-by-line. Credibility: **3** for exact wording; **5** for the *principle* of lineal-descent-based enrollment, which is corroborated across multiple sources.)

Acceptable documentation chains run back to entries on the **historic Monacan Rolls** — primarily the surviving documentary record covering roughly **1700-1900**: the William Johns 1833 deed and successive deeds; pre-1924 federal census enumerations; Bear Mountain Indian Mission School records; St. Paul's Episcopal sacramental registers (baptisms, marriages, burials); and family Bibles. After 1924, the Plecker era systematically corrupted state-issued birth and marriage certificates, so post-1924 Virginia state records are generally *not* dispositive — applicants must reach back through the corrupted layer to pre-1924 documents.

A **separate genealogist's resource**, Joe Payne's *brightstar.html* page (joepayne.org), is widely cited in Monacan family research circles as a genealogy index, though the page also contains contested claims (some Cherokee-affiliated researchers have argued specific names on Monacan rolls are misattributed). The tribe's official enrollment process, not third-party websites, is the dispositive authority. (Source: joepayne.org/brightstar.html; cross-referenced search results. Credibility: **2** for the personal site as a primary source; useful as a research starting point only.)

**Practical guidance for the Smallwood family:** if Monacan enrollment is on the table for any descendants, the path runs through three steps — (1) build a documented lineage chain from Brady's generation back through the Clark family to a pre-1900 Monacan ancestor, (2) cross-reference that ancestor against the surviving Monacan Rolls held by the tribe's enrollment office, (3) submit a complete enrollment packet during the tribe's open enrollment window (currently ages 0-18 only, January 1 – March 31 annually; older age cohorts have not yet had a window opened by tribal council). (Source: official Monacan enrollment page. Credibility: **5**.)

### Annual gatherings

- **Annual Powwow** — third weekend of May (sometimes early June), at 225 Laurel Cliff Rd, Monroe, Virginia. Public event. Dancers, drummers, and singers from across North America. Gates 10 a.m., grand entry 12 p.m.
- **Annual Homecoming and Scholarship Auction** — first Saturday in October, on Bear Mountain. Tribal-citizen-focused but open to the public.

(Source: *monacannation.gov/powwow*; *visitamherstcounty.org*; *virginiahumanities.org*. Credibility: **5**.) These are the two best public-facing entry points for a family that wants to begin re-engaging with living Monacan culture.

## 6. Active issues 2020–2026

### The Rassawek victory (2014–2022)

The single most consequential Monacan public action of the past decade was the successful protection of Rassawek from a proposed water pumping station.

In **2014**, the **James River Water Authority (JRWA)** — a joint Fluvanna and Louisa County entity — announced plans to build a water intake and pumping station at Point of Fork to supply development around Zion Crossroads. The site is the documented location of Rassawek, the Monacan capital. Once the Monacan Nation gained federal recognition in 2018, it acquired formal Section 106 consulting-party standing in the federal permitting process and used that leverage hard.

Key escalation points:

- **2019** — A whistleblower exposed that the archaeologist hired by JRWA's consultant lacked proper credentials; the Virginia Department of Historic Resources rejected the cultural-resource survey.
- **2020** — Preservation Virginia listed Rassawek as endangered. The **National Trust for Historic Preservation named Rassawek to its "11 Most Endangered Historic Places" list**. Approximately 12,000 individuals and organizations filed comments opposing the project with the Army Corps of Engineers.
- **March 16, 2022** — JRWA voted unanimously to **abandon the Rassawek site** and relocate the pumping station 2.3 miles upstream. Archaeological testing at the alternative site (December 2021) showed no human remains. JRWA also **transferred ownership of the Rassawek parcel to the Monacan Indian Nation**.

(Sources: Wikipedia *Rassawek*; *culturalheritagepartners.com/saverassawek*; *fluvannareview.com* coverage 2022-2024; *National Geographic*. Credibility: **5**.)

This was a textbook case of federal recognition translating directly into protective leverage. Without recognition, the Monacan would have been a stakeholder; with recognition, they became a consulting party with legal teeth. The capital city the colonial record never recorded a European entering is now under Monacan ownership, four hundred years after Newport's 1608 expedition mapped it from a distance.

### Other land and sacred-site issues (current)

Monacan environmental and cultural-resource staff continue to engage in Section 106 reviews across central Virginia for federally permitted projects (transmission lines, pipelines, federally funded transportation, federal land actions). The 2024 EPA Priority Climate Action Plan for the Monacan Indian Nation indicates the tribe is also actively engaged in climate-resilience planning around water, forest health, and traditional foodways. (Source: *epa.gov* Monacan PCAP April 2024. Credibility: **5**.)

### Language revitalization — Tutelo-Saponi Monacan Living Dictionary

The Monacan language has been functionally extinct since the 19th century — the last fluent speaker of the closely related Tutelo dialect, **Nikonha**, died in 1871 at age 106 after recording roughly 100 words for the ethnologist Horatio Hale. (Source: Wikipedia *Tutelo language*. Credibility: **4**.)

A modern revitalization push is now active. The **Living Tongues Institute for Endangered Languages** received a Native Voices Endowment grant from the Endangered Language Fund to build the **Tutelo-Saponi Monacan Living Dictionary** — a digital dictionary targeting 3,000+ words and phrases with audio recordings, slated for completion in 2023 and continuing development. (Source: *livingtongues.org/tutelo-saponi-monacan-grant*. Credibility: **5**.)

The dictionary is a joint effort across descendants of the Monacan Alliance, including five tribes:
- Monacan Indian Nation
- Occaneechi Band of the Saponi Nation
- Sappony Tribe
- Haliwa-Saponi Tribe
- Saponi Nation of Ohio

Linguist **Corey Roberts** (University of Arizona doctoral candidate, community linguist) has led teaching efforts. Virginia Tech has hosted intertribal language gatherings. (Sources: *cals.vt.edu/magazine* Spring 2024; *ictnews.org*. Credibility: **5**.)

For a family teaching the next generation, this is the active, learnable, current cultural project. Words are coming back.

### Economic and educational programs

The tribe operates a working portfolio of federally funded and tribally administered programs through its administrative office — including housing assistance, family services, food bank, scholarship support (funded in part by the October Homecoming Scholarship Auction), environmental services, and museum operations at the Ancestral Museum. There is no casino and the Act explicitly forbids one; tribal economic development emphasizes services, cultural programs, federal grant administration, and environmental/heritage tourism around Bear Mountain. (Source: Monacan official history and program pages. Credibility: **5**.)

## 7. Key books, documents, and archives

The high-confidence reading and visiting list for the Smallwood family.

### Books

- **Peter W. Houck, *Indian Island in Amherst County*** (Lynchburg: Lynchburg Historical Research Co., 1984; expanded 2nd ed. 1993, with Mintcy D. Maxham). The foundational scholarly work proving the Amherst Indian community is Monacan. Houck was a Lynchburg physician and historian; the book directly enabled state and federal recognition. **Read this first.** Credibility: **5**.
- **Karenne Wood, ed., *The Virginia Indian Heritage Trail*** (Charlottesville: Virginia Foundation for the Humanities, 2007). Wood was Monacan, a poet, anthropologist, and director of the Virginia Indian Programs at Virginia Humanities. The Heritage Trail is the canonical guidebook to all eight (now eleven, with federal-era expansion) Virginia tribes' physical sites and cultural centers. Credibility: **5**.
- **Karenne Wood, *Markings on Earth*** (2001) and ***Weaving the Boundary*** (2016). Her two collections of poetry. *Markings on Earth* won the Diane Decorah Award from the Native Writers' Circle of the Americas. Wood died of cancer on **July 21, 2019**, at her home in Fluvanna County; her ashes were spread over Bear Mountain. **Acknowledging her loss to the family is appropriate** — she was the most visible Monacan public intellectual of the recognition era, and she did not live to see Rassawek protected in 2022. Credibility: **5**.

### Archives and documentary collections

- **Bear Mountain Indian Mission School records** — sacramental registers, attendance records, photographs. Held jointly by the Monacan Indian Nation Ancestral Museum (Bear Mountain) and the Episcopal Diocese of Southwestern Virginia archives. The single richest documentary source for late-19th-/early-20th-century Monacan family lineage. Credibility: **5**.
- **Virginia Indian Archive** (*virginiaindianarchive.org*) — digital archive maintained by Virginia Humanities, including the Monacan Nation collection, the Bear Mountain Mission School photographs, and the State Recognition documentary record. Credibility: **5**.
- **Encyclopedia Virginia, *Monacan Indian Nation* entry** — the best-curated single-page overview, peer-reviewed under Virginia Humanities editorial control. Credibility: **5**.
- **Library of Virginia, Dictionary of Virginia Biography** — biographies of Karenne Wood, Walter Plecker, and other figures central to the Monacan story. Credibility: **5**.

### Smithsonian / National Museum of the American Indian

- The **Smithsonian sponsored excavations at Monasukapanough** (a Monacan town site in Albemarle County) in **1911 and 1931**, and holds artifacts from those excavations.
- A **1979 federal Native American grant** funded a Monacan pottery cooperative at the Bear Mountain mission; some pieces were acquired by the Smithsonian.
- Karenne Wood worked with the **National Museum of the American Indian (NMAI)** prior to her 2019 death; the Monacan Nation maintains an active relationship with NMAI's Cultural Resources Center in Suitland, Maryland.

(Source: cross-referenced from multiple search results above. Credibility: **3** — specific catalog accession numbers and full holdings list would require a direct NMAI Cultural Resources Center inquiry.)
