# Monacan Indian Nation — Leadership, Governance, and Recent Controversies

**Prepared for:** Brady Smallwood
**Tone:** Investigative-journalist, public-records-only
**Date:** 2026-05-06
**Scope:** Current leadership, federal-recognition era leaders, Rassawek fight, federal recognition, enrollment rules, key cultural figures, and active 2024-2026 issues. No speculation; gaps flagged where the public record is sparse.

---

## 1. Current Chief & Council (2024-2026)

**Chief:** Diane Shields (Linda Diane Johns Shields), elected **June 17, 2023** at the regularly scheduled tribal election held at the close of Kenneth Branham's term. She is the second woman to hold the office, after Sharon Bryant. Shields was born in Virginia and relocated to Ohio at age five; her paternal heritage is Monacan. Before running for chief she served as Assistant Chief, Tribal Secretary, and Tribal Treasurer at various points, sat on the Monacan-Pamunkey-Mattaponi (MPM) Consortium and United Indians of Virginia boards, and after federal recognition in 2018 directed the Monacan Nation Cultural Foundation. She also did substantial documentary work assembling the genealogical evidence used in the BIA petition. (Source: tribal bio PDF, monacannation.gov; cred 5.)

**Assistant Chief:** Edith "Lou" Branham Parrish — eldest daughter of Ronnie and Eleanor Branham (both Monacan). (Source: monacannation.gov; cred 5.)

**Tribal Council** (per the official government page, monacannation.gov, fetched 2026-05-06):
- Dean Branham
- Daniel B. Branham
- Mark Bryant
- Bertha Riley
- Amber Rasnick Fink
- Isaiah Moss
- Rhonda Matthews

**Other officers:** Tribal Secretary Teresa Pollak; Tribal Treasurer Pamela Thompson.

**Election cadence:** Chief terms have historically been four years; Branham served four consecutive terms (16 years) before stepping down in 2023. The official site references a "BIA Letter regarding Elections" but does not publish the full procedural rules to the public web. (Cred 4 — partial visibility.)

**Contested elections / governance dispute (2022-2023):** In June 2022, nine Monacan members — led plaintiff **Bobby Thompson Jr.** — filed suit in U.S. District Court for the Western District of Virginia (Lynchburg Division) against then-Chief Kenneth Branham, Tribal Administrator Adrian Compton, and Secretary Amber Fink (now a sitting Council member). The complaint alleged the leadership "improperly and unlawfully" prevented the plaintiffs from receiving roughly **$91,600** in collective per-capita distributions of federal funds (CARES Act / federal recognition-era allocations) due to them as enrolled members. (Source: News & Advance, WSET ABC13 Investigates; cred 4.)

The plaintiffs claimed they had held a separate vote to remove Branham and his council and elected their own replacement council. The defense, led by Cultural Heritage Partners attorney **Greg Werkheiser**, characterized the breakaway slate as "a pretend government" and argued the dispute was a non-justiciable intra-tribal political controversy protected by tribal sovereign immunity. Branham's side described it as a small faction unhappy with the size of their checks. The plaintiffs framed it as one side following Monacan and federal law while the other "made up rules as they go." (Source: WSET; cred 3-4 — local broadcast paraphrase of partisan quotes.)

**Outcome:** A federal judge dismissed the suit in **June 2023**, holding the court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction over an internal tribal dispute and that the Nation's sovereign immunity barred the claims. The case is *Brooks v. Branham* (W.D. Va.). (Source: News & Advance June 2023; Native American Rights Fund / Turtle Talk archive of the order; cred 5.)

The dismissal removed the federal lever; nothing in the public record since 2023 indicates the breakaway slate has continued as a formal challenger, and the official tribal site lists Amber Fink (one of the original named defendants) as a current Council member, suggesting the Branham-era leadership and its successors absorbed the controversy without structural fracture. **Gap:** there is no published forensic accounting of how the federal funds in dispute were ultimately distributed.

## 2. Chief Kenneth Branham — Federal Recognition Era

- **Years served:** Approximately **2007 through June 2023**. Public reporting describes him as serving "four terms" totaling 16 years; the New Era Progress called June 18, 2023 his last day as chief. (Cred 4 — exact start year not nailed down to a single source; first re-election covered by *Indian Country Today* archive in the late 2000s.)
- **Defining accomplishment:** Federal recognition of the Monacan Nation under the Thomasina E. Jordan Act, signed January 29, 2018 (PL 115-121). Branham was the public face of the Monacan piece of an 18-year campaign and is the chief most associated with the win.
- **Other accomplishments:** Public-facing education work; museum upgrades at Bear Mountain; coordination with the other five Virginia tribes during the federal-recognition push; a tribal headquarters expansion in Madison Heights post-recognition; purchase of approximately 1,300 acres in Amherst County for tribal infrastructure; groundbreaking in 2021 of the IHS-funded Mid-Atlantic Tribal Health Center on Highview Drive (13,000 sq ft, primary care + dental + behavioral health + diabetes + pharmacy + lab). Construction was still progressing into 2023 per local press.
- **Why he stepped down:** Branham publicly framed it as voluntary generational handoff. Quote in News & Advance: *"I think it's time to step down and let new leadership emerge, and I believe it will."* He acknowledged he was asked to run again and believed he probably would have won. **Public record contains no statement linking his departure to the 2022 lawsuit**, though the timing — election held just over a year after the suit was filed — is observable. He did not resign mid-term; he served out his elected term to its scheduled June 2023 end. (Cred 4 — direct quote, but motivations beyond the quote are not in the public record.)

## 3. Sharon Bryant — First Female Chief (2011-2015)

- **Full name & dates:** Sharon Rebecca Bryant, June 22, 1961 – June 23, 2015 (died one day after her 54th birthday). (Source: News & Advance obituary; Find a Grave; Wikipedia; cred 5.)
- **Tenure:** Elected in **2011**, serving until her death in **2015**. First woman to hold the office of Monacan chief. (Note: an early WSET-cited search result called Diane Shields the "first female chief," which is incorrect; Bryant was first, Shields is second. Treat that single citation as wrong.)
- **Background:** Resident of Monroe, Virginia. Lay minister at St. Paul's Episcopal Church (the parish historically tied to the Bear Mountain Mission); reportedly seeking ordination as an Episcopal priest at the time of her death.
- **Accomplishments as chief:** Continued the federal-recognition campaign that Branham would ultimately complete; advocated for tribal community welfare; positioned as a unifier and ceremonial figure during a period when the recognition push was stalled in Congress.
- **Cause of death:** **Liver cancer**, at her home, June 23, 2015. (Source: News & Advance, "Monacan Chief Sharon Bryant dies of cancer"; cred 5.) A memorial service drew tribal members, state officials, and Episcopal clergy.

## 4. Rassawek Pipeline Fight (2018-2022)

**The fight:** The James River Water Authority (JRWA) — a joint utility of Fluvanna and Louisa counties — announced in 2017 plans to build a pumping station and water-intake project at the confluence of the James and Rivanna rivers near Columbia, Virginia. The site is **Rassawek**, documented by Captain John Smith in 1612 as the "chiefest town" of the Monacan, and the location of ancestral burials. Archaeological evidence places continuous Monacan occupation across approximately 200 generations / ~4,730 years. (Source: Cultural Heritage Partners; National Trust for Historic Preservation; Wikipedia; SELC; cred 5.)

JRWA had not consulted the Monacan Nation before announcing the location. The conflict became one of the most-watched cultural-resources fights in the eastern U.S., named to the National Trust's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places list. (Cred 5.)

**Who led the fight:**
- **Monacan Indian Nation** as the political principal. Chief Kenneth Branham and his council were the formal voice; Karenne Wood (until her July 2019 death) and Marion Werkheiser / Greg Werkheiser of **Cultural Heritage Partners** were prominent in cultural and legal strategy.
- **Southern Environmental Law Center (SELC)** provided environmental and permitting counsel.
- **National Trust for Historic Preservation** elevated the campaign nationally.
- Coalition partners included tens of thousands of citizens and preservation organizations; the campaign deployed federal levers under the National Historic Preservation Act, NEPA, and the Clean Water Act (404 permitting through the Army Corps of Engineers).

**Outcome:** On **March 15-16, 2022**, JRWA voted **unanimously** to abandon the Rassawek site and pursue an alternative location at the **Forsyth site**, approximately two miles away. Monacan-retained archaeologists confirmed low risk of ancestral remains at the alternative. The Nation issued a public letter of support for the relocated project. JRWA also agreed to **transfer the Rassawek parcel it had acquired to the Monacan Indian Nation**. (Source: Virginia Mercury; Washington Post; SELC; 29News/CBS19; cred 5.)

**Significance:** This was the first major test of the Monacan Nation's federal recognition powers. Archaeologist Jeffrey Hantman, in a 2024 interview, observed that recognition let the tribe invoke federal law to assert *"This will not happen. This is a sacred site, the ancestors are buried here, it was the chiefest of our towns, and it should not be disturbed."* (Source: National Trust 2024 retrospective; cred 5.)

**Tribal members on the other side:** **The public record contains no documented case of a Monacan tribal member publicly siding with JRWA against the tribal government's position.** The opposition was almost entirely from the two utility-member counties' boards and JRWA staff. Flag this as a clean-record finding rather than evidence of unanimity — internal dissent that did not surface in press wouldn't appear in a public-records-only review.

## 5. Federal Recognition (2018) — The Thomasina E. Jordan Act

- **Statute:** Thomasina E. Jordan Indian Tribes of Virginia Federal Recognition Act of 2017, **Public Law 115-121**, signed by President Trump on **January 29, 2018**. House version: H.R. 984. (Source: congress.gov, govtrack; cred 5.)
- **Senate sponsors:** Senators **Tim Kaine (D-VA)** and **Mark Warner (D-VA)** were the lead Senate cosponsors of the companion bill S. 691 and managed the floor process; both publicly celebrated passage. (Source: kaine.senate.gov, warner.senate.gov press releases; cred 5.) The package was the first congressional tribal-recognition bill in over a decade and reflected an unusual bipartisan Virginia delegation effort.
- **Six tribes recognized:** Chickahominy; Eastern Chickahominy (Chickahominy Indians–Eastern Division); Upper Mattaponi; Rappahannock; Monacan; and Nansemond. The Pamunkey had been federally recognized separately in 2015 via the BIA administrative process. (Cred 5.)
- **Gaming:** The Act explicitly **prohibits gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act** for these six tribes. This was a deliberate political concession to secure passage; gaming opposition had been a key obstacle to prior recognition attempts. (Source: PL 115-121 text; Hobbs Straus general memo 18-006; cred 5.)
- **Practical effects:** Eligibility for federal grants and programs only open to recognized tribes (HHS/IHS, HUD ICDBG, BIA, USDA), repatriation rights under NAGPRA, government-to-government consultation requirements, and standing to assert tribal sovereign immunity in federal court — the lever that ended the 2022-2023 Brooks v. Branham case.

## 6. Enrollment Criteria & Disputes

**Standard:** Lineal descent — **not blood quantum**. The Monacan enrollment packet requires applicants to document direct lineal descent **within three generations** to a Monacan citizen on the current rolls. Required documents include certified state-issued birth certificates, marriage records, and death records sufficient to construct the family tree to a documented Monacan ancestor. The burden of proof rests on the applicant. (Source: monacannation.gov enrollment page; enrollment_packet_8_5_2025.pdf; cred 5.)

**Open enrollment windows:**
- **Ages 0-18:** Open annually **January 1 - March 31**.
- **Ages 19-59:** Currently closed — *"No dates currently set by Council."*
- **Ages 60+:** Currently closed — *"No dates currently set by Council."*
- Applications submitted outside the window or for closed age groups are returned unprocessed. (Source: monacannation.gov/enrollment, fetched 2026-05-06; cred 5.)

This is a tight policy: an adult who has never enrolled cannot currently apply. The 0-18 window functions as a generational membership-perpetuation mechanism rather than a re-opening of the rolls. **Implication for descendants seeking enrollment as adults:** the gate is structurally closed. This is pertinent to anyone — including descendants of the Clark/Irish Creek line in Rockbridge County — investigating present-day citizenship pathways.

**Buffalo Ridge Cherokee dispute (Amherst County):** A separate community in the same geography — the **United Cherokee Indian Tribe of Virginia (Buffalo Ridge Band of Cherokee)**, with members in Amherst and Appomattox/Nelson counties — claims Cherokee identity rather than Monacan. The historical question is real: prior to **Peter Houck's 1984 book** *Indian Island in Amherst County*, most "free people of color" in the Buffalo Ridge area who claimed indigenous ancestry identified as **Cherokee**. Houck was the first scholar to argue many were instead Monacan descendants. (Source: virginiaplaces.org; Wikipedia; Saponitown.com; cred 4.)

In **2013**, the Virginia General Assembly considered state-recognition bills for the Appalachian Cherokee Nation and the United Cherokee Indian Tribe of Virginia. The bills failed — opposition came from the **three federally recognized Cherokee tribes** (Eastern Band, Cherokee Nation, United Keetoowah Band), who routinely oppose state recognitions of unaffiliated Cherokee groups. The Monacan Nation has not formally taken a public position on Buffalo Ridge identity claims; the public record reflects an *implicit* boundary dispute rather than an active tribal-vs.-tribal conflict. (Cred 3 — sparse public record on Monacan internal posture toward Buffalo Ridge.)

**Disenrollment cases:** The most documented case is the **1996 banishment of George Whitecalf**, attributed in secondary sources to a change in Monacan political leadership. Whitecalf's banishment was eventually reversed and his membership restored. (Source: Wilkins & Wilkins, *Dismembered: Native Disenrollment and the Battle for Human Rights*; cred 4.) **Gap:** no other named Monacan disenrollment cases surface in public sources. The 2022 *Brooks v. Branham* dispute did not allege disenrollment — it alleged denial of per-capita distributions, which is a distinct issue.

## 7. Karenne Wood — Poet, Director of Virginia Indian Programs

- **Dates:** **May 31, 1960 - July 21, 2019**, died of cancer at her home in Fluvanna County. (Source: Wikipedia; Library of Virginia DVB; UVA Today; Virginia Humanities; cred 5.)
- **Roles:** Director of **Virginia Indian Programs at Virginia Humanities** (formerly Virginia Foundation for the Humanities), based in Charlottesville. Directed a tribal history project for the Monacan Nation. Served on the National Congress of American Indians' Repatriation Commission. Conducted research at the National Museum of the American Indian.
- **Academic distinction:** Earned a doctorate in linguistic anthropology from the University of Virginia in **2016** — the **first Monacan to receive a UVA doctorate**.
- **Literary legacy:** Published poet (*Markings on Earth*; *Weaving the Boundary*); studied the Monacan language (no longer actively spoken). Named to the Library of Virginia's "Virginia Women in History" in **2015**.
- **Significance to Monacan storytelling:** Wood was the principal public translator of Monacan history and identity to non-Native audiences across the federal-recognition decade. Her tribal history project produced much of the genealogical and cultural archive Monacan leaders cited in the BIA petition. UVA installed a memorial portrait of her in 2020-2021.
- **Successor:** **Savannah Baber** was named the new Coordinator for Virginia Indian Programs at Virginia Humanities in **April 2023**. Baber descends from the Chickahominy Tribe of Virginia and the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina — not Monacan — so the Virginia Humanities role is no longer Monacan-held. (Source: virginiahumanities.org; cred 5.) **Gap:** the public record does not identify a single named "successor" to Wood as the *Monacan*-specific cultural-historical voice. That role appears to have diffused across Cultural Heritage Partners, the Monacan Nation Cultural Foundation, and individual tribal members; no one figure has clearly inherited her platform.

## 8. Active 2024-2026 Issues

**Legislative advocacy (2025 Virginia General Assembly):** Chief Diane Shields and counterparts from the other federally recognized Virginia tribes lobbied for **HB 2134 / SB 949**, a bill updating the Virginia Code to formally reflect the presence and inherent sovereignty of federally recognized tribal nations headquartered in the Commonwealth. Patrons: **Del. Paul Krizek (D)** and **Sen. Ryan McDougle (R)**, both of whom received Tribal Champion awards from tribal leaders in 2025. (Source: Cultural Heritage Partners; cred 4.) **Gap:** I did not confirm final passage status of HB 2134/SB 949 as of this writing.

**Health center construction:** The **Mid-Atlantic Tribal Health Center** on Highview Drive (Madison Heights) — IHS-funded, 13,000 sq ft, primary care + dental + behavioral health + diabetes + pharmacy + lab — broke ground October 2021, was progressing in mid-2023 per local press, and is intended to serve tribal members across the federally recognized Virginia tribes. **Gap:** I could not confirm a public opening date through standard 2024-2025 press searches. (Cred 3 — opening status unclear.)

**Land base expansion:** The Nation has acquired roughly **1,300 acres** in Amherst County for community infrastructure (health, senior, housing, cultural). The transfer of the Rassawek parcel from JRWA was part of this land-back posture. (Cred 4.)

**Litigation:** No publicly reported new litigation involving the Monacan Indian Nation has surfaced in 2024-2026 beyond the dismissal of *Brooks v. Branham* in June 2023. The American Bar Association's *Recent Developments in Tribal Court Litigation* surveys for 2024 and 2025 do not flag a Monacan matter. (Cred 4 — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.)

**Cultural visibility:** Coverage of Lily Gladstone's 2024 Best Actress Oscar nomination (*Killers of the Flower Moon*) prompted Monacan women to comment on representation; the Nation's annual Powwow held its 31st installment in 2024. (Source: WSET; cred 4.) These are low-stakes signals of normal tribal cultural life, not flashpoints.

**Open-source intelligence sparse zones:**
