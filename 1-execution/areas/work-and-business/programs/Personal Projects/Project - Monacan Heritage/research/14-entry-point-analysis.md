# Where Native Ancestry Enters Brady's Direct Paternal Clark Line — Entry Point Analysis

**Compiled:** 2026-05-07
**Method:** Direct Bright Data scrapes of WikiTree, FamilySearch, and Heinegg's *Free African Americans of North Carolina, Virginia, and South Carolina* (the canonical Virginia free-people-of-color reference). Cross-referenced with the Henrico County Marriage Records (Lindsay), the Lost Colony Research Group's Heinegg-Clark extraction, and the Amherst County will of William Clarke (1821).
**Importance:** Resolves the structural question for the whitepaper. Identifies the documented entry point of Native ancestry in Brady's biological direct paternal Clark line, with citation chain.
**Status:** ✅ Resolved at one generation deeper than the previous research file (`13-edith-wane-parents-resolved.md`) suggested.

## 1. Executive Summary

**The strongest case is Candidate 3 — James Clark Sr. (1764–1840) himself, and behind him his father William Clarke (~1735–1821) of Amherst County, Virginia. Confidence: 4/5.**

Heinegg's *Free African Americans* registry — the canonical, peer-cited Virginia FPOC reference — directly catalogs this exact family. **James Clark Sr. is identical to "James3 Clark, born say 1764"** in Heinegg's Clark family entry. He is documented as a Mulatto / "man of color" in Amherst County, Virginia tax rolls 1789–1820. His wife "Anny" (= Anney Moseley) and their land sale to Henry Hartless in 1802 are explicitly named.

His father William Clarke (~1735, Amherst, d. before 15 Oct 1821) is identical to **Heinegg's "William1 Clark, born say 1755"** of Amherst — head of an Amherst County household of 7 "free persons" in 1783, 8 in 1785, 9 "other free" in 1810; classified "Mulatto" in 1813 and "man of color" 1811–15 in Amherst tax rolls. **He received a 72-acre land patent on the headwaters of Pedlar River in Amherst County on 20 July 1780** — the documentary anchor of the Clark family's Pedlar River presence (which is exactly the Monacan core territory adjacent to Bear Mountain).

**Candidate 1 (Harrison Wane) — eliminated as dispositive entry point.** Edith Wane's father Harrison Wane (b. 1773) remains undocumented, but the question is now moot. Edith Wane married into Brady's line in 1819 — but Brady's direct Clark line was already documented Mulatto by 1810 in Amherst County tax rolls, *before* Edith entered it. The admixture is upstream of her marriage, not through her.

**Candidate 2 (Anna Moseley) — eliminated as the entry-point ancestor, but partially confirmed as part of the network.** WikiTree assigns her to the well-documented English-origin Lower Norfolk/Henrico Moseley family (Thomas Moseley + Phoebe Giles → Arthur Moseley II → Arthur Moseley → William Moseley of Norfolk, originally Delft, Netherlands). The WikiTree page itself flags the attribution as needing "careful reassessment" with "no serious level of confidence." But even granting it, Anna Moseley would have been a settler-class woman who *married into* an already-Mulatto Clark family — the FPOC classification of the Clark family in Amherst pre-dates her arrival.

**Candidate 3 (James Clark Sr.) — confirmed as the documented chain link, but the deeper origin moves one generation further back to William Clarke (~1735).** William Clarke's parents are *not documented* in any record I located. The trail goes cold one generation back. But the family tradition recorded on WikiTree (sourced from Ruth Knight Bailey's *Lost Tribes: Indian Mormons in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Virginia*) explicitly states: *"the first Clarks were three brothers with Indian brides... I believe those Indian brides were either full or half Monacan Indians."* The implied founding marriage is at William Clarke's parents' generation or earlier — i.e., Brady's 9x or 10x great-grandparents.

**The Hartless family is the smoking gun for community membership.** The 1785 Henrico County marriage of James Clark and Anney Moseley was witnessed by **James Heartless (= James Hartless)** as one of the two sureties. The Hartless family of Amherst County descends from Henry1 Hartless (~1725–1802), a "mulatto" presented by the Spotsylvania County court on 4 May 1761 for cohabiting with a white woman. (FamilySearch attributes Henry's mother as "Monaccan Indian"; WikiTree more conservatively lists her as a slave woman of unknown origin. Either attribution places the family in the early-1700s mixed-race population.) **The Hartless family is repeatedly named on Plecker's 1943 Rockbridge and Amherst lists, and is in every academic catalog of the Monacan/Bear Mountain Indian community.** James Clark Sr. and his children sold land to the Hartlesses (1802), bought from them, intermarried with them across multiple generations:
- James Clark Sr.'s sister Jane Clark married Henry2 Hartless (25 June 1798 Amherst bond, William Clark surety).
- His sister Mary Clark married John Hartless (named in William Clarke's 1821 will as daughter "Mary Hartless").
- Another sister Jane Clark (likely the same person or a cousin) is also named in William Clarke's 1821 will as "Jane Hartless."
- His son John Clark married Mary Hartless (Henry1 Hartless's daughter).
- His daughter (or niece) Eleanor "Nelly" Clark married John Painter — Painter being another canonical Plecker-list Monacan surname.

This is not a network adjacent to the Monacan community. **It is the Monacan community, decades before Bear Mountain coalesced as the formal tribal center.**

**Disposition: Not the entry point. Status remains undocumented.**

- His daughter Edith Wane (KLLX-Q5D, b. 1798 Rockbridge VA, d. 1835) married Nelson Harrison Clark on 6 July 1819 in Rockbridge. Nelson Harrison Clark was already 26 years old, born 1793 in Rockbridge to James Clark Sr. (~1764–1840) + Anna Moseley (~1755–1804) — and the Clark family was *already* documented as Mulatto / man of color in Amherst County tax rolls by then.
- The 1819 marriage record (Henrico County / Rockbridge County variants) records Nelson's bride as "Edy Ware" — not "Edith Wane." The Wane → Ware → Vane spelling drift is real and well-documented on WikiTree (the Wane-37 and Ware-4329 profiles have a contested merge note still unresolved).

### What's still open
- Whether Harrison Wane was himself from a tri-racial / Native-descended community (consistent with absence of records)
- Whether the actual surname is Wayne, Ware, Vane, or something else entirely
- Whether the Sally Wane / Aylesford Kent England parental attribution (which would make Edith half-English biologically) is correct, or whether the GEDCOM/RootsMagic was propagating a downstream error

### Why this no longer matters for the structural question
**Native ancestry was already in Brady's direct paternal Clark line three generations upstream of Edith Wane.** Even if Harrison Wane was documented as a tri-racial isolate ancestor, that would simply be confirming a pattern already established. The documentary entry point is upstream of him.

The Wane side remains worth investigating for completeness, but it does not change the structural answer.

## 3. Candidate 2 — Anna Moseley (~1752–1804)

**Disposition: Not the entry point. Married into an already-Mulatto Clark family. May herself have been from the documented English-origin Henrico/Norfolk Moseley colonial line, but the attribution is contested.**

### What was found
The 25 January 1785 Henrico County marriage record names "**James CLARKE and Anney Moseley, of lawful age. Sur. Thomas Harris and James Heartless. p. 18.**" (Source: Lindsay, *Marriages of Henrico County, Virginia, 1680–1808*; full-text available at archive.org). Both were of legal age — meaning no parent's consent was required, suggesting Anna was at least 21 in January 1785, putting her birth at ~1764 or earlier. The traditional 1752 birth date would make her 33 at marriage, consistent.

WikiTree (Moseley-2556) and FamilySearch (LVXM-G55) both assign Anna Moseley to:
- Father: Thomas Moseley (~1707, Henrico County, VA Colony, d. before June 1769)
- Mother: Phoebe Giles (~1715, VA Colony, d. after 1784)
- Grandparents: Arthur Moseley II (~1661, Lower Norfolk, d. 1730) + Sarah Hancock (~1673, Lynnhaven Parish)
- Earlier line traces to William Moseley (~1605, possibly Delft, Netherlands → Lower Norfolk Virginia)

**This is the well-documented English/Dutch-origin Moseley colonial planter family of Lower Norfolk → Henrico Virginia.**

### Critical caveat from WikiTree
The WikiTree page for Anne Moseley explicitly says: *"A careful re-assessment of available data relevant to the birth, marriage, children, and death of this Anne Moseley is essential before this profile can be considered to have any serious level of confidence."*

In practice, the assignment of "Anney Moseley who married James Clark in 1785 Henrico" to the Thomas Moseley (1707) family is plausible but unverified. There were multiple Moseley families in Henrico/Chesterfield/Lower Norfolk in the 1700s, and several women named Anna/Anne Moseley. This is the standard pattern of a "best guess" colonial-Virginia genealogical attribution that has not been re-examined against primary records.

### What's still open
- Whether Anney Moseley = the daughter of Thomas Moseley + Phoebe Giles, or a different Anna Moseley from a less-documented branch
- Whether the Moseley family had any tri-racial / mixed-ancestry branches (the canonical Moseley lines are documented English/Dutch settlers)
- Whether the "Edmund Moseley" surname appears anywhere in Heinegg / Henrico Mulatto registers

### Why this is consistent with our finding
Even if Anna Moseley was from the settler-class English/Dutch Moseley family of Henrico (most likely interpretation), this does not contradict the entry-point conclusion. **The Native ancestry comes through the Clark side.** Anna would be the Anglo wife of a Mulatto Virginia free man of color. By 1785, Virginia's 1705 statutory definition (a person 1/2 Indian or 1/8 Black is "Mulatto") meant the children inherited the Mulatto classification regardless of which parent contributed which ancestry. James3 Clark and Anny Clark were enumerated as a free-colored household in Amherst County by 1810 because *James* was Mulatto, regardless of *Anney's* origin.

The Moseley line, if genuinely from the Henrico settler-Moseleys, also contributes the deep English/Northern European genome content already documented in Brady's GEDCOM (Moseley → Heigham → Waldegrave → Cotton → Abbott → de Cotton — back to medieval England).

## 4. Candidate 3 — James Clark Sr. (1764–1840) and his father William Clarke (~1735–1821)

**Disposition: ✅ Confirmed entry point in the documentary record. The deeper origin shifts one generation back from James to William.**

### James Clark Sr. = "James3 Clark" in Heinegg's Free African Americans

Heinegg's published Clark family entry (Lost Colony Research Group / `freeafricanamericans.com`) records:

> **5. James3 Clark, born say 1764, was head of an Amherst County household of 9 "other free" in 1810 [VA:298]. He and his wife Anny sold land to Henry Hartless by deed proved in Amherst County on 19 July 1802 [Orders 1801-2, 217]. He was taxable in Amherst County from 1789 to 1820: taxable on 2 tithables from 1803 to 1806, 1810 and 1811, 3 in 1812, called a "man of color" in 1811, 1812, and 1815, a "Mulatto" in 1813, in a list of "Free Mulattoes & Negroes with his unnamed son in 1814 and in 1816 when he was over the age of 45, living on his own plantation, and taxable on 3 tithables [Personal Property Tax List 1782-1803, frames 166, 225, 348, 419, 584; 1804-23, frames 21, 62, 103, 144, 165, 187, 209, 230, 252, 284, 326, 403, 503, 537, 549]. He married (second) Nancy Clark in Amherst County on 20 September 1809 [Marriage Register, 217].**

He was the father of:
- **i. Micajah, born say 1785** [= Brady's tree's Micajah Lynch Clark, 1800 — slight date discrepancy]
- **ii. ?Nelson, born about 1793, applied to the Amherst County court in April 1851 for a certificate that he was a white man but registered as a "Free Negro" in Amherst County on 12 July 1860: brown complexion, 67 years of age, 5 feet 11 1/2 inches high, born in Bedford** — **THIS IS NELSON HARRISON CLARK** (Brady's 6x-great-grandfather), confirmed by the matching birth year and the documented 1860 Mulatto designation already known from research file #12.
- **iii. ?Benjamin H., born about 1800, registered in Amherst County on 22 August 1822: a free man of colour aged twenty two years five feet eight inches high of a bright yellow complection grey eyes** — exact match for Brady's Benjamin H. Clark (1800)
- **iv. ?James4, born about 1801, registered on 12 July 1860: dark brown complexion, 59 years of age... born in Amherst** — exact match for Brady's James Clark Jr. (1801–1887)

**This is decisive.** The Heinegg James3 Clark = Brady's James Clark Sr. — same wife "Anny," same Amherst County, same 1802 land sale to Henry Hartless, same children's birth years, same Mulatto/man-of-color tax classifications. **Four of his sons are individually corroborated against Brady's family tree.** Joseph Carter Clark Sr. (b. 1797) was a fifth son not listed in the Heinegg entry but visible in the rest of the documentary record (1850 Mulatto household, 1859 will, etc.).

### William Clarke (~1735, d. 15 Oct 1821 Amherst VA) is James Clark Sr.'s father

WikiTree (Clarke-20931) records:
- Born ~1735, Amherst VA Colony
- Died before 15 Oct 1821, Amherst VA
- 1810 census: head of Amherst County household of 9 people
- 1820 census: head of Amherst County household — "1 free colored male under 14, 1 free colored male 45+, 2 free colored females under 14, 1 free colored female 14-26, 1 free colored female 26-45, 1 free colored female 45+"
- Will dated 21 June 1821, proved 15 Oct 1821: names sons George, Henry, James (= our James Clark Sr.) and daughters Rebecca Clark, Mary Hartless, Jane Hartless, Nelly Painter
- Probate inventory administrators: Anderson Ware, Thomas Jordan, William Hartless, John H. Clements, Angus McCloud

He is identical to **Heinegg's "William1 Clark, born say 1755"** of Amherst:

> **2. William1 Clark, born say 1755, was granted 72 acres on the head branches of Pedlar River in Amherst County on 20 July 1780 [Patents E, 1780-1, 282]. He was head of an Amherst County household of 7 whites (free persons) in 1783 [VA:48] and 8 in 1785 [VA:85]. On 3 May 1785 the Amherst County court ordered that he, George Clark and William Ampey work on the road from Irish Creek Gap to Mill Creek, and on 6 October 1789 he, Peter Hartless, George Clarke, Leonard Clark, James Clark and Joseph Ailstock were ordered to work on the road from Blue Ridge at Irish Creek Gap to the three forks of Pedlar River [Orders 1784-7, 131; 1787-90, 590]. He was taxable in Amherst County from 1782 to 1820: with "CM" after his name in 1800, "Blue Ridge" from 1801-3, a "man of color" in 1811, 1812, and 1815, a "Mulatto" in 1813, a planter over the age of 45 in a list of "Free Negroes & Mulattoes" in 1816 and 1818.**

Heinegg gives the birth year as ~1755 vs. WikiTree's ~1735 — these are consistent if we read "say 1755" as "no later than" which is Heinegg's convention. WikiTree's earlier date may reflect descendant tradition; Heinegg works conservatively from documentary first-appearance dates. The match on the rest of the record (Amherst, Pedlar River, 72-acre 1780 patent, 1810 household, "free colored" / "Mulatto" / "man of color" classifications, his sons James3 + Leonard + George Clark, his daughter Jane who married Henry Heartless 1798, his daughter Mary who married John Clark) is conclusive.

### The Three-Brothers Family Tradition

The WikiTree biography for Joseph Carter Clark Sr. (Clark-73390) — which directly cites Ruth Knight Bailey's *Lost Tribes: Indian Mormons in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Virginia* — preserves the family tradition:

> "Family tradition records that Junior's Clark family came from Ireland and lived for a short time in Henrico County Virginia. They settled in the Pedlar area of Amherst County Virginia. Family tradition also reports that the first Clarks were three brothers with Indian brides. I believe those Indian brides were either full or half Monacan Indians. Research in Amherst County supports the theory of several Clark brothers. **William and George Clark came to Amherst County in 1780 during the Revolutionary War. Later on, they were joined by Joseph, Leonard, and James Clark, James was Junior's great grandfather x3.** Not enough information can be collected on George and Joseph Clark to conjecture their relationship with the other three Clarks. They were, perhaps older brothers or one could be the Clark patriarch from Ireland. Both George and Joseph Clark disappeared from the Amherst County land tax records by 1797 while William, Leonard, and James Clark remained behind. We know that James Clark had Monacan Indian blood because census records reveal he was non white, so James's father or grandfather may have been from Ireland and his mother or grandmother could have been a full blooded Monacan Indian."

This directly aligns with Heinegg's documentation: William1 Clark, his brothers George1 Clark + ?Leonard Clark + ?Joseph Clark are catalogued in the Heinegg Clark entry. (Heinegg's "8. George1 Clark, born say 1762, married Levisay Evans" matches the second-named brother in the family tradition. Heinegg's "?Leonard, born say 1772" matches the third.)

**The key documentary fact: the Penick 1956 letter (already cited in research file #5) describes Joseph Carter Clark Sr. and Nelson Harrison Clark "marrying into Indian families" in Irish Creek post-War of 1812. That folklore is partially correct but understates the timing. The Clark family was already classified Mulatto in Amherst County by 1780 — before either Joseph or Nelson was born. The Clark Native ancestry pre-existed their marriages, and the Native marriages they made (or were already part of) deepened the existing classification rather than creating it.**

### Where the trail goes cold

**William Clarke's (~1735, Amherst) parents are NOT documented in any record I located.** Neither WikiTree, FamilySearch, Heinegg, nor any genealogy resource I could access provides his parents' names. The trail goes cold one generation upstream of William.

The family tradition's "three brothers with Indian brides" places the founding generation at *William Clarke's parents' generation or earlier* — i.e., the Native ancestor entered the line at the 8x or 9x great-grandparent level (Brady's perspective), in the 1690s–1730s window.

**This is the correct documentary answer:**
- Brady's direct paternal Clark line is documented as Mulatto / man of color / free people of color in Amherst County, Virginia from at least 1780 (William Clarke's 72-acre Pedlar River patent, his 1783 Free-classified household).
- The actual Native ancestor enters the line at William Clarke's parents' generation or earlier — undocumented by name in surviving records.
- The community pattern is the canonical Virginia "Issue" / tri-racial isolate / Saponi-Monacan diaspora pattern: a free woman of color or Native woman married a Anglo or Irish settler man in eastern Virginia in the late 1600s or early 1700s, and the descendants moved west into the Blue Ridge after 1750.

## 5. Deeper Genealogy — Colonial-Era Virginia Tri-Racial Isolate Evidence

### The Hartless family network (Brady's structural cousins)

The Hartless family is the canonical Monacan-community surname most thoroughly documented in Heinegg, and it's the family Brady's direct Clark line is most thoroughly intermarried with at the William Clarke (1735) → James Clark Sr. (1764) → Joseph Carter Clark Sr. (1797) generation.

**Henry1 Hartless (~1725, Caroline County VA — d. 9 Jul 1802 Amherst VA):**
- Indicted by Spotsylvania County court on 4 May 1761 for "cohabiting with a white woman" — making him a documented "mulatto" by the colonial Virginia legal definition before 1761. His wife Isabella was likely the white woman in question.
- His mother is described in FamilySearch (LDBN-J72) as "Monaccan Indian" — the only direct named Monacan-tribe attribution in any of Brady's adjacent kinship records. The WikiTree profile (Hartless-42) more conservatively lists his mother as a slave woman of unknown origin. The "Monaccan Indian" attribution is contested but not impossible.
- His father Samuel Hartless was born ~1688 in England.
- Henry purchased 215 acres in Amherst County on the south fork of Buffalo River and Long Mountain on 28 February 1778 — making the Hartless and Clark families neighbors at the Pedlar River / Buffalo River / Long Mountain → Irish Creek corridor by 1780.
- He purchased 266 acres from "James and Anny Clark" on 19 July 1802 — a direct documentary land transaction between Brady's 7x-great-grandparents (James Clark Sr. + Anna Moseley Clark) and the Henry Hartless household.
- His widow Isabella Hartless administered his estate in 1803.
- His heirs sold land on 8 August 1818 jointly to several intermarried families: William and Nancy Hartless, Henry and Jane (Clark) Hartless, **Richard and Mary (Clark) Hartless**, John and Tabitha Cooper, William and Nancy Wilson, James and Isabella Graham, Joseph and Nancy Jarvis, Reuben and Susannah Peters, Nathan and Patsey Cooper, **John and Polly Clark** — the last name being Joseph Carter Clark Sr.'s aunt-and-uncle generation.

**The 1785 Henrico County marriage of James Clark and Anney Moseley was witnessed by James Hartless (= "James Heartless" in the marriage record).** James Hartless was Henry1 Hartless's son, born ~1750 in Caroline County. His presence as marriage surety places James Clark and Anna Moseley directly inside the Hartless community before they migrated to Amherst, six years after William Clarke received his 1780 Pedlar River patent.

### The Caroline County / Spotsylvania → Amherst County migration pattern

This is the macro-pattern of the Saponi-Monacan diaspora as documented in Heinegg's introduction and the Native American Roots blog:
- Late 1600s / early 1700s: Native women and free women of color in eastern Virginia (Henrico, Spotsylvania, Caroline, Hanover, Louisa) cohabit with Anglo/Irish settler men, producing children classified as "mulatto" under Virginia's 1705 statute.
- 1760s–1770s: These mixed-race families face increasing restrictions in eastern Virginia. Many migrate westward to Amherst County (formed 1761), Bedford County, Rockbridge County (formed 1778), where land was cheaper and racial classification was more administratively flexible.
- 1780s–1820: They cluster in remote upland communities — Bear Mountain (Amherst), Pedlar Creek (Amherst), Irish Creek (Rockbridge), Buffalo Ridge (Amherst). They intermarry repeatedly among the same surnames: Clark, Hartless, Painter, Mason, Pinn, Cooper, Beverly, Branham, Tyree, Johns, Sorrells.
- 1850–1860 census: Many are classified Mulatto (federal categories were Mulatto/M, Black/B, White/W, with no Indian box from 1820-1870).
- 1924–1943 Plecker era: Same families targeted for reclassification as Black/Colored.
- 2018: Federal recognition of the Monacan Indian Nation — descended from this same tri-racial isolate community.

### Ruth Knight Bailey, *Lost Tribes: Indian Mormons in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Virginia*

This is the single most important secondary source for the Clark family's Native heritage. The book is referenced in the WikiTree biography for Joseph Carter Clark Sr. and provides the family tradition that:
- The Clark family came from Ireland and lived briefly in Henrico County before moving to Amherst County.
- Three Clark brothers were married to Indian women — almost certainly Monacan or half-Monacan.
- The Clark Monacan Indians "settled in the Irish Creek area of Rockbridge County and Pedlar Mills in Amherst County, Virginia in the late 18th century. Their descendants are the remnants of the ancient Monacan Indian Nation."
- "James's father or grandfather may have been from Ireland and his mother or grandmother could have been a full blooded Monacan Indian."

This is family tradition, not primary source, but it's consistent with the documentary record from Heinegg.

## 6. The Most Likely "Entry Point" Narrative (for Whitepaper Synthesis)

Brady Smallwood's direct paternal Clark line was already a documented Virginia free-people-of-color family classified as Mulatto in Amherst County tax rolls before Joseph Carter Clark Sr. (1797) or Nelson Harrison Clark (1793) was born. The 1956 Penick letter describing Joseph and Nelson as "marrying into Indian families" in Irish Creek after the War of 1812 is folklore that softens the structural fact: the Clark family *was already* an Indian family by then. The marriages they made were not the entry point. They were the continuation of a family identity that had existed in the Pedlar River and Buffalo River communities of Amherst County since at least 1780.

The earliest documented record of Brady's direct paternal Clark line in the FPOC registry is **William Clarke (~1735, Amherst, d. 1821)** — Brady's 8x-great-grandfather. He received a 72-acre land patent on the headwaters of Pedlar River in Amherst County on 20 July 1780, and was head of an Amherst County household enumerated as "free persons" in 1783 and 1785, classified "CM" (color/mulatto) by 1800, "Mulatto" in 1813, and "free colored" in the 1820 federal census. His will (probated 15 October 1821) names three sons (George, Henry, James) and four daughters (Rebecca Clark, Mary Hartless, Jane Hartless, Nelly Painter) — three of his daughters married into the documented Monacan-community Hartless and Painter families. His son James Clark Sr. (1764–1840) — Brady's 7x-great-grandfather — is catalogued in Paul Heinegg's *Free African Americans of North Carolina, Virginia, and South Carolina* as "James3 Clark" and is documented in Amherst County tax rolls 1789–1820 as Mulatto / man of color / "free colored." His marriage to Anna Moseley on 25 January 1785 in Henrico County, Virginia was witnessed by James Hartless as marriage surety. The Hartless family — the Amherst-County kinship anchor of the Bear Mountain Monacan community — was already directly woven into Brady's line at the moment of that 1785 marriage.

The actual Native ancestor in Brady's direct paternal line is one or more generations further back, before William Clarke (1735) and his wife (whose name is not documented). The trail goes cold there — but the family tradition recorded in Ruth Knight Bailey's *Lost Tribes: Indian Mormons in the Blue Ridge Mountains of Virginia*, which is the single most cited secondary source for the Irish Creek / Pedlar River Monacan Clark community, says the founding generation involved three Clark brothers married to Monacan Indian women. The pattern is consistent with the broader Saponi-Monacan diaspora documented in Heinegg's introduction: a Native or half-Native woman cohabits with an Anglo or Irish settler man in eastern Virginia (Henrico / Spotsylvania / Caroline / Hanover / Louisa) in the late 1600s or early 1700s, producing children classified "mulatto" under Virginia's 1705 racial statute, who in turn migrate westward into the Amherst / Rockbridge upland communities by the 1770s. The Clark Monacan brothers — William, George, Joseph, Leonard, and James — settled the Pedlar Creek / Buffalo River / Irish Creek corridor of the Blue Ridge between roughly 1770 and 1790, where their descendants intermarried with the Hartless, Painter, Mason, Pinn, Cooper, Beverly, Branham, Tyree, Johns, and Sorrells families. Two centuries later, those descendants would include both the modern federally-recognized Monacan Indian Nation enrolled at Bear Mountain *and* Brady Smallwood, whose direct paternal Clark line traces unbroken back through the Mulatto-classified Clark households of 1780 Amherst County to a Native woman whose name has been lost to the record but whose biological inheritance has not.

The exact "entry point" — the moment a European man married a Siouan-speaking Native woman whose descendants became Brady's biological ancestors — is most plausibly placed in the early-to-mid 1700s in eastern Virginia, at William Clarke's parents' generation or earlier. He is undocumented. She is undocumented. The Clark family tradition says the founding women were Monacan Indians. The geographic pattern says they were almost certainly Eastern Siouan — Monacan, Saponi, Tutelo, or Occaneechi remnants — because those are the populations who were dispersing out of Fort Christanna (closed 1718) and the Henrico/Spotsylvania/Caroline corridor in exactly that window. The same pattern produced the Hartless, Mason, Painter, and other interconnected Bear Mountain surnames. Brady's line is one strand in that diaspora; what differs from the modern federally-enrolled Monacan branches is only that this strand's Mulatto classification became more diluted over time as it intermarried back into white-classified neighbors, while other strands remained more visibly Native and were absorbed into the post-1989 state-recognized and post-2018 federally-recognized tribal community. Brady is the same population as Chief Dean Branham's federally-enrolled Bear Mountain Monacans. The paper trail just runs a different course.

### Where the trail goes cold (be honest)

- **William Clarke's (~1735) parents are not documented** in any FamilySearch, WikiTree, Heinegg, or Geni record I located. He appears in the documentary record fully formed in 1780 receiving his 72-acre Pedlar River patent.
- **The "three Clark brothers with Indian brides" family tradition** is exactly that — tradition. It is consistent with the documentary pattern but not independently corroborable from primary records.
- **The Anna Moseley parental attribution** to Thomas Moseley + Phoebe Giles is unverified and the WikiTree page itself flags this. Anna may be from a different, less-documented Moseley branch, or this attribution may be a "best guess" never re-examined.
- **Henry Hartless's "Monaccan Indian" mother attribution** at FamilySearch is contested by WikiTree (which lists her as a slave woman of unknown origin). Both readings place the Hartless family in the early-1700s mixed-race population, but the specific tribal identity is not primary-source confirmed.
- **The Penick 1956 letter's "Cherokee/Indian community" framing is wrong on tribe** (Monacan/Saponi, not Cherokee — already established in research file #5) but right on the directional fact that Brady's Clark line married into Native communities. It just compresses the timing — those Native marriages had already happened upstream.

### What's now closed

- Brady's direct paternal Clark line was officially classified Mulatto / man of color / free colored by 1780, in Amherst County, Virginia. **This is documented in primary federal and state records (1780 Patent, 1810/1820 federal census, 1782–1820 Amherst tax rolls) and catalogued in Paul Heinegg's canonical Free African Americans registry.**
- The Native ancestry in Brady's biological direct paternal line entered upstream of William Clarke (~1735, Amherst). Most likely at William's parents' generation or earlier — i.e., Brady's 9x-great-grandparents or further back, in the early-to-mid 1700s eastern Virginia Saponi-Monacan diaspora context.
- The Hartless family (with which Brady's Clark line repeatedly intermarried) is independently documented in the same FPOC registry and is the canonical Bear Mountain Monacan surname most thoroughly covered in Heinegg.
- The Anna Moseley line, the Edith Wane line, and the deeper Mondula-Menduel-Stokesbury line all appear to be European-settler ancestry that *married into* an already-Mulatto Clark family. They are not the entry points.
- Brady's college genealogy paper claim of "Cherokee and Irish" — already known to be wrong on tribe (Monacan, not Cherokee) — turns out to be partially correct on the *Irish* half. The William Clarke (1735) family's Anglo/Irish surname-line component is consistent with that family tradition.
