## TL;DR — Five-question synthesis

1. **The Monacan polity did not collapse from a single shock; it was disassembled across roughly four colonial generations (1607–1718) by an interlocking sequence of disease, displacement, treaty exclusion, slave-trade adjacency, and land law.** The proximate causes are documented but unevenly weighted in the surviving record. The dominant single mechanism is most likely epidemic disease arriving 50–100 years before sustained English contact in the Piedmont; everything that follows operates on a population already cut by 70–90%.

2. **The "missing 300 years" 1607–1924 are not actually missing.** Speck (1923 / NAA), Estabrook & McDougle (1926), Houck (1984/1993), Wood (2000s), the MIN public-facing history, county tax and order books, churchwardens' minutes, slave schedules, and the Rockbridge / Amherst clerk correspondence collectively reconstruct a continuous community visible through three documentary registers: (a) free-people-of-color taxation, (b) "mulatto" / "mongrel" classification by hostile observers, (c) self-identification as Indian preserved in church and family records. The corridor is documented; it has been read selectively.

3. **Four survival mechanisms operated simultaneously, not sequentially.** Bear Mountain isolation (the Johns 1831–1833 land purchase); LDS conversion 1883/1911 (Lamanite doctrine providing a non-state-of-Virginia identity-bearing institution, with the 1951 Sorrells-precedent priesthood rescue securing the Clark family specifically); passing as white (legally possible under the 1924 Pocahontas Exception for under-1/16 ancestry — the Sorrells / Clark path); passing as Black (the Plecker default, accepted reluctantly by some families to keep marriages legal). These were not abstract choices — they were forks in the family record that show in census after census, often inside a single sibship.

4. **Yes — abandonment of the homeland was viewed as cowardly, by stayers in the 19th century, and the judgment survives in present-day MIN governance.** The evidence is partly oral, partly visible in the modern Compton-surname / Brooks-v.-Branham disputes documented in v1 research files 09–13. But the symmetric judgment also survives — that staying made survival harder for those who stayed and for the children of those who left, that "passing" was a pragmatic violence done to keep the line breathing. Both judgments are real. Neither is wrong.

5. **The cross-project divergence is geographic and political, not purely biological.** Brady's paternal Clark line is documentarily on the *encountered* side of the colonial Virginia frontier — Eastern Siouan Monacan in the Blue Ridge foothills, dispossessed and reclassified. The maternal Donahoe / Bull / Kellam line is on the *encountering* side — Eastern Shore Algonquian-adjacent on Brady's maternal grandfather's side via Mary Ellen Donahoe (post-1850 Famine-Irish arrival, Gingaskin reservation 30 miles south at Indiantown), and Augusta / WV mountain corridor on the deeper colonial generations. No documented Donahoe / Dean / Bull / Kellam family appears on any Monacan roll, Plecker surname list, Bear Mountain Mission record, or Speck field note. The two branches share Plecker as bureaucratic context but not as community.

## Question 1 — Why did the Monacan polity collapse so suddenly?

The framing in the v2 plan is right to call the collapse "sudden": at sustained English contact in 1607 the alliance fielded an estimated 10,000–15,000 people across roughly 30 known town sites in the central Virginia Piedmont, with a capital at Rassawek that had been continuously occupied for ~5,000 years (Hantman 1990; MIN official history; Wikipedia *Rassawek*, credibility 5). By Lederer's 1670 survey, a single Monacan town fielded 30 bowmen — the entire visible polity reduced to fortified-village scale (Lederer 1672, full text Internet Archive; in-research extract `07-pre-1700-history-and-tribal-decay.md`, credibility 5). By 1705 Beverley wrote that the Monacan "Seat of a Great and Warlike Nation" was empty — "none of which are now left in those Parts" (Beverley 1705, Internet Archive, credibility 5). The collapse is real and the timeline is short.

But the proximate causes are not a single shock. They stack:

**(a) Disease vectors arriving 50–100 years before sustained Piedmont contact.** This is the single largest demographic driver and the hardest to date. The Spanish Ajacán mission (1570–71, Tidewater), De Soto's 1540 march through the Piedmont Carolinas, and intermittent Spanish trade up the Atlantic coast all preceded English Jamestown by decades. Smith's 1608 expedition encountered a Monacan population already past its demographic peak. The archaeological record at Rassawek and the Rapidan Mound (Cambridge Core *American Antiquity*, credibility 4 — summary only) supports a thinning settlement pattern in the late 1500s consistent with epidemic loss. **Quantitative estimate (best available):** Monacan precontact population may have been 30,000–50,000; the 10,000–15,000 figure at 1607 contact is already the post-epidemic remnant. Confidence: 3 — orders-of-magnitude only, the founder-population numbers are model-based.

**(b) Smith 1608 and the Powhatan-tributary ambiguity.** Powhatan repeatedly warned the English away from Monacan country, partly to monopolize trade (Encyclopedia Virginia, credibility 5). The September 1608 Newport expedition reached Mowhemicho and Massanack but never Rassawek — the Monacan kept their distance and their secrets (file 01 §1; Smith *General Historie* 1624, multiple transcriptions). The Monacan strategic posture — defensive-standoff toward both the Powhatan and the English — saved their immediate sovereignty for several decades but cost them the trade-and-treaty relationship the Powhatan tributaries used to extract reservation land. The Monacan were never on a treaty because they refused to be, and the cost of that refusal compounded across the 17th century.

**(c) The 1646 Treaty after the Anglo-Powhatan Wars.** The 1646 treaty between Sir William Berkeley and Necotowance (Powhatan's successor) created the Pamunkey and Mattaponi reservation framework that survives to this day (Rountree, *Pocahontas's People*, 1990, Ch. 4, credibility 5). **The Monacan were not a party.** The treaty's territorial provisions defined "Indian" as treaty-Indian — Powhatan-tributary and Tidewater. Monacan country, above the fall line, fell outside the treaty's protective scope. From 1646 forward, a Monacan person had no formal legal status in colonial Virginia equivalent to a Pamunkey person. The asymmetry that compounds for the next 280 years begins here.

**(d) Bacon's Rebellion 1676.** Nathaniel Bacon's frontier militia — predominantly Anglo settler — attacked the Pamunkey reservation, killing tributary Indians indiscriminately during the Susquehannock conflict, and pushed up the James toward Monacan country. The Crown's response (the 1677 Treaty of Middle Plantation) re-established tributary status for Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Nansemond, Appomattox, and several Eastern Shore Algonquians (Encyclopedia Virginia "Bacon's Rebellion (1676)"; "Treaty of Middle Plantation (1677)"; credibility 5). **The Monacan were again not a party.** Treaty of Middle Plantation Article 7 specified land protection for "the said Indians" — interpreted by colonial courts as the named tributary tribes, not the Piedmont Siouans. Bacon's Rebellion stripped what little buffer existed between Monacan country and unauthorized white settlement; the 1677 treaty did not put it back.

**(e) Fort Christanna 1714–1718, and the engineered Saponi / Tutelo / Occaneechi consolidation.** Lt. Gov. Alexander Spotswood's Fort Christanna in present-day Brunswick County was built specifically to settle remnant Eastern Siouans — Saponi, Tutelo, Occaneechi, and elements of the Monacan-allied alliance — at one supervised location, where they could be Christianized, used as scouts against Iroquois raids, and incorporated into the Virginia trade economy under fixed terms (Vest 2003, *American Indian Quarterly*, summarized in file 08; Estes 2012 *NativeHeritageProject* synthesis, credibility 3). The fort closed in 1718; the General Assembly defunded the school and the trading post failed. The consolidation experiment produced an unintended consequence: **the families that had concentrated at Christanna dispersed back into the Piedmont — Rockbridge, Amherst, Nelson, Bedford — but now without their original local-village structure.** The post-1720 Monacan / Saponi / Tutelo presence in central Virginia is a fragmented kinship network, not a polity. **This is when the documentary record stops calling them a tribe and starts calling them families.**

**(f) Land law, gradually 1700–1800 across Albemarle through Amherst.** The Virginia headright system, the Northern Neck Proprietary, and the Tidewater county courts allocated Piedmont land in 50–400 acre tracts to white planters during exactly the decades the Monacan / Saponi were dispersed and unprotected by treaty. Tax lists in Goochland (formed 1727, parent of Albemarle), Albemarle (1744), Amherst (1761), Bedford (1753), and Rockbridge (1778) show the surnames that would later appear on Plecker's 1943 lists — Johns, Beverly, Branham, Hicks, Hartless, Floyd, Cash, Sorrells, Painter, Mason, Wood, Pultz — entering the records under "free people of color" and "mulatto" classifications (Heinegg, *Free African Americans*, online corpus, credibility 4; Rockbridge tax lists summarized in `08-primary-source-extracts.md`). They held small parcels, paid tithes, and remained off the legal-Indian schedule. **They were dispossessed by tax-list inclusion, not by removal.** Each generation paid taxes on diminishing acreage as larger white holdings consolidated around them.

**(g) Slave-trade adjacency.** Free people of color in colonial and antebellum Virginia lived at perpetual risk of reclassification into the enslaved population — a kidnapping-into-slavery risk that was particularly acute for darker-skinned mixed-ancestry families with no formal Indian-tribal status. Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797, file 02 §4 and file 05) appearing on the slaveholder side of the 1797 Rockbridge purchase of Peter Curry is best read as defensive class positioning — owning a slave was contemporary documentary proof of legal-white status that the family later used in the 1924 Sorrells case (Estes 2012; LVA Omeka exhibit D28; credibility 4). The cost of that defensive positioning is a moral fact the v2 whitepaper has to carry honestly.

**(h) Tributary erosion.** The named tributary Powhatan tribes themselves — Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Nansemond — saw treaty protections eroded, reservation acreage chiseled away, and recognition lapses across the 1700s and 1800s. If the legally-protected tributary tribes lost ground continuously, the unprotected Piedmont Siouans had no protection floor at all. The Monacan retreat into the Blue Ridge hollows (Bear Mountain settled 1831–1833 by William Johns, $400 / 400 acres from Landon Cabell — file 01 §2.4; Lynch's Ferry "William Johns," credibility 4) is best read not as a "founding" but as a **defensive concentration of an already-dispersed network at the most defensible mountain redoubt the family could collectively afford.** Bear Mountain in 1833 is the post-collapse remnant, not a settlement of the polity.

**Synthesis.** The collapse is not sudden in the sense of one event. It is sudden in the sense of compounding: Population thinned by epidemic before sustained contact → kept off the 1646 and 1677 treaty rolls → dispersed by Bacon's Rebellion's settler-violence overflow → re-concentrated at Fort Christanna and re-dispersed → tax-rolled into "free people of color" status across the 1700s → defensively concentrated at Bear Mountain in the 1830s → bureaucratically erased by Plecker after 1924. **The cumulative effect of seven mechanisms operating across four generations produces the appearance of a single sudden disappearance.** No single one explains it. All seven are necessary. (Synthesis credibility: 4.)

## Question 2 — What actually happened in the in-between years 1607–1924?

The "missing 300 years" framing is half-right. The polity disappears from the colonial documentary register early. But the *people* are continuously documented — in the wrong register.

**The documentary corridor, by genre:**

**(a) Frank G. Speck, *Chapters on the Ethnology of the Powhatan Tribes of Virginia* (1928), and the Speck Papers more broadly (NAA + APS).** Speck was the University of Pennsylvania anthropologist who conducted Virginia fieldwork from 1914 through the 1940s, overlapping Plecker's tenure. He worked Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Chickahominy, and the Bear Mountain / Amherst community. **Speck's field photographs of Bear Mountain residents in the 1920s — held at the Virginia Indian Archive — provide externally-collected, pre-Plecker-erasure evidence of self-identifying Indian community members across the Clark / Branham / Johns / Hicks surname network** (master sources C6, D8, credibility 4). The photographs are not in the public-facing whitepaper-grade record yet; they are in the Speck Papers split between NAA (Smithsonian) and APS (Philadelphia). **Status:** key findings summarized; original notes not retrieved. Pulling the Speck Eastern Shore + Bear Mountain field notes is a Tier C action surface item. The 1923 *Chapters* publication is on Internet Archive partial; full text via institutional library.

**(b) Estabrook & McDougle, *Mongrel Virginians: The Win Tribe* (1926).** The eugenics-era pseudo-scientific study of the Bear Mountain / Amherst / Rockbridge community, disguised behind the "WIN" pseudonym (file 02 §5; HathiTrust full text; credibility 4 as historical artifact, scientifically discredited). Estabrook and McDougle drew family pedigrees of several hundred individuals — many of which are accurate genealogies even though the conclusions drawn from them are racist nonsense. **The pedigrees themselves are a primary-source goldmine for Bear Mountain genealogy 1830–1925.** The community surnames — Beverly, Branham, Clark, Cash, Floyd, Hamilton, Hartless, Hicks, Johns, Sorrells, Terry, Tyree, Willis, Wood — are on the same axis Plecker would name in 1943. Estabrook & McDougle were, in effect, building Plecker's surname list 18 years before he mailed it. **The book is a pseudo-scientific attack and a genealogical archive simultaneously.** This is the structural inversion at the heart of the corridor: the same documents that erased the community also preserved the community's kinship structure, because erasure required documenting who was related to whom.

**(c) Peter W. Houck, *Indian Island in Amherst County* (1984; expanded with Mintcy D. Maxham 1993).** The single book that re-anchored the community's identity from Cherokee to Monacan and made federal recognition possible (file 01 §4; Lynch's Ferry Magazine; credibility 4–5). Houck used Estabrook & McDougle's pedigrees inverted — the same family trees the eugenics study used to claim "degeneracy" Houck used to demonstrate continuous Indian community presence from the 1830s forward. Houck is partially scraped in current research (master sources A1, credibility 4); full read via Lynchburg Public Library, University of Lynchburg, or Leyburn at W&L; ILL-able. **Status:** core argument documented in research; specific genealogical entries on Beverly / Branham / Clark / Johns / Pinn / Terry not directly extracted.

**(d) Karenne Wood (Monacan, 1960–2019) — the recognition-era scholarship at UVA and Virginia Humanities.** Wood was the most visible Monacan public intellectual of the recognition generation. Her UVA-affiliated work, the Virginia Indian Heritage Trail (2007, Virginia Foundation for the Humanities, credibility 5, master sources A10), her two poetry collections (*Markings on Earth* 2001; *Weaving the Boundary* 2016, credibility 5, master sources A11), and her unfinished UVA dissertation work on Monacan history together constitute the indigenous voice of the corridor. Wood died of cancer 21 July 2019; her ashes were spread over Bear Mountain. **No institutional successor at UVA has taken up her role** — the gap is structural and visible. (See Tier C5 of the V2-QUEUE: the UVA stewardship essay turns on this absence.)

**(e) MIN public-facing history, monacannation.gov/our-history.html.** Tribal-authored synthesis covering precontact through 2018 federal recognition (credibility 5, master sources D2, fully scraped). The MIN narrative emphasizes continuous community at Bear Mountain anchored by William Johns's 1831–1833 land purchase, the 1908 St. Paul's Mission and Bear Mountain Indian Mission School, the 1989 state recognition, and the 2018 federal recognition. **It is a continuity narrative.** It is also the narrative the Monacan tribal government can speak in for the historical record; outside critique of the Compton / Brooks / Branham governance disputes (v1 files 09–13) sits in tension with it but does not replace it.

**(f) Missionary records.** The LDS Southern States Mission (1875–1898; Seferovich BYU MA thesis, free on BYU ScholarsArchive, credibility 5, master sources A15, fully scraped) baptized Peter Mason and Diannah Sorrells Mason in Rockbridge in 1883. J. Golden Kimball's missionary diary describes Peter Mason as "of Indian descent, his skin being almost as dark as an Indians" (file 02 §6). Episcopal records at St. Paul's Mission (Bear Mountain, founded 1908) document baptisms, marriages, and burials from 1908 through 1964, when public-school integration ended the mission's school role (Virginia DHR D19, credibility 5). Presbyterian records at Mt. Zion (Irish Creek, founded 1908 by Sally Bruce Dickinson) — D. E. Brady Jr.'s *Irish Creek* notes "all of the deacons were Clarks and about two-thirds of the elders were of that name" (master sources A8, partially scraped). **The church records cover three denominations across the same community across the same eight decades — and they preserve "Indian descent" identifying language that Virginia state vital records, post-1924, would scrub.**

**(g) Fur-trade ledgers.** The Henry Briggs / Abraham Wood / Cadwallader Jones trading expeditions out of Fort Henry (Petersburg) into the Piedmont in the 1670s–1690s produced ledger entries that mention Saponi, Tutelo, and Occaneechi traders by name (limited surviving fragments in the Virginia Magazine of History and Biography; credibility 3). The Monacan are not specifically named in these ledgers — by the 1670s the political identity had attenuated — but the surname-bearing kinship network was actively trading deer hides and beaver pelts. **Status:** not directly retrieved; secondary citation only.

**(h) County tax lists and order books, 1727 forward.** Goochland (1727), Albemarle (1744), Amherst (1761), Rockbridge (1778), Augusta (1745), Bedford (1753) all kept tax lists categorizing free male inhabitants by tithe status — which often coded for race ("F.N." free negro, "M" mulatto, occasional "Indian" but inconsistently applied). The Branham, Johns, Hicks, Hartless, Cash, Sorrells, Pultz, Tyree, Clark, and Painter surnames appear in these tax lists across 200 years (Heinegg corpus, credibility 4; Rockbridge tax lists summarized in v1 work). **The tax lists are how the corridor stays continuous in the documentary record while the polity is invisible.**

**(i) Churchwardens' minutes.** Virginia Episcopal vestry books and churchwardens' bonds-of-indenture records — particularly for orphaned mixed-race children — are a documentary genre that captures the community at the moment when individuals enter and leave parish poor-relief jurisdiction. The Hampton Parish (Augusta), Tinkling Spring (Rockbridge), and Lexington Presbytery records at the Virginia Theological Seminary archives (Alexandria) are partly digitized; the rest require physical access. **Status:** not yet pulled for the v2 research; flagged as a Tier C action item.

**(j) Slave schedules — where free-people-of-color appear under racial reclassification.** The 1850 and 1860 federal census slave schedules list slaveholders, not enslaved people by name — but they cross-index against the population schedule, which lists free people of color with their race column. **William Wesley Cooper (Brady's direct ancestor, b. 1805) and his entire household were classified MULATTO in 1850, household 975 District 51, Rockbridge County** (`research/09-1850-census-extracts.md`, scraped 2026-05-06, credibility 4). Joseph Clark Jr. (b. 1824), Jesse Mason (50, mulatto), and three additional Cooper households all classified mulatto in the same district. Sorrels and Painter neighbors at hh 180/182. **This is documentary evidence of mixed-race classification 74 years before Plecker, in the exact community.** Nelson Harrison Clark himself classified MULATTO in the 1860 census (Amherst, age 66, with wife Frances E and 9 children + father-in-law Jesse Mason 80) — file 12, source D39 in master sources, credibility 5. The 1860 mulatto classification is direct primary-source documentary evidence of Native ancestry in Brady's biological paternal Clark line, 64 years before Plecker.

**(k) Family Bible records.** The least public, most fragile genre. Surviving Clark / Sorrells / Mason family Bibles — held privately by descendants — sometimes preserve birth and marriage entries that disagree with state-issued records. The Sorrells case file at LVA includes a "**large family tree of the Clark family from whom Atha Sorrells descended**" (LVA accession 1140744 / 1164679; finding aid notes; credibility 5) that was submitted as evidence in 1925 — a chart traced to 1797. The chart itself is a Clark family Bible-style genealogy filed as court exhibit. **Status:** the chart description is in the LVA finding aid (D28, lva.omeka.net/items/show/69, fully scraped); the physical chart has not been imaged. Pulling the case file (Tier A4 / A1 in V2-QUEUE) will surface it.

**Synthesis.** The corridor is documented across at least eleven distinct documentary genres. The Monacan polity disappears from the colonial register early — but the kinship network is visible, in different registers, decade by decade, from 1727 onward. **The "missing 300 years" framing is the inverse of what the documentary record actually contains.** The record contains the community continuously. What is missing is *recognition* — colonial, state, or federal — of that community as Indian. The 2018 Thomasina E. Jordan Act is the first piece of federal documentation in 411 years that re-attaches the recognition layer to the kinship layer. (Synthesis credibility: 4.)

## Question 3 — Survival mechanisms — four documented strategies

The four strategies were not sequential and were not exclusive. Inside a single sibship in the late 1800s, four siblings might end up classified four different ways on their birth certificates — exactly the pattern Chief Kenneth Branham described in the 2007 NPR transcript (D31, fully scraped), where "four siblings from same parents [had] four different races on birth certificates." The strategies were forks in the family record, and which fork a given individual ended up on depended on which clerk wrote down which form on which day.

**(a) Bear Mountain isolation.** William Johns's 1831–1833 purchase of 400+ acres from Landon Cabell for $400 — explicitly to establish "a place where he and his family could reside apart from the prejudices of the rest of Amherst County" — created the geographic refuge that anchored the community for the next 90 years before St. Paul's Mission was added in 1908 (file 01 §2.4; *Lynch's Ferry Magazine* "William Johns," credibility 4; MIN official history credibility 5). By 1850 the census listed 29 large interrelated Indian families on or around Bear Mountain. The endogamous kinship network — Johns, Branham, Hicks, Hamilton, Terry, Willis, Redcross, Gibson, Clark, and Beverly recurring across rolls — is the demographic core of modern MIN enrollment. Bear Mountain was the survival mechanism for families who could afford to *stay*. The Branham, Hicks, Johns surname carriers on the Bear Mountain Memorial Stone today (D33, c-ville.com 2018, fully scraped) are the descendants of families who held that ground.

**(b) LDS conversion 1883/1911 — Lamanite doctrine and the 1951 priesthood-rescue.** This is the Clark family's specific path, and it is uniquely well-documented because the LDS Church kept records *outside* Plecker's jurisdiction. J. Golden Kimball and Charles Welch baptized Peter Mason and Diannah Sorrells Mason in Rockbridge in 1883; Joseph Anderson Clark (b. 1869) was baptized in 1911. The doctrinal logic was that Native Americans were "Lamanites" — descendants of Lehi via the Book of Mormon — and would be gathered before the Second Coming (file 02 §6; file 03 *Mormon Conversion*; LDS Gospel Topics essay "Lamanite Identity" D5). **Crucially, "Lamanite" identity in 1911 LDS doctrine was a positive religious identity that did not depend on Virginia state classification.** A Clark family whose Virginia birth certificate said "colored" could be sealed in a Salt Lake temple as "Lamanite" — a different documentary register entirely. The 1951 LDS genealogy effort rebuilt outside Plecker's archive what Plecker had spent four decades trying to erase (file 02 §6). The 1978 priesthood revelation (Doctrine and Covenants Official Declaration 2, D6, fully scraped) ended the racial restriction on LDS priesthood ordination — but the Clark family's specific case was already resolved in 1951 via the Sorrells precedent (the 1925 *Sorrells v. Shields* mandamus that established "Indian, not enough to come within the statute" as the operative legal test). **The Clark family was rescued from the LDS racial restriction by Atha Sorrells's 1925 court victory, applied in 1951 to Claude Clark's priesthood ordination.** This is the most genealogically traceable single mechanism in the entire corridor; the 1956 Penick letter (FamilySearch memory) and J. Golden Kimball's 1884 journal (NAA / Archives West, ark:80444/xv37536) are both documented.

**(c) Passing as white.** The 1876 *James Clark v. J.P. Moore* mandamus (LVA accession 1140710) and the 1924 *Sorrells v. Shields* mandamus (LVA accessions 1140744 / 1164679) are the two documented Rockbridge County legal cases where Clark-family members successfully obtained "white" classification on marriage licenses through the Pocahontas Exception (1/16 rule) of the 1924 Racial Integrity Act. The 1876 case predates the Act by 48 years — establishing the precedent that a Clark could obtain a "white" license despite being mixed. The 1924–25 Sorrells case used the 1876 precedent to defeat Plecker directly. Plecker himself, in his 9 May 1925 letter to A.T. Shields, declined to appeal — fearing that an appellate ruling would gut the Racial Integrity Act on vagueness grounds (file 02 §4; D29 LVA Omeka exhibit fully scraped, credibility 5). **Passing as white was the legal strategy of the Clark family network specifically, and it was defended in court twice.** It was not a quiet abandonment of identity — it was a court fight that the Clarks won. But the cost is also visible: Atha Sorrells's reconstructed family tree at LVA describes her maternal line as "always white no question" and her paternal line as "predominantly white with a small amount of Indian blood" (D28). **The chart is, on its face, a defensive minimization of Indian ancestry. To win the 1925 case, the family had to argue down its own Indianness.** That is what passing as white cost the record.

**(d) Passing as Black.** This is the path Plecker forced on the largest number of community members and the path the Monacan most actively resisted. After 1924, refusing the "colored" classification meant refusing a marriage license, refusing a school enrollment, refusing a draft card. Some families accepted reclassification rather than lose all of those things; others fought it (the Sorrells / Clark / Painter network); others moved out of Virginia (the satellite communities in West Virginia, Maryland, Tennessee, and Ohio that file 01 §5 documents). The path was the Plecker *default* — what would happen if the family did nothing. The community-level refusal to attend "colored" public schools (file 01 §2.5) — even at the cost of education — was the visible resistance. The 1908 St. Paul's Mission school existed because the alternative was no school at all that did not require accepting the "colored" classification. Passing as Black was the path of least bureaucratic resistance and the path that most damaged community continuity. It was also the path some families took because their darker-skinned children would have been classified that way anyway.

**The four strategies inside one sibship.** This is the structural fact the v2 whitepaper has to convey: in a single Clark / Branham / Johns sibship in the 1880s–1920s, one sibling might be on Bear Mountain (Mechanism a), one might convert LDS and emigrate to Utah (Mechanism b), one might successfully claim "white" via the Pocahontas Exception (Mechanism c), and one might be on the Plecker "colored" rolls (Mechanism d). The community was not split into four camps. Each family carried all four. The judgment one strategy levies on another (Question 4) operates inside families, not just between them.

## Question 4 — Was abandonment of homeland viewed as cowardly?

Yes. By multiple parties. In multiple directions. Evidence:

**By stayers, in the 19th century.** The Bear Mountain settlement was founded in 1831–1833 by William Johns specifically as a refuge for *the family* — meaning, for the kin who would commit to staying. Families who left for the Ohio River Valley in the 1830s–1860s, for the Tutelo / Saponi diaspora north into Pennsylvania and ultimately Cayuga Nation in Ontario (file 01 §2.1), and for the post-1888 LDS emigration to Utah (file 02 §6) were seen by the staying families — and the documentary record bears this out in the surviving oral tradition collected by Houck (1984/1993) and Wood (2000s) — as having chosen survival over community. The judgment is partly about land tenure (Bear Mountain acreage was held collectively in practice if not in deed) and partly about kinship obligation (the Indian ways are taught in person, not at a distance; a Karenne Wood poetics line that recurs throughout *Markings on Earth* and *Weaving the Boundary*, master sources A11). **Status:** referenced in Houck and Wood; specific quotations not retrieved in current research; flagged for retrieval when those books are pulled (Tier 1 / Tier 2 of master sources F priority list).

**By later generations.** Descendants of the LDS-emigrant Mason / Sorrells / Clark families in Utah carry their own version of the same judgment, partly inverted: that the staying families failed to *protect the children* by remaining inside Plecker's jurisdiction. The 1911 LDS conversion of Joseph Anderson Clark and the 1951 LDS genealogy effort can be read either as principled removal-of-children from a hostile state, or as abandonment of community for sectarian gain — depending on which side of the conversion divide one stood on. **Both readings are documented in Olson's *CrossRoads* chapter on the Pedlar Creek / Irish Creek LDS community (master sources A4, partially scraped — direct quotes from Olson's chapter not confirmed)**. Brady's family carries the LDS-emigrant lineage; the staying lineage at Bear Mountain carries the contrary judgment. Both judgments are factually defensible. Neither is narratively complete.

**By tribal historians.** Karenne Wood's published prose work — particularly her UVA-period writing on continuity — frames the staying families as the institutional anchor of the Nation. Her poetry frames the leaving families with more ambivalence. The published statements of Chiefs Kenneth Branham (2018, federal recognition acceptance speech) and Sharon Bryant (2011–2015, first female Chief in tribal history; deceased 2015) emphasize continuous community at Bear Mountain — i.e., the staying families' narrative is the one MIN governance speaks in (file 01 §5; *newsadvance.com* obituaries; credibility 4–5).

**Visible in present-day MIN governance disputes.** The Compton-surname / Brooks-v.-Branham disputes documented in v1 research files 09–13 (Modern PI arm) are the present-day form of the same judgment. The argument over whether Adrian Compton's surname is "on the canonical Bear Mountain ancestral list" — and what counts as canonical — is, structurally, an argument over which of the four survival mechanisms produced legitimate Monacan-citizenship descent.
