# The Palatine Current
## The German and Swiss Stream Nobody Named — From the Rhineland to the Valley of Virginia

**Brady Smallwood — Family Whitepaper, Companion Document**
**Compiled August 2026 | Source-rated 1–5 (5 = primary archive / peer-reviewed; 1 = anecdotal)**

---

## The third stream

This family has told itself two origin stories. The first is the one this project was built to investigate: the Monacan-descended Clark line of Irish Creek and the Blue Ridge. The second, documented on the maternal side, is the English Eastern Shore line — the Bulls of Pungoteague Creek, in Virginia since the 1650s.

There is a third stream, and it has never been named. It runs through Brady's great-great-grandmother **Ethel May Knicely (1904–1949)**, the wife of Charles Richard Smallwood, and behind her it opens into something entirely different from either of the other two: a broad current of **German and Swiss families** — Knisely, Zumbro, Weismann, Geldmacher, Baumgartner, Gabbert, Maurer — who left the Rhineland and the Swiss cantons in the early 1700s, landed in Pennsylvania, and came down the Great Wagon Road into the Valley of Virginia.

The DeYoung pedigree holds this entire stream and never remarks on it, because it never assembles the surnames into what they collectively are. Assembled, they are unmistakable. This paper names the stream, traces its families, and shows the single most charming fact in the whole workbook: **one of these families' names translated itself into English in front of our eyes.**

Sourcing note: the family-specific facts below rest on the DeYoung workbook (FamilySearch consensus, rated **2** unless cross-checked). The historical frame — why these people left, how they moved, where they settled — is standard, well-documented American colonial history, rated **4 as context**, with no family-specific claims smuggled inside it.

## 1. Why they left — the Rhineland context

The region these families came from — the Rhine valley and its Swiss headwaters — spent the century before their departure being systematically wrecked. The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) depopulated whole districts of the Palatinate. The wars of Louis XIV finished the job: French armies deliberately devastated the Palatinate in 1674 and again, methodically, in 1688–89. Onto ruined farms came punishing taxation, religious pressure that varied by the ruler's confession, and finally the catastrophic winter of 1708–09, which killed vines and fruit trees across the region.

The response was one of the first mass emigrations of the modern era. In 1709 alone, some 13,000 "Poor Palatines" descended the Rhine to Rotterdam and crossed to England, overwhelming the docks of London. The English redistributed them — some to Ireland, some to the Hudson Valley — but the durable channel became **Pennsylvania**, where William Penn's colony had been actively recruiting German-speaking settlers since the 1680s with promises of cheap land and, most importantly, freedom from confessional harassment. Through the port of Philadelphia, decade after decade in the 1720s–1750s, came Lutherans, German Reformed, Mennonites, Dunkers, and Moravians — the people who became the "Pennsylvania Dutch" (*Deutsch*). *(Context, 4.)*

Every dated European birth in this stream of the family fits that window and that map. This is not a coincidence of surnames; it is the migration itself, recorded one household at a time.

## 2. The families, one by one

### 2.1 The Zumbros — Switzerland, three countries in three generations

The cleanest arc in the whole workbook *(sheet data, 2)*:

| Generation | Person | Born | Died |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Anthon Zumbro** | 1694, **Switzerland** | 1749, Germany |
| 2 | **Johan Jacob Zumbro** | 1725, Germany | 1793, **Pennsylvania** |
| 3 | **Peter Henry Zumbro** | 1761, Colonial Pennsylvania | 1838, **Virginia** |
| 4 | **Sarah Catherine Zumbro** | 1805, Virginia | 1852, Virginia |

Read the birthplaces downward: Switzerland → Germany → Pennsylvania → Virginia. One family, four generations, executing the entire Palatine migration like a diagram. Anthon was born in a Swiss canton and died in Germany — a family already in motion before the Atlantic. His son Jacob made the crossing and died in Pennsylvania. His grandson Peter Henry, born in colonial Pennsylvania on the eve of the Revolution, moved down into Virginia and died there in 1838. His great-granddaughter Sarah Catherine was a Virginian, full stop.

Wife-side entries deepen it: Johan Jacob's wife **Anna Maria Margaret Lorentz** (1716, Pennsylvania–1809) was the American-born daughter of **Henery "Lorentz or Lawrence"** (1702, Germany–1751, Pennsylvania) — the sheet's own uncertainty, "Lorentz or Lawrence," is anglicization caught mid-step. Peter Henry's wife **Elizabeth Magadelena Scheib** (1765, Virginia–1852) carries the stream's classic Rhineland naming — and note her generation was already being born *in Virginia* by 1765, which dates this family's arrival in the Valley to the earliest settlement wave. *(Sheet data, 2.)*

### 2.2 The Geldmachers — the name that translated itself

The single best story in the workbook. Follow the surname column *(sheet data, 2)*:

| Person | Born | Died | Surname as recorded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Johann L. **Geldmacher** | 1687, Germany | — | Geldmacher |
| Johann Daniel **Geldmacher** | 1720, Germany | — | Geldmacher |
| Johann Ludwig **Geldmacher** | 1748, Germany | — | Geldmacher |
| Daniel David **Moneymaker** Sr. | 1796, **Virginia** | 1861, Virginia | **Moneymaker** |
| Sarah Jane **Moneymaker** | 1838, Virginia | 1916 | Moneymaker |

*Geld* is German for money. *Macher* is German for maker. Somewhere between Johann Ludwig — born in Germany in 1748 — and his son Daniel David, born in Virginia in 1796, the family stopped carrying its name in German and started carrying it in English, **word for word**. Not phonetically mangled like most immigrant names — *translated*, precisely, as though the family looked at the new country and decided the name should mean the same thing to their neighbors that it had always meant at home.

There are Moneymakers all over the Valley of Virginia and East Tennessee today, and this is where they come from — a documented American surname of exactly this origin. Brady's kids can be told, accurately: one branch of your family is named Moneymaker because your ancestors translated themselves into English. *(Sheet data, 2; the Geldmacher→Moneymaker anglicization pattern as a Valley phenomenon, context, 3–4.)*

Johann Ludwig's wife **Dorothea Elisapatha Baumgardner** (1758, Virginia–1808) links this family to the Baumgartners below; Daniel David's wife **Jane Reed** (1798–1861, Virginia) adds a British-Isles thread into the German current — by the 1790s, the Valley's communities were intermarrying across the old language line. *(Sheet data, 2.)*

### 2.3 The Kniselys — the name on Ethel May's birth certificate

The surname that ends the stream is also its best specimen of spelling drift. The workbook records it, across generations and branches, as **Knisely → Kneisley → Knicely** — three spellings of one German name (cognate with *Kneissl/Kniesel*) being worn smooth by English-speaking clerks *(sheet data, 2)*:

- **John Knisely** (1708, Germany – 1757, Pennsylvania) — the immigrant, and wife **Mary Christina Sechrist** (b. 1700, colonial Pennsylvania — her name itself an anglicization of *Siegrist*)
- **Anthony Knisely** (1728, Pennsylvania – 1801, Pennsylvania) and wife **Anna Barbara Derr**, daughter of **John George Derr** and **Maria Catharina Alberth**, both born 1700 in Germany — a branch that stayed in Pennsylvania
- **Anthony A. Kneisley** (1740, Pennsylvania – 1799, **Virginia**) and wife **Anna Barbara Maurer** — the branch that took the Wagon Road south (on Anna Barbara's recorded 1745–1855 lifespan, see §4)
- **David Knicely** (b. ~1782, Virginia) and **Mary Margaret Wragley** (b. ~1786, Virginia); **Ebenezer Knicely** (1813–1883, Virginia) and **Mary Ann Mcquire** (1822–1893); **Jacob Knicely** (b. 1826) and **Matilda Frazier**; **Adam W. Knicely** (1846–1923) and **Martha C. Lockey**; **George Robert Knicely** (1852–1944) and **Sarah Amanda Wiseman** (1860–1934); **Elijah Gold Knicely** (1878–1955) and **Cora Jane Knicely** (1877–1956, a Knicely marrying a Knicely — the stream folding back on itself)
- **Ethel May Knicely** (1904–1949) — who married Charles Richard Smallwood, and whose son Nelson Roudnal carried this entire current into the Smallwood line.

By the Virginia generations, the family is thoroughly of the Valley: marrying Fraziers and McGuires (Scots-Irish names — the Wagon Road's other great population), Lockeys and Moneymakers and Wisemans. The German community did not stay a German island; within three generations it had married into every stream flowing down the Valley. *(Sheet data, 2; Scots-Irish/German Valley intermarriage pattern, context, 4.)*

### 2.4 The Weismanns/Wisemans — from Germany to a Virginia farm in three steps

Another textbook arc, with the anglicization visible in the sheet's own erratic capitalization ("WeiSman") *(sheet data, 2)*:

- **Johann Peter Weismann** (1700, Germany – 1752, Germany) and **Anna Catharina Knapp** (1701–1753, Germany) — the generation that never left
- **John Peter Weisman** (1729, Germany – 1798, **Virginia**) and **Elizabeth Margaret Hemm** (b. ~1740, Pennsylvania — daughter of **Johann Georg Heim**, 1699–1743, Germany) — the immigrant generation: born on the Rhine, died in the Valley
- **John George Wiseman Sr.** (1765, Pennsylvania – 1838, Virginia) and **Margaretha Peggy Kaumann** (1766, colonial Virginia – 1845) — the pivot generation, German at home and English on paper
- **John Jacob Wiseman** (1798, Pennsylvania – ~1854, Virginia) and **Sarah Catherine Zumbro** (1805–1852) — Wiseman marries Zumbro: the streams braiding
- **William Wiseman** (1833, Virginia – **1862**, Virginia) and **Sarah Jane Moneymaker** (1838–1916) — Wiseman marries Moneymaker: braiding again
- **Sarah Amanda Wiseman** (1860–1934) — who married George Robert Knicely, completing the weave that produces Ethel May.

Pause on **William Wiseman, dead in 1862 at twenty-nine.** A Virginia farmer of prime military age, dead in the second year of the Civil War, leaving a two-year-old daughter (Sarah Amanda, b. 1860) and a widow who lived another fifty-four years and never appears in the sheet with a second husband. **The sheet does not say how he died,** and this paper will not guess — 1862 Virginia offered a young man death by battle, by camp disease (the greater killer), or by ordinary misfortune. His name belongs at the top of the same service-records search already planned for Henry J. Smallwood in the companion paper. If the record shows what the date makes plausible, this stream paid the war a son. *(Sheet data, 2; open question deliberately unresolved.)*

### 2.5 The Swiss Baumgartners, the Gabberts, the Maurers — the supporting current

Briefer arcs, same river *(all sheet data, 2)*:

- **Hans Johannes Baumgartner** (1695, **Switzerland** – 1751, **colonial Virginia**) and **Maria Elisabetha Leuenberger** (1713, Switzerland – 1750, Virginia) — a Swiss couple who made it all the way to Virginia themselves; *Leuenberger* is as Bernese as a surname gets. Their son **Christian Bumgardner** (1736, Virginia – 1795) was born in the Valley — which, along with the Scheib entry above, puts this family among the *first* wave of Valley settlement in the 1730s, when the Beverley and Borden land grants were actively recruiting exactly these Pennsylvania-German and fresh-off-the-ship families. The spelling drift (Baumgartner → Bumgardner) happened within one generation.
- **Johann Friedrick Gabbert Sr.** (1699, Germany – 1748, colonial Virginia) and **Susanna Catharina Boger** (1692, Germany – 1738, colonial Virginia) — another immigrant couple dying in Virginia by the 1730s–40s, first-wave again. Their daughter **Maria Magdalena Gabbert** (1730, Germany – 1789, Virginia) married Christian Bumgardner — Swiss and German branches joining in the Valley.
- **Johann Rudolf Maurer** (1681, Germany – 1740, Pennsylvania) → **Jurg Hans Maurer** (~1720, Germany – 1776, Virginia) → **Anna Barbara Maurer**, who married Anthony A. Kneisley. *Maurer* — "mason," "wall-builder." Another trade name, this one never translated.
- The **Zumbro-adjacent Cowmans**: **John Peter Cowman** (1732, Germany – 1809, Virginia), surname almost certainly anglicized from *Kaumann* — the same name his granddaughter-generation kinswoman Margaretha Peggy Kaumann still carried in German form. One family, both spellings, both preserved in the sheet.

## 3. The convergence — how the current reaches Brady

Every family above funnels, marriage by marriage, into a single person:

```
Zumbro ──┐
         ├─→ Wiseman ──┐
Moneymaker (Geldmacher)┘│
                        ├─→ Knicely ──→ ETHEL MAY KNICELY (1904–1949)
Maurer ──┐              │                      │
         ├─→ Kneisley ──┘              m. Charles Richard Smallwood
Baumgartner/Gabbert (collateral)               │
                                    Nelson Roudnal Smallwood (1925–1955)
                                               │
                                       m. Lutie Fannie Clark
                                               │
                                        Gene Smallwood → Brady
```

Ethel May Knicely is the delta where the whole Palatine current empties into the family's main channel. She carried, in one person, the accumulated lines of at least seven German and Swiss immigrant families and roughly two centuries of migration — and she died in 1949, the year before her grandson Gene was born, which is very likely why this entire stream vanished from the family's told story. Nobody was left at the table to say the old names.

So the family's full composition, as now mapped across all papers, is **four streams**: the Monacan-descended Clark/Sorrells/Cooper stream of the Blue Ridge; the English Bull/Dean stream of the Eastern Shore; this German-Swiss Palatine stream of the Valley; with English (Wane), Scottish ("Mondula"/Menduel), and Irish (Ledwidge, Stokesbury) threads woven through. Three of the four converge in Brady's paternal grandparents' single marriage — Nelson Roudnal Smallwood (English surname line + Palatine maternal current) to Lutie Fannie Clark (Monacan-descended line). That 1940s Rockbridge-area wedding was, genealogically, the meeting of most of Virginia's colonial history in one room. *(Synthesis of this project's papers, 3–4.)*

## 4. Data cautions — what this paper does not vouch for

Consistent with this project's discipline, the flags found while assembling the stream:

1. **Anna Barbara Maurer, recorded 1745–1855.** A 110-year lifespan is almost certainly a records conflation (two women, or a wrong death entry). Unverified; treat the death date as suspect.
2. **Maria Gertrudis Limpens, recorded b. 1775, wife of John Peter Cowman, b. 1732.** A 43-year age gap with children born before the wife's recorded birth is impossible as charted; one of these entries is misattached in FamilySearch. Flagged.
3. **Ellin "Mnu"** (b. 1695) — "Mnu" is FamilySearch's placeholder for "maiden name unknown," not a surname. Recorded here so nobody ever goes searching for the Mnu family of the Rhineland.
4. **All pre-immigration entries (German/Swiss parishes, 1680s–1720s)** are consensus-tree data of exactly the kind that European parish registers can verify — and exactly the kind that accumulates errors when they can't. The named-parish verification pass is future work; until then every European-born individual above carries its (2).
5. **No claim of religious affiliation is made for any family** — Lutheran, Reformed, Mennonite, and Brethren all rode the same ships and the same Road, and nothing in the sheet says which of these any family was. Church-book research (the Valley's congregational registers are unusually well preserved) would likely answer it and is listed in the research plan.

## 5. Research plan

1. **Ship lists.** Philadelphia arrivals are compiled in Strassburger & Hinke's *Pennsylvania German Pioneers* (three volumes, indexed, with passengers' own signatures). Knisely, Zumbro (Zumbrunnen?), Geldmacher, Weismann, Gabbert, Baumgartner are all searchable. Finding an ancestor's actual signature on an oath of allegiance, in his own German hand, is a realistic outcome.
2. **Valley church books.** Identify the congregations for the Knicely and Wiseman clusters (likely Rockingham/Augusta/Rockbridge-area Lutheran or Reformed registers) — this pins baptisms, marriages, and the German-to-English transition generation precisely.
3. **William Wiseman, d. 1862** — service-records search alongside Henry J. Smallwood's (companion paper, research item 4).
4. **The Geldmacher translation moment** — locate Daniel David Moneymaker Sr.'s earliest Virginia records to date the surname switch; check whether Johann Ludwig himself appears in Virginia under either spelling.
5. **Resolve the flagged conflations** (Maurer lifespan, Limpens attachment) on FamilySearch, correcting the public tree while we're there — this family fixes what it finds.
6. **European parish verification** for the two or three lines the ship lists confirm — done from home; the registers are digitized.
