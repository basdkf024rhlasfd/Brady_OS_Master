# Before Joseph Anderson Clark

### The Earlier Generations Who Actually Carried the Story
### Five figures, factually rendered

**Brady Smallwood — Family Whitepaper, Companion Document**
**Compiled May 2026 | Source-rated 1–5 (5 = primary archive; 1 = anecdotal)**

---

## A note on calibration

The main whitepaper centers on Joseph Anderson Clark (1869–1963) because he is the figure who routes our community's history into Brady's specific bloodline, and because he is the most-quoted family voice in the existing scholarship (via Ted Olson's *Crossroads* chapter). But Joseph is not "Subject 0" in any objective sense. He was one participant in a fight that started 70+ years before he was born and that involved more dramatic actions by other people — most of them in his extended family.

This document is the corrective. Five figures, in chronological order, with what is actually documented about each. The point is to show that **the Irish Creek / Pedlar Creek Monacan community is the protagonist** — not any single person — and that what we inherited was a multi-generational refusal carried by many hands.

A caution before we start: the documentary record on the earlier generations is patchy. I've tried to be honest about what is established vs. what is reasonable inference vs. what is speculation. Source ratings are at the end of each section.

## 1. Joe Clark Sr. (born 1797) — The Patriarch

### What is documented

- **Born 1797, Rockbridge County, Virginia.** Source: family-tree exhibit submitted as evidence in *Sorrells v. Shields*, 1924–25, Library of Virginia accession 1140744 / 1164679.
- **He owned at least one enslaved person — a man named Peter Curry, purchased at auction.** This fact is stated in the *Sorrells* case file family tree and is corroborated in the Native Heritage Project's 2012 article on Atha Sorrells.
- **He was legally classified white in pre-1865 Virginia records.** This is not stated explicitly in any source I could pull, but it is necessarily true: in antebellum Virginia, only legally white persons could own slaves with full legal protection. "Free people of color" technically could (and a small number did), but their legal standing as slaveholders was constantly contested. Joe Clark Sr.'s undisputed ownership of Peter Curry, with documentation surviving into the 20th century, places him on the legally-white side of the antebellum racial line.
- **He is the genealogical anchor of the Clark family tree** that was submitted as evidence in the 1924 Sorrells case. His descendants form the documented Irish Creek Clark line.

### What this means in context

Joe Clark Sr.'s position — Indian-descended, classified white in records, slave-owning — is a textbook example of what historians call the **"tri-racial isolate" pattern** in 19th-century Virginia. Communities of mixed white / Native / sometimes African ancestry routinely appeared in records as different races depending on the year, the enumerator, and the political need. Joe Clark Sr.'s legal whiteness in his own lifetime is what allowed his descendants — including James Clark in 1876 and Atha Sorrells in 1925 — to argue that the Clark line was *historically* white and that any later "colored" reclassification was a clerical novelty, not a return to settled fact.

### What he did NOT do

I want to be careful here. **There is no documentary evidence that Joe Clark Sr. did anything individually heroic.** He is the patriarch of the family tree. He owned an enslaved person, which by modern moral standards is the opposite of heroic. He lived in a system that classified him as white because of his economic standing and his property holdings, not because of any moral virtue.

What he accomplished — without intending to — is **establishing a documented chain of legal whiteness** that his descendants could later use as a defensive shield when the Plecker era tried to reclassify the entire community as colored. He did not do this on purpose. But the chain is what made the 1876 James Clark case and the 1924 Sorrells case legally winnable.

### What is still open

- His parents' identities. Almost certainly recoverable from 1810 / 1820 / 1830 federal census records for Rockbridge County.
- The exact date of his death. Not yet established.
- Whether he himself ever made any public statement about his own racial identity.
- Whether Peter Curry has surviving descendants (he should — and any descendants of Peter Curry are a moral connection our family ought to acknowledge).

**Source rating:** 4 for the basic facts (primary archive evidence in Sorrells case file). 2 for the inference about legal whiteness (logical but not directly documented).

## 2. James Clark (the 1876 mandamus winner) — The Legal Pioneer

### What is documented

- **He was a Rockbridge County resident.** Specific birth and death dates are not in the public record I could access.
- **In December 1876, he applied to County Clerk J. P. Moore for a marriage license to marry Cerinda Robison, "a white woman."** Moore denied the license on the grounds that Clark was "a black man." (Source: Library of Virginia finding aid for case file accession 1140710.)
- **Clark filed a petition for writ of mandamus** in Rockbridge County Circuit Court to compel Moore to issue the license. His argument: he was of mixed blood, not Black, and was less than 1/4 Indian or Negro under the relevant statute.
- **The court ruled in his favor and ordered the license issued.** The case is recorded in the County Court order Book, 1876, pages 137 and 174.
- **The case file includes correspondence with the Amherst County clerk** inquiring into Clark's racial background. This is the earliest documentary proof that Rockbridge and Amherst county officials understood the two-county Indian community as a single shared population.

### What this means in context

The 1876 *James Clark v. J. P. Moore* case is the **earliest known successful legal challenge by a member of our family to Virginia's racial classification regime.** It happened 48 years before Atha Sorrells's case used it as direct precedent. It happened during Reconstruction, when the South's racial laws were briefly in flux and the federal government's Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments offered a rhetorical opening for arguments like Clark's.

What James Clark accomplished is concrete and significant: he established, in Rockbridge County's own court records, that a Clark could be legally classified as something other than Black. He won by presenting himself as mixed-blood — the same argument his great-niece Atha Sorrells would make 48 years later, on the same family tree, in front of the same county clerk's office.

### What is still open

- His exact relationship to Joe Clark Sr. He is documented as the son of Polly Clark (née Clark), and the Sorrells case treated him as a descendant of Joe Clark Sr. The exact generational chain is recoverable from the case file but I do not have it directly.
- Whether his marriage to Cerinda Robison happened immediately after the ruling, and whether it produced children. (It almost certainly did — the case would have been moot otherwise, and the court would not have issued the mandamus.)
- His occupation, residence, and date of death.
- Whether he played any role in subsequent racial-classification disputes in Rockbridge before his death.

### A note on heroism — careful framing

James Clark's case is the first documented act of legal resistance in our family record. **It is reasonable to call this a courageous act** — he sued a county clerk in a Virginia court in 1876 to demand recognition of his mixed-race identity, in a state that would soon move sharply backward on race. He chose to fight in court instead of leaving the community, accepting a "colored" classification, or simply giving up. The case won.

What it is NOT is a deliberate political act on behalf of the community. There is no evidence James Clark intended his case to be precedent for anyone else. He wanted to marry his fiancée. He fought for that and won. The fact that 48 years later his great-niece used the case file to win her own fight is a downstream consequence — significant, but not the original intent.

This is the right way to frame heroism in our family history: **practical, specific acts of refusing to be wrongly classified, taken by ordinary people for personal reasons, that later turned out to be foundational for the community.**

**Source rating:** 5 for case existence and outcome (Library of Virginia primary archive). 4 for the family tree placement (corroborated in the Sorrells case file). 2 for biographical details beyond the case.

## 3. Peter Mason of Pedlar Creek (b. ~1814) — The First LDS Convert in the Community

### What is documented

- **Born approximately 1814.** Documented as "age 70 in 1884" in J. Golden Kimball's missionary journal — implying birth year approximately 1814.
- **Lived on Pedlar Creek, Amherst County, Virginia** — across the Blue Ridge from Irish Creek but part of the same continuous Monacan-descended community.
- **Wife: Diannah Sorrells Mason.** Same Sorrells family network that, two generations later, produced Atha Sorrells. The Mason-Sorrells-Clark intermarriage web defines our entire community.
- **Household of approximately 42 multigenerational family members** in 1883–84. Source: J. Golden Kimball missionary journal.
- **Baptized into The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in 1883** by missionaries J. Golden Kimball and Charles Welch. He is the **first documented LDS convert in our community**, 28 years before Joseph Anderson Clark.
- **J. Golden Kimball described him in writing as "of Indian descent, his skin being almost as dark as an Indians. His hair was long and black."** Source: Kimball field journal, cited in Ted Olson, *Crossroads*, 2004.
- **He is the figure who opened the door for the LDS missionary circuit** in our community. After his baptism, the Mason and Sorrells families became the Mormon staging ground for missionary work that would eventually reach Joseph Anderson Clark in 1911 on Irish Creek.

### What this means in context

Peter Mason is the literal Subject 0 of the Mormon thread of our family story. **Without his 1883 baptism, the LDS missionaries would not have had a Pedlar Creek / Irish Creek circuit to ride. Joseph Anderson Clark would never have been baptized in 1911.**

What is striking about Peter Mason's conversion is that he was already in his late sixties when it happened. He was an old man by the standards of 1883 rural Appalachia. He chose, in the last decade or so of his life, to accept a dramatically heterodox new religion — preached by missionaries who were widely hated in the post-Reconstruction South, who were actively being threatened with violence (the Cane Creek Massacre would happen the next year, 1884) — and then to host them in his household.

His decision propagated forward across at least four generations. The 1951 Clark priesthood ordinations included Alvin Coleman and Garvis Wheeler — both direct descendants of Peter and Diannah Mason. Branch President Eugene Larsen referred to them as "Lamonite [sic] brothers" of Claude Clark.

### What is still open

- Peter Mason's parents and earlier ancestry. Almost certainly Monacan and/or Saponi.
- His exact date of death. Likely sometime in the late 1880s or 1890s.
- Whether he himself articulated a theological reason for converting, or whether the conversion was more about sheltering missionaries who would have been killed otherwise.
- His relationship to other Pedlar Creek families — Coleman, Wheeler, etc.

### Heroism — what's defensible to say

**Peter Mason did something genuinely consequential.** He is not a famous person, but his decision to accept LDS baptism in 1883 — at age ~70, in a community that would become widely known as a Mormon enclave a generation later — is the foundational act of the Mormon thread of our family history. Calling him "the first" is factually accurate and not inflated.

What we cannot say is that he was politically motivated, or that he understood the long-term implications of what he was doing. He probably didn't. He responded to two specific missionaries who knocked on his cabin door, told him he was a Lamanite of the House of Israel, and offered him membership in a church that did not classify him as Black. He said yes. That is what is documented.

**Source rating:** 4 for the basic facts (Kimball journal, secondary citations in Olson). 2 for the broader contextual claims about his community influence.

## 4. Atha Sorrells (1904–1979) — The Courtroom Hero

### What is documented

- **Born 1904, Rockbridge County, Virginia.** Source: Sorrells v. Shields case file.
- **Died 1979.** She lived 75 years and lived to see the 1967 *Loving v. Virginia* decision, the 1978 LDS priesthood revelation, and the early Virginia legislative reckoning with the Plecker era — though not the 2018 federal recognition of the Monacan Nation.
- **Father: William Sorrells.** Filed as her "next friend" because she was a minor (under 21) at the time of the case.
- **Maternal great-grandparents: Joe Clark Jr. and Julia (Sorrells) Clark.** This is the genealogical link that makes Atha a Clark cousin and *Sorrells v. Shields* a Clark family case.
- **Genealogical chain through the case file:** Atha → Joe Clark Jr. + Julia (Sorrells) Clark → James Clark (the 1876 mandamus winner) and Polly Clark → Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797).
- **In November 1924, age 20, pregnant, she applied for a marriage license to marry Robert Painter** at the office of County Clerk A. T. Shields. Shields refused, citing the Racial Integrity Act of 1924. Source: case file and Native Heritage Project article.
- **On November 14, 1924, she filed a petition for writ of mandamus** in Rockbridge County Circuit Court to compel Shields to issue the license.
- **Witnesses subpoenaed and depositions taken November 1–15, 1924.** Hearing before Judge Henry W. Holt January 9–10, 1925.
- **Walter Plecker himself testified for the defense** — that is, against Atha. Source: case file.
- **Judge Holt ruled for Sorrells in January 1925.** The marriage license was ordered issued. Holt's opinion held that the evidence covering 130 years showed Sorrells had no non-white blood "except Indian, and there is not enough of that to come within the statute."
- **Plecker, in a letter to Shields dated May 9, 1925, advised NOT to appeal** because if the Virginia Supreme Court upheld the ruling, it would establish precedent that could "embolden the Rockbridge tribe."
- **Atha Sorrells and Robert Painter married on May 31, 1925.** Their first child — a daughter — was born in July 1925.
- **She lived as Atha Sorrells Painter for the next 54 years, dying in 1979.**

### What this means in context

Atha Sorrells is, on the documented evidence, **the single most consequential individual actor in our family's resistance to the Plecker era.** She did three things no one else in the family record is documented to have done:

1. **She personally sued a county official under the Racial Integrity Act** within months of its passage, while pregnant, as a 20-year-old minor.
2. **She won.** Her victory is the only known successful courtroom defeat of the Racial Integrity Act in Rockbridge County during Plecker's tenure.
3. **The Clark family tree she submitted as evidence became a documentary asset** that her cousin Joseph Anderson Clark used 26 years later, in 1951, to overturn the LDS priesthood ban for the Clark men. The exact same evidence package, in two different fights, separated by 26 years.

Her decision to fight matters more in context than out of it. **Plecker had spent 12 years building the bureaucratic apparatus of paper genocide before Atha sued.** He had powerful allies (John Powell, Earnest Cox, Madison Grant, the Anglo-Saxon Clubs of America). The Racial Integrity Act had passed unanimously in the Virginia legislature. Newspapers were almost uniformly supportive. Atha was 20 years old, pregnant, from a rural mountain community that the rest of Virginia treated as expendable. She sued anyway. She won.

### Why Atha matters more than I gave her credit for in the whitepaper

Atha Sorrells is the figure I underweighted in the whitepaper. The Sorrells case is named for her — but the whitepaper framed it as a Clark-family case, which is technically true (her maternal line is Clark) but *practically misleading*. The case was brought by Atha, in her own name, with her father William as next friend, on her own evidence and with her own attorneys (Fred T. Deaver and C. S. Glasgow). The Clarks were the genealogical evidence. They were not the plaintiffs.

**Atha Sorrells is the courtroom hero. Joseph Anderson Clark, in 1951, used her 26-year-old precedent to win his own fight inside the LDS Church. That sequence — Atha first, Joseph second — is the correct order.**

### What is still open

- The names and ages of Atha's children, and whether they self-identified as Indian, white, colored, or some combination across their lives.
- Whether Atha herself ever spoke publicly about her case after 1925. The case got Richmond Times-Dispatch coverage at the time; whether Atha was ever interviewed in old age (she lived until 1979, well into the Indian Heritage / federal recognition era) is unknown to me.
- Where she is buried.
- Whether there are surviving direct descendants who would be cousins to us today.

### Heroism — what's defensible to say

**Atha Sorrells is the cleanest case for heroism in our family record.** She did a thing that took moral courage, that had no guaranteed outcome, that exposed her to public attention and likely retaliation, and that she did when she had every reason not to (young, pregnant, female, rural, mixed-race in 1924 Virginia). She won. The win mattered for her, for her descendants, and indirectly for our line.

I want to be clear that this is *not* an inflation of her significance. The contemporary newspaper coverage treated her case as significant. The Library of Virginia treats it as significant. The Encyclopedia Virginia treats it as significant. Calling her the courtroom hero of our family record is the consensus view of the available scholarship, not my rhetoric.

**Source rating:** 5 for the case facts (primary archive at Library of Virginia, Encyclopedia Virginia primary documents, Native Heritage Project synthesis). 3 for biographical detail beyond the case.

## 5. The 1951 Operatives — Eugene Larsen, William S. Tanner, and Two Unnamed Utah Missionaries

This is the most important episode in the LDS thread of our family history, and I underweighted it in the whitepaper by attributing it almost entirely to Joseph Anderson Clark. The actual operative figures were four people — possibly five — who together did the genealogical and ecclesiastical work. Joseph Clark initiated the fight. Other people executed it.

### Eugene Larsen — Buena Vista Branch President

**Documented:**
- Branch President of the Buena Vista LDS Branch in 1950–51.
- Received the priesthood-ordination request from 12-year-old Claude Clark.
- Discovered the "colored" classification on the family courthouse records and was bound by then-current LDS policy not to ordain.
- **Wrote to LDS Church headquarters in Salt Lake City requesting a genealogical review.** This single act is what triggered the entire 1951 rescue.
- After the genealogical work was completed, **personally ordained Claude Clark, Alvin Coleman, and Garvis Wheeler in 1951.**
- Olson notes that Larsen referred to the Mason descendants as "Lamonite [sic] brothers" — using the Lamanite-doctrine framing that would later allow the Church to accept the Native-ancestry argument.

**What this means:** Eugene Larsen was not Indigenous. He was not from Virginia. He was a branch president — by LDS structure, a lay volunteer running a small congregation. His decision to escalate Claude Clark's case to Salt Lake instead of simply quietly denying ordination is the practical hinge of the whole story. **A different branch president might have just said no and the Clark men would have remained unordained.** Larsen chose to fight for them inside the institution. That is a real ecclesiastical choice with personal risk.

### Elder William S. Tanner — Church Genealogical Society

**Documented:**
- Wrote a letter to Eugene Larsen on **May 1, 1951**.
- The letter contained the bad news first: courthouse marriage records confirmed Joseph Anderson Clark and Mary Susan Clark had been married "as colored" in Amherst County in 1906.
- Tanner appears to have been a staff genealogist at the LDS Church Genealogical Society (later FamilySearch / Family History Department) in Salt Lake City. His exact role and tenure I have not been able to verify in this research pass.

**What this means:** Tanner is the institutional figure who could have ended the inquiry by reporting the courthouse "colored" finding back to Larsen and stopping there. He didn't. He continued the work and, presumably, dispatched or coordinated the two genealogical missionaries who traveled to Virginia. Without his decision to keep digging after the bad news, the case would have died at the Bureau of Vital Statistics record.

### Two Unnamed Utah Genealogical Missionaries

**Documented:**
- Per the Olson / Shaybo synthesis of the Buena Vista LDS branch history: two LDS genealogical missionaries traveled from Utah to Rockbridge County in 1951 and worked with Joseph Anderson Clark to assemble counter-evidence to the "colored" classification.
- They appear to have used: (1) the 1925 Atha Sorrells case as legal precedent; (2) cross-family evidence showing Clark cousin lines classified as "Indian" or "white"; (3) antebellum free-people-of-color records showing Indian, not African, lineage; (4) microfilm copies of Rockbridge County court records they brought with them or assembled on-site.

**Their names are not in the public record I could access.** They almost certainly are in the LDS Church History Library files in Salt Lake City. **This is the single most important open question in the 1951 part of our family history.** If we can name them, we should. They are the people who did the actual document work that broke the trap.

### A possible fifth: Brigham D. Madsen

**Speculative:**
- Brigham D. Madsen (1914–2010) was a University of Utah historian who specialized in Western American history and Native American genealogy.
- His memoir *Against the Grain: Memoirs of a Western Historian* (Signature Books, 1998), page 85, is cited in our family genealogy paper as relevant to the 1951 Clark effort.
- The exact connection — whether Madsen was one of the two unnamed missionaries, or whether he supplied research support from Utah without traveling — is **not established in the sources I could access in this research pass**.
- This is the most important book to obtain. Page 85 of Madsen's memoir may name the missionaries directly.

### Joseph Anderson Clark's actual role in 1951

**He initiated and pushed.** The standard account, from the family genealogy paper Brady wrote in college, says Joseph "worked along with a few LDS genealogical missionaries to prove to the church that he and his family were not 'negro' but in fact Native American." The verb "worked along with" is correct. He was 82 years old, lived in Lexington (having moved from Irish Creek after his wife's death in 1954 — wait, the timeline doesn't work, the genealogy says Mary Susan died 1954, so Joseph would still have been on Irish Creek in 1951; this is a small inconsistency to resolve), and his role was to provide the family memory, the local geography, and the moral demand that the case be taken seriously.

**He did not write the genealogical brief.** That was the two unnamed missionaries.
**He did not file the ecclesiastical inquiry.** That was Eugene Larsen.
**He did not staff the Church Genealogical Society.** That was William S. Tanner.

What Joseph did was insist that this fight was worth having and stand behind it as the family patriarch. That is a real and significant role. But it is the role of an elder who refuses to let a wrong stand, not the role of the principal investigator.

### What is still open

- **The names of the two Utah genealogical missionaries.** Recoverable from the LDS Church History Library, Buena Vista Branch records.
- **The exact contents of the genealogical brief they assembled.** Likely held in the Buena Vista Branch records or the Family History Department archives.
- **Brigham Madsen's actual involvement.** Resolvable by reading page 85 of *Against the Grain*.
- **Eugene Larsen's full biography** — when he served, where he was from, what happened to him after 1951.
- **William S. Tanner's role at the Church Genealogical Society** — dates of service, other cases he worked.
- **Whether the 1951 brief or any related correspondence cited the Sorrells case explicitly** or just used the legal-genealogical method without citing the precedent.

### Heroism — what's defensible to say

The 1951 episode is not a single-person story. **It is a small institutional rescue carried out by four (possibly five) people working across two states over roughly a year.** Calling Joseph Clark "the architect" overstates his role. Calling him "the patriarch who insisted on the fight" is accurate.

Eugene Larsen and William S. Tanner are the institutional figures who chose to take the inquiry seriously. The two unnamed Utah missionaries are the people who did the field genealogy work. Joseph Clark provided the family memory and the moral demand. Claude Clark, the 12-year-old, was the proximate cause but not an agent in the institutional sense.

**The most accurate way to teach this episode to your kids is as an example of how institutional injustices get unwound: not by a single hero, but by a small chain of people each willing to do their specific job — refuse to ordain on bad evidence, refuse to stop digging when the first record was bad, refuse to file an easy report and instead do the hard genealogy, refuse to let an old man's family memory die without a fight.**

That is a more complicated lesson than "great-great-great-grandfather was a hero." It is also more useful, because it is the kind of story that maps onto real institutional change — which never has one author.

**Source rating:** 4 for Eugene Larsen and the Tanner letter date (Olson / Shaybo synthesis). 2 for the unnamed missionaries' involvement (deduced from the synthesis but not directly named). 1 for the Madsen connection (cited in family genealogy paper without corroboration).

## What this corrected portrait shows

Five people, across roughly 150 years (1797–1951), in the same extended family network:

| Year | Figure | Documented act |
|---|---|---|
| 1797 | Joe Clark Sr. born | Patriarch; legally white; slave-owning; foundational genealogical anchor |
| 1876 | James Clark | Won mandamus case in Rockbridge for white marriage license |
| 1883 | Peter Mason | First LDS convert in our community (Pedlar Creek) |
| 1911 | Joseph Anderson Clark | First LDS convert in Brady's direct line (Irish Creek) |
| 1924–25 | Atha Sorrells | Won mandamus case in Rockbridge against Plecker himself |
| 1951 | Eugene Larsen, William S. Tanner, two unnamed Utah missionaries (with Joseph Clark) | Used Sorrells precedent and family genealogy to overturn LDS priesthood exclusion for Clark men |

**No single one of these people built the whole arc.** Joe Clark Sr. didn't intend his legal whiteness to be a generational asset. James Clark was fighting for his own marriage. Peter Mason was responding to two specific missionaries who knocked on his door. Atha Sorrells was 20 years old and pregnant and wanted to marry Robert Painter. The 1951 operatives were doing institutional jobs.

What unites them is that **at every key inflection point, someone in or near our family network refused to accept the wrong classification quietly.** The James Clark / Atha Sorrells / Joseph Clark thread is one specific repeated pattern — go to the documents, build the genealogical brief, force the institution to issue the right ruling. The Peter Mason / Joseph Clark thread is another — accept the religious offer that elevates Native identity, even at significant social cost.

Across 150 years and at least three institutional fights (1876 court, 1924–25 court, 1951 LDS Church), the consistent move is **using the documentary record itself as a weapon against bureaucratic erasure.** That is a practical, repeatable method, not a single heroic act. It is also exactly the kind of method that can still be applied today — by us, going to the Library of Virginia, pulling the original case files, and continuing the work.

## Recommended additions to the main whitepaper

Based on this deeper dig, the main whitepaper should be revised in three places:

1. **Headline #2 (Plecker's hit list):** keep as-is, this is solid.
2. **Headline #3 (Sorrells v. Shields):** rewrite to credit Atha Sorrells as the principal actor, with the Clark family tree as the evidence, not the other way around. Joseph Anderson Clark was a 55-year-old contemporary, not a participant in the case.
3. **Headline #5 (1951 priesthood case):** rewrite to credit the four-person team — Eugene Larsen, William S. Tanner, two unnamed Utah missionaries, with Joseph Clark as the patriarch who insisted on the fight. Not a solo Joseph Clark act.
4. **The closing note for the kids:** "your great-great-great-grandfather refused to be erased" should become something closer to "**a community of cousins, across five generations, refused to be erased — and your great-great-great-grandfather was the figure who routed that refusal into your specific bloodline.**"

This is more accurate. It is also more interesting, because it teaches that institutional change is built by chains of people doing specific jobs, not by single heroes.
