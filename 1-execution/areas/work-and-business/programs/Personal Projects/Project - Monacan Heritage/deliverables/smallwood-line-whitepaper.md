# The Name Itself
## The Smallwood Line — The Least-Known Branch in the Family Tree

**Brady Smallwood — Family Whitepaper, Companion Document**
**Compiled August 2026 | Source-rated 1–5 (5 = primary archive / peer-reviewed; 1 = anecdotal)**

---

## Why this paper exists

Every whitepaper in this project so far has chased the Clark line — the Monacan question, the Plecker era, the courtroom fights, the Mormon conversion. That was the right place to start; it's where the mystery was.

But there is a strange fact sitting in plain sight: **the surname on the cover of every one of these papers is the line we know least about.** Brady is a Smallwood. His kids are Smallwoods. And until this paper, the Smallwood line itself — the men who carried the name from the 1790s to the present — had never been written up at all.

This paper corrects that. It is built primarily on the pedigree compiled by Marcene DeYoung (December 2022), preserved in this project at `research/source/deyoung-research/`, cross-checked against the line-by-line analysis of that file completed in May 2026. It is honest about what that source is: a careful FamilySearch-anchored consensus tree, not yet independently verified against primary records. Where the paper states a fact from that sheet alone, it is rated **(2–3)**. Where a fact has been cross-checked in this project's research files, it is rated higher and says so.

One more reason this paper matters. The Clark research surfaced a documentary war over what this family *was* — Indian, colored, white, depending on the year and the clerk. The Smallwood line has none of that turbulence in the record so far. It appears, on present evidence, to be the quiet English-named stream of the family. Knowing which streams carried the storm and which did not is part of understanding the whole river.

## The line, stated plainly

Seven generations of the name, as documented in the DeYoung pedigree (FamilySearch consensus; rated 2–3 pending independent verification):

| Gen | Name | Born | Died | Spouse (as documented) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **James Smallwood** | 1792, Virginia | ~1865, Virginia | Elizabeth Gear (b. ~1800, Virginia) |
| 2 | **Henry J. Smallwood** | 1831, Virginia | 1904, Virginia | Ann Elizabeth "Hell" (1838–1920) — see naming note below |
| 3 | **Hamilton Osborn Smallwood Sr.** | 1864, Virginia | 1938, Virginia | Ida Florence Johnson (1869–1948) |
| 4 | **Charles Richard Smallwood** | 1902, Virginia | 1977, Virginia | Ethel May Knicely (1904–1949) |
| 5 | **Nelson Roudnal Smallwood** | 1925, Virginia | 1955, Virginia | Lutie Fannie Clark (1926–2001) |
| 6 | **Dennis Eugene "Gene" Smallwood** | 1950, Virginia | 2014, Virginia | Susan (Dean) |
| 7 | **Brady Smallwood** and siblings | — | — | — |

Two things leap off this table before a single story is told.

First: **the line never leaves Virginia.** Seven generations, every recorded birth and death inside the Commonwealth. Whatever else the Smallwoods were, they were Virginians without interruption for more than two centuries.

Second: **the line goes dark above 1792.** James Smallwood has no parents recorded in the pedigree. Every other major family stream in the DeYoung workbook — the Clarks, the German Valley lines, the Deans — extends eight, twelve, in some cases nineteen generations. The surname line stops at a single man born the year after the Bill of Rights was ratified. More on what that means, and doesn't mean, below.

## 1. James Smallwood (1792–~1865) — The Man at the Top of the Dark

**What is documented (2).** Born 1792 in Virginia; died about 1865 in Virginia; wife Elizabeth Gear, born about 1800 in Virginia. FamilySearch ID G4MH-YYR. That is the entire record as it stands in this project.

**What this means in context.** A man born in 1792 in Virginia came of age in the era of the War of 1812, farmed or labored through the antebellum decades, and died — "about 1865" — as the Civil War ended and the Virginia he had known his whole life collapsed around him. Whether he died before or after Appomattox, whether the war touched his household directly, whether the "about" hides a wartime death — none of that is in the record we hold. The dates frame an extraordinary span of Virginia history; the record itself is silent on all of it.

**What he did NOT do, so far as we know.** Nothing in this project's research connects James Smallwood to the Irish Creek / Bear Mountain community, to any court case, to any of Plecker's lists, or to any racial-classification event. No Smallwood appears on the 1943 Plecker surname list entries pulled for Rockbridge and Amherst in this project's files. On present evidence the Smallwoods were simply not part of that story. *(Absence of evidence, rated as such — the race columns of the census years 1850–1870 have not yet been pulled for this household. That pull is on the research plan.)*

**What is still open — the big one.** Who were James's parents? Three honest possibilities, in no ranked order because we have no evidence to rank them:

1. **A gap in FamilySearch, not in history.** Most dead-ends in consensus trees are just un-transcribed records. Virginia county records of the 1780s–1790s (personal property tax lists, marriage bonds, wills) may name his father in an afternoon of archive work.
2. **A migration from another Smallwood cluster.** The best-known American Smallwoods are the Charles County, Maryland family — the line of General William Smallwood of the Maryland Line, Revolutionary War fame and later governor of Maryland. Maryland borders Virginia; families crossed the Potomac constantly. **There is no documented connection between Brady's line and the Maryland Smallwoods, and this paper makes no such claim.** It is listed here only as the obvious hypothesis a records search would test first. *(Context, 3, for the Maryland family's existence; no rating for any link because none is claimed.)*
3. **An origin that was quietly dropped.** The Clark research taught this family that Virginia lines sometimes go dark because someone had a reason to stop writing things down. There is currently zero evidence of that here — no anomalous census classification, no community tie — but the Clark experience earns the possibility a mention, and the same records pull that tests hypothesis 1 would surface it if it exists.

The surname itself is English — habitational, from the place-name Smallwood in Cheshire — one of the ordinary English surnames of the colonial Chesapeake. *(Context, 3.)*

**A data flag for the record.** In the DeYoung workbook as extracted, Henry J. Smallwood (gen 6, chart 2.04) and Hamilton Osborn Smallwood Sr. (gen 5, chart 2.02) carry the *same* FamilySearch ID: GQ97-TPK. A father and son cannot share one profile ID; one of the two entries is a merge artifact or copy error in the sheet. This does not undermine the line itself — the census trail for these generations is straightforward to verify — but it means one of those two men's FamilySearch profile has not actually been looked at yet. Small thing, worth an hour, could surprise us. *(Observation on the source file itself, 5 — the discrepancy is verifiable in the preserved extraction, line 28 vs. line 33.)*

## 2. Henry J. Smallwood (1831–1904) — The Question Mark in Gray

**What is documented (2).** Born 1831, Virginia; died 1904, Virginia; wife Ann Elizabeth (surname rendered "Hell" in the sheet — almost certainly a transcription of Heil, Hull, Hall, or Hawse; see note), 1838–1920.

**Naming note.** The sheet places Jacob Hawse (1786–1861) and Sarah Ann Anthony (1807–1884) at the chart positions that would make them Ann Elizabeth's parents — which would make her surname *Hawse*, not "Hell." Alternatively "Hell" is a phonetic rendering of the German *Heil*. Either way the printed surname is a drift artifact, and either resolution is interesting: Hawse and Heil are both Valley-German-adjacent names, which would mean the Smallwood men were marrying into the German stream a full two generations before Charles Richard married Ethel May Knicely. Flagged for verification. *(Analysis of the source sheet, 3.)*

**What this means in context.** Henry was 30 years old when Virginia seceded. A 30-year-old Virginia farmer in 1861 sat squarely in the conscription window that the Confederate draft acts of 1862 would formalize. **Whether Henry J. Smallwood served, hired a substitute, was exempted, or evaded is completely unknown** — no service record has been sought, let alone found. This is the single most answerable open question in this paper: the National Park Service Soldiers and Sailors database and Fold3's compiled service records make "did a Virginia man named Smallwood, born 1831, serve?" a one-evening question. Until someone spends that evening, this paper claims nothing. *(Open question, deliberately unrated.)*

What we can say: he survived the war decade, whatever his part in it, raised a family through Reconstruction, and lived to 1904 — long enough to have known his grandson's generation. His son Hamilton was born in 1864, in the last full year of the war, while Virginia was a battlefield. A child born to a Rockbridge-area family in 1864 entered a world of requisitioned crops, absent men, and Confederate money worth nothing. That is context, not biography — but it is true context. *(Context, 4.)*

## 3. Hamilton Osborn Smallwood Sr. (1864–1938) — The Reconstruction Child

**What is documented (2).** Born 1864, Virginia; died 1938, Virginia; wife Ida Florence Johnson (1869–1948). The "Sr." suffix implies a Hamilton Osborn Jr. — a collateral line not yet traced.

Ida's line is one of the sheet's quiet strengths: her parents Richard James Johnson (1835–1895) and Frances Elizabeth Manning (1840–1924), her grandparents James Johnson (b. 1790) and Lucy Ledwidge (b. 1800), John Manning (1810–1886) and Elizabeth Hill (1811–1899). Ledwidge is an Irish surname — a thread of Irish ancestry entering the Smallwood side that has never been remarked on anywhere in the family's self-story. *(Sheet data, 2; surname origin context, 3.)*

**What this means in context.** Hamilton's life is the bridge every Southern family of this era has: born in the war, a child of Reconstruction, an adult of the New South, dead two years before the Second World War reached America. He is also the first man in this line whose life is fully inside the era of systematic vital records — Virginia's Bureau of Vital Statistics began statewide birth and death registration in 1912, and the federal census caught him at every decade of his adult life. His household enumerations (1880 through 1930) are sitting in the FamilySearch stacks un-pulled. He is, in research terms, the lowest-hanging fruit in the whole line. *(Context, 4.)*

## 4. Charles Richard Smallwood (1902–1977) — The Long Life

**What is documented (2–3).** Born 1902, Virginia; died 1977, Virginia; married Ethel May Knicely (1904–1949). Their son Nelson Roudnal was born in 1925.

Charles Richard is the only man in the documented patriline between 1792 and 1955 who lived past 75. And that long life contains the line's deepest sorrow, which the dates alone tell: **he outlived his wife by twenty-eight years and his son by twenty-two.** Ethel May died in 1949 at 45. Nelson Roudnal died in 1955 at 30. Charles Richard buried them both before his own father's age at death, then lived on — through the entire childhood and young adulthood of his grandson Gene — until 1977.

He is also the man in this line whom living memory can still reach. Brady's father Gene was 27 when Charles Richard died; Gene's half-siblings and cousins knew him. **Someone alive today sat in that man's kitchen.** Every question this paper can't answer about the earlier Smallwoods — where the family farmed, what church they kept, what Henry did in the war, whether anyone ever said where James came from — had a chance of being answerable in Charles Richard's living room, and may still be answerable one interview away. *(Sheet data, 2; the interview imperative, see Research Plan.)*

Through Ethel May Knicely, this line joins the family's German-Swiss stream — the Knicelys, Zumbros, Wisemans, and Moneymakers of the Valley. That stream gets its own companion paper, *The Palatine Current*, written alongside this one.

## 5. Nelson Roudnal Smallwood (1925–1955) — The Missing Man

**What is documented (2).** Born 1925, Virginia; died 1955, Virginia, at age 30. Married Lutie Fannie Clark (1926–2001). Father of Dennis Eugene Smallwood, born 1950.

**What this means — and here the two halves of the family history meet.** Nelson Roudnal Smallwood married Lutie Fannie Clark. The DeYoung workbook says it structurally — its sheets are literally labeled "SMALLWOOD–Gene father" and "CLARK–Gene mother." **This marriage, sometime in the late 1940s, is the single junction where the quiet English-named Smallwood line and the storm-tossed Monacan-descended Clark line become one family.** Every question this project has pursued about Irish Creek, about Joseph Anderson Clark's 1911 baptism, about the Sorrells courtroom and Plecker's lists, enters Brady's bloodstream through Lutie. Every letter of Brady's surname enters through Nelson. One couple. *(Structural fact from the pedigree, 3; the Clark side independently corroborated at 4–5 throughout this project.)*

And then the fracture. Consider what the dates say about the years 1948–1955, without one word of embellishment:

- **1948** — Ida Florence Johnson Smallwood dies. Nelson loses his grandmother.
- **1949** — Ethel May Knicely Smallwood dies at 45. Nelson, 24, loses his mother. Gene is not yet born.
- **1950** — Gene is born.
- **1955** — Nelson Roudnal Smallwood dies at 30. **Gene is about five years old.**

Cause of death: not in our records. Accident, illness, anything else — unknown, and the death certificate (Virginia, 1955, obtainable) will say. What is certain is the shape of what followed: Lutie Fannie Clark was widowed at about 29 with a five-year-old son, and Gene grew up with half-siblings whose surnames — **Simmons, Weyerman, Pugh, Lee** — mark her subsequent remarriage(s). The May 2026 analysis flagged an open question here worth stating carefully: it has not been independently verified whether Gene's surname-at-birth was Smallwood through this documented chain, or whether a step-adoption pattern touched the name along the way. The FamilySearch trail says Nelson Roudnal was his father; the half-sibling surname pattern says the household story was complicated; a 1950 Virginia birth record would settle it flatly. *(Sheet + ANALYSIS.md, 2–3; open question honestly marked.)*

Here is the human fact underneath the citations: **the Smallwood name reached Brady through a house of absences.** Gene carried a surname belonging to a father he could barely have remembered, kept it among siblings who bore four other names, and passed it to his sons. The name survived not because an unbroken chain of Smallwood men handed it down around the dinner table — after 1955 there was no Smallwood man at the table — but because a Clark widow kept it on her boy. The Clark line gave this family its blood-history; it may be that a Clark woman is also the reason the Smallwood name itself survived to be written about.

## 6. Dennis Eugene "Gene" Smallwood (1950–2014)

Gene's full story belongs to living memory and to his children, not to a records-based paper — this project keeps its whitepapers on the far side of the privacy line. What belongs here is only his place in the chain: son of Nelson Roudnal and Lutie, the boy who kept the name, father of Brady and his siblings, died in Virginia in 2014. The master whitepaper's subtitle already names his children as its audience: *the children of Gene Smallwood, grandchildren of Lutie Clark.*

## 7. What the Smallwood line is NOT — the negative finding, stated as a finding

This project has learned to treat absences as data. So, stated plainly:

- **No Smallwood appears in any Monacan-community record pulled by this project** — not on the joepayne.org rolls, not in the Sorrells or 1876 Clark case files, not on Plecker's 1943 Rockbridge/Amherst surname lists. *(4 — absence across multiple verified sources.)*
- **No racial-classification anomaly** has been found on any Smallwood record — with the honest caveat that the 1850–1870 census race columns for James's and Henry's households have not yet been pulled. *(2 — pending the pull.)*
- **No migration** — the line is Virginian at every recorded event for two-plus centuries. *(2–3.)*

The composite picture, subject to verification: an ordinary English-surnamed Virginia farming line, untouched by the classification wars that battered the Clarks, which merged into the Monacan-descended stream in a single mid-century marriage and then nearly vanished as a male line within a decade. The ordinariness *is* the finding. Half of Brady's surname-story is the extraordinary persecution of the Clarks; the other half is the plain Virginia anonymity the Clarks were being denied. One family now holds both.

## 8. Research plan — closing the dark

Ranked by leverage per hour:

1. **Interviews, now.** Gene's half-siblings (Simmons, Weyerman, Pugh, Lee lines) and any surviving cousins knew Lutie; the eldest may remember Charles Richard and secondhand stories of Nelson. Every year this window narrows. One recorded conversation outranks any archive trip. **This is the only item on this list that expires.**
2. **Nelson Roudnal's death certificate (Virginia, 1955)** — cause of death, informant, residence, burial. Also his 1940s marriage record to Lutie and Gene's 1950 birth record. Three documents, one Virginia vital-records request, settles the fracture decade.
3. **The Hamilton pull** — census 1880–1930 for Hamilton Osborn's household + resolve the GQ97-TPK shared-ID artifact between Henry and Hamilton's profiles.
4. **Henry J. and the war** — NPS Soldiers & Sailors + Fold3 search for Virginia Smallwoods matching b. 1831. One evening.
5. **The James problem** — Virginia personal property tax lists and marriage bonds, 1780s–1815, for Smallwood entries; test the Maryland-origin hypothesis against Charles County records only if Virginia comes up empty.
6. **The 1850–1870 race columns** for James's and Henry's households — expected to be unremarkable; pulled so the negative finding in §7 can be upgraded from (2) to (5).
7. **Resolve "Ann Elizabeth Hell"** — Hawse vs. Heil, and with it the question of whether German-stream marriages entered the Smallwood line two generations earlier than currently mapped.
