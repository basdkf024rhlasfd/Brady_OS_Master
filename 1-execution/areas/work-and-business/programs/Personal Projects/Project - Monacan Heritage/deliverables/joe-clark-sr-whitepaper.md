# Ole Joe Clark
## The Life, Land, and Legal Legacy of Joseph Carter Clark Sr. (1797–1859)

**Brady Smallwood — Family Whitepaper**
**Compiled May 2026 | Source-rated 1–5 (5 = primary archive; 1 = anecdotal)**

---

## A Note Before Starting

There is a tombstone in Rocky Mountain Cemetery in Rockbridge County, Virginia. It reads:

> **IN MEMORY OF OLE JOE CLARK**
> **1797 — 1859**
> **BURIED AT ROCKY MT. CEMETERY**

That stone is the reason for this document.

The man buried there is Joseph Carter Clark Sr. — farmer, lumberman, patriarch of nine children, owner of 2,561 acres on Irish Creek, and the great-great-great-grandfather from whom Brady Smallwood descends through the line of Lee Harrison Clark. He died on 8 January 1859 of kidney stones, at age 61. His family buried him and carved his name into stone. His community named a folk song after him. A century later, his descendants — fighting Walter Plecker's paper genocide — used the documented chain of his life as the evidentiary backbone of two successful courtroom challenges to Virginia's Racial Integrity Act.

He almost certainly never thought of himself as a historical figure. He thought of himself as a man trying to build something on Irish Creek.

He was right about that. What he built turns out to be more than lumber.

## Part 1 — Who He Was

**Full name:** Joseph Carter Clark Sr.
**FamilySearch ID:** LVTG-YCP (32 attached sources, 18 memories)
**Born:** 1797, Amherst County, Virginia
**Died:** 8 January 1859, Rockbridge County, Virginia
**Cause of death:** Gravel (19th-century term for kidney or bladder stones)
**Buried:** Rocky Mountain Cemetery, Rockbridge County, Virginia
**Race in primary records:** Mulatto (1850 U.S. Census); Colored/Black (1859 Virginia Death Register)

Joseph Carter Clark Sr. is the earliest ancestor in Brady's direct paternal Monacan line for whom we have named primary source documentation. He appears in five federal census households (1830, 1840, 1850), in a marriage bond from 1819, in his own will dated 3 January 1859 (probated the same week he died), and in the 1859 Virginia Death Register. His name appears on a tombstone. His children named him in the death record informant line. His grandson's family submitted a family tree tracing back to him as evidence in a Virginia Circuit Court case in 1924.

He is not a mystery. He is a documented man who has been hiding in plain sight because the state of Virginia spent a century trying to make people like him disappear from the record.

## Part 2 — His Parents and Origins

Joseph's parents were **James Clark Sr.** (1770–1840) and **Anna Moseley** (1752–1804). His mother died when he was approximately seven years old. He spent the bulk of his childhood and early adulthood with his father and siblings — a household that the 1830 and 1840 censuses would classify as Free Colored.

His documented siblings include:
- Jane Clark (1786–)
- **Nelson Harrison Clark** (1793–1871) — his closest brother, who figures directly in the Irish Creek settlement story
- Micajah Lynch Clark (1800–)
- Benjamin H. Clark (1800–)
- James Clark Jr. (1801–1887)

The 1859 death register, completed by Joseph's son **Lee Harrison Clark** as informant, names his father as **James Clarke** — a small but significant detail. The family maintained knowledge of the preceding generation's identity into Joseph's old age and recorded it at his death.

### The Henrico County → Blue Ridge Migration

A 1956 letter to the editor of the *Lexington County News* — written by Mary Monroe Penick and now attached as a source memory on Joseph's FamilySearch profile — provides the family tradition of origin. The letter is contemporaneous with a Roanoke Symphony performance of Dr. Charles Vardell's "Joe Clark Steps Out," a composition based on the famous folk song, and was written to document for the public record where "Old Joe Clark" actually came from.

Penick's account: *"Sometime after the War of 1812, Joseph and his brother Nelson Clark left their home in Henrico County and came to the Blue Ridge section in recognition of military service, with land grants recorded in the County Courthouse Deed Books. They settled among a Cherokee/Indian community in the mountains, and Joseph and his brother Nelson married into Indian families; the descendants lived in Irish Creek."*

Several points are worth unpacking here:

**The War of 1812 service claim.** The Sorrells v. Shields case (1924–25) explicitly submitted War of 1812 service records as evidence of the Clark family's Indian (not African) ancestry. Military service in that era was used by mixed-blood families as documentation of their standing — free men of standing served; enslaved men and men classified as free Negroes faced different rules. Whether Joseph himself served or his father James Clark Sr. served, the family preserved a military service tradition that their descendants treated as a genealogical anchor.

**Henrico County origin.** Henrico County in 1797–1815 was not rural backwater — it contained Richmond and the surrounding area. The Clark family's movement from Henrico to the Blue Ridge is consistent with a broader pattern of Monacan-descended families retreating into the mountains after the Fort Christanna closure (post-1720). Some of those families circulated through the Richmond corridor before moving further west.

**"Married into Indian families."** Joseph married **Mary Margaret "Polly" Clark** in 1819. She bears the same Clark surname — a classic feature of community endogamy, where a small intermarried group circulates the same surnames across generations. The family tradition of "marrying into Indian families" encodes what the census would record as Mulatto: the tri-racial community of Irish Creek was the Indian families they married into.

**"Cherokee."** The letter uses "Cherokee" — consistent with the 19th and early 20th-century relabeling discussed elsewhere in this research. The population the Clarks settled among was not Cherokee in the linguistic or tribal sense. It was the Eastern Siouan / Monacan-descended community of Irish Creek and the adjacent Bear Mountain corridor in Amherst County. "Cherokee" was the only Native identity widely legible to 20th-century newspaper readers, so that is the word Penick used. (Credibility 3/5 — secondary account of family tradition; the tradition is consistent with primary record evidence.)

**Birth in Amherst County.** Joseph was born in Amherst County, which is directly south of Rockbridge across the Blue Ridge. Amherst is Bear Mountain country — the heart of the Monacan Indian Nation's documented territorial base. His later move to Irish Creek in Rockbridge brought him to the northern wing of the same community. He did not move away from his people; he moved within the extended territory of the same population. (Credibility 4/5 — 1859 death register gives Amherst as birthplace.)

## Part 3 — The Marriage: Joe and Polly

Joseph Clark Sr. married **Mary Margaret "Polly" Clark** on **14 August 1819** in Rockbridge County, Virginia. He was 22. She was also born in 1797, making them near-exact contemporaries. The original 1819 marriage bond survives and is attached as an image memory on his FamilySearch profile. The bond names **Leonard Clarke** as the second party — possibly a relative standing surety on the bond, a common practice.

Polly Clark's surname is the same as her husband's — Joseph Clark married a Clark. This is not coincidence. It is the defining pattern of the Irish Creek / Bear Mountain Monacan community: a small, geographically bounded, endogamous population that had been intermarrying under the same core surnames (Clark, Tyree, Sorrells, Painter, Mason, Hicks, Johns) for generations. When you married a Clark in Irish Creek in 1819, you married someone who was almost certainly a cousin, a second cousin, or the child of a family friend who had been part of the same mountain community since the Fort Christanna dispersal a century earlier.

Polly Clark outlived her husband by twenty-three years, dying in 1882 at approximately age 85. She raised nine children in the Irish Creek community. She is listed alongside Joseph in the 1850 census as Mulatto, age 52.

**(Credibility 5/5 — primary marriage bond, death register, and census records.)**

## Part 4 — Race on Paper: What the Records Actually Show

This is the part that requires direct engagement, because the paper record is contradictory and the contradictions are not accidental.

### 1830 Census
The household is listed under Free Colored classifications. Three Free Colored Males under 10 (consistent with young children). One Free Colored Male age 24–35 (Joseph, then approximately 33). Free Colored Females in younger brackets. No individual names in 1830 — just head of household and counts by age/gender/color category.

### 1840 Census
Similar structure. Three Free Colored Males under 10 and three Free Colored Males aged 10–23, consistent with a growing family by the late 1830s.

### 1850 Census — The Full Household Snapshot
**District 51, Rockbridge County, Virginia, household 991, dwelling 979, line 11. Enumerated in Rockbridge Parish.**

| Name | Sex | Age | Race | Birthplace |
|------|-----|-----|------|------------|
| Joseph Clarke | M | 52 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |
| Polly Clarke | F | 52 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |
| Anna Clarke | F | 31 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |
| Albert Clarke | M | 24 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |
| James Clarke | M | 22 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |
| Lucy J. Clarke | F | 18 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |
| William Clarke | M | 16 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |
| Lee Clarke | M | 13 | **Mulatto** | Virginia |

Eight people in one household. Every one of them: Mulatto. The whole family — consistent, deliberate, uniform — recorded as Mulatto by a federal census enumerator in 1850.

Notably absent from the 1850 household: Joseph Carter Clark Jr. (born 1825, age 25 in 1850 — likely living independently or newly married), Sarah W. Clark (born 1826 — married and living in a Tyree household), and Isaiah W. Clark (born 1839 — possibly listed under a different entry or the ages are slightly off).

### 1859 Death Register
The death register lists Joseph as "Colored (Black)." This discrepancy — Mulatto in 1850, Colored in 1859 — is not mysterious. It reflects the inconsistency baked into Virginia's racial classification regime. "Mulatto" and "Colored" were not scientific categories. They were administrative labels applied by different clerks in different years with different incentives. Joseph Clark Sr. did not change between 1850 and 1859. The clerk changed.

### What "Mulatto" Actually Meant
A FamilySearch profile note explains the legal context directly: under Virginia race laws (1705, reinforced by legislation in 1823), persons of Native American ancestry — including descendants of the Monacan Indian Nation — were classified in records as "Mulatto," and after the Civil War as "C" / "Colored." This was not accidental. Virginia law recognized only two categories: white and nonwhite. Indian, as a legal category, had been effectively erased by statute. The only nonwhite category available to census enumerators for a mixed-blood Indian-descended person was Mulatto — and later, Colored.

Joe Clark Sr. appearing as Mulatto in 1850 is not evidence that he had African ancestry. It is evidence that he had Monacan ancestry, and that Virginia's legal apparatus had no other box to put him in.

**(Credibility: 5/5 for primary census and death register; 4/5 for the legal interpretation.)**

## Part 5 — The Land Empire: 2,561 Acres on Irish Creek

This is the part of the story that separates Joe Clark Sr. from most of his contemporaries in the Irish Creek community. He was not just a farmer trying to hold on. He was a businessman assembling a territorial stake.

Beginning in **1842**, he started acquiring land on Irish Creek in Rockbridge County. By **1856**, he had assembled approximately **2,561 acres** through a series of documented purchases from neighboring landowners:

| Year | Tract | Acres | Seller |

| 1846 | — | 116 | Moore family |
| 1847 | — | 59 | Patrick Garland |
| 1849 | — | 59.107 | Samuel Donald |
| 1850 | — | 100 | David Garland |
| 1854 | — | 200 | William Donald |
| 1854 | — | 200 | David Garland |

| 1856 | Gibson Tract | 75 | Samuel Reid |

| 1856 | John Alexander Tract | 826 | — |
| 1856 | Moore Tract | 640 | — |

**Total: approximately 2,561 acres.** Assembled over 14 years of systematic acquisition, peaking in 1856, three years before his death.

The core business was **lumber**. Irish Creek sits in the Blue Ridge at an elevation that produced large-growth chestnut — one of the most commercially valuable timber species in 19th-century Appalachia. Joseph put his sons to work hauling chestnut logs and making shingles. He built a family lumber operation with a documented reputation for quality in **Lexington, Virginia**, the county seat and a significant commercial center for the region. According to family history, the operation later supplied lumber to the Confederate Army — though Joseph himself died in January 1859, before secession, so the wartime contracts were executed by his sons.

Two things are important about this land record:

**First, it establishes Joe Clark Sr. as a man of economic standing.** A Mulatto-classified person accumulating 2,561 acres of timber land and building a recognized commercial operation in Rockbridge County in the 1840s and 1850s is not a marginal figure. He had capital, he had credit or cash to make repeated land purchases, he had commercial relationships in Lexington, and he was able to execute complex multi-party transactions over fourteen years. The record of his land acquisitions is also a record of his legal competence — he executed and filed deeds, which means courts and clerks acknowledged his legal standing to do so.

**Second, this is why "Clarktown" still exists.** Clarktown Lane, off Wert Faulkner Highway (Route 130) near Arnolds Valley Lane outside Glasgow, Rockbridge County — this surviving place name is almost certainly an artifact of the Clark family land footprint that Joe Clark Sr. assembled. When a family owns 2,561 acres in a mountain holler, the holler gets their name. It is still on the map.

**(Credibility: 4/5 — land records cited in the FamilySearch profile brief life history; individual deeds would be confirmable at Rockbridge County Circuit Court.)**

## Part 6 — Nine Children: The Downline

Joseph and Polly Clark had nine documented children. All nine appear in Joseph's will, dated 3 January 1859. Together they produced the documented Clark-Tyree-Sorrells-Painter-Peters web that defines the Irish Creek Monacan community in the late 19th century.

### The Children

**1. Anna Clarke** (1819 – deceased) — listed in the 1850 household at age 31, still in her parents' home. One documented child: Joseph Addison Clark (1851–1925), who himself had seven children.

**2. Joseph Carter Clark Jr.** (1825–1886) — the most prolific branch. Ten children, including the multigenerational Clark family visible in the 1850 census as Household 178 (Joseph Clark Jr., age 26, Mulatto, Farmer — confirmed in primary census records for Rockbridge District 51). His line produced twelve grandchildren through daughter Alwilda Caroline Clark alone.

**3. Albert G. Clark** (1826–1906) — no documented children.

**4. Sarah W. Clark** (1826–1912) — **The Clark-Tyree link.** Sarah married into the Tyree family. Through this marriage, the two most prominent surnames on Plecker's 1943 target lists — Clark and Tyree — are directly connected by blood at the second generation from Joe Clark Sr. Sarah's eleven Tyree children include Josephus C. Tyree (13 children), Sidney Baxter Tyree (12 children), and George D. Tyree (11 children with Sarah Jane Clark — a Clark marrying back into the Clark line). The Tyree cascade from Sarah W. Clark is itself a major demographic branch of the Monacan community.

**5. James Leonard Clark** (1828–1887) — nine children, including James Cornelius Clark (1878–1941), whose first marriage was to Aurilla Johns and whose second marriage brought the Southers surname into the family. (Both Johns and Southers/Southards appear on Plecker's 1943 Amherst and Rockbridge lists.)

**6. Lucy Jane Clark** (1832–1906) — married into the Peters family. Four children in the Peters line. Her daughter Georgiana Virginia Peters (1862–1937) bridges the Clark community into the Peters surname.

**7. Lee Harrison Clark** (1837–1870) — **Brady's direct ancestor.** Lee Harrison Clark is identified in the FamilySearch data as "Lutie Fannie Clark's 2nd great-grandfather" — placing him in Brady's direct line of descent. Lee Harrison died young, at 33, in 1870. His documented children include:
  - Patrick Harrison Lee Clark (1858–1860) — died in infancy
  - Almira Jane Clarke (1859–)
  - Abner Gilt Clark (1866–1945)
  - James William Clark (1866–)
  - **Joseph Anderson Clark (1869–1963)** — Brady's 3rd great-grandfather; the central figure of the main whitepaper in this series

Lee Harrison Clark was thirteen years old in the 1850 census, listed in his father's household as Mulatto. He died in 1870, one year after Joseph Anderson Clark was born. He never saw his son grow to adulthood.

**8. William Albert Clark** (1838–1875) — died young at 37. Four children: William Henry Clark (1862–1956), Luther Jackson Clark (1866–1940), Alfred C Clark (1870–1905), James Edward Clark (1873–1898).

**9. Isaiah W. Clark** (1839–1899) — two documented children: Abner Clark (1867–) and Ann D. Mays (1870–).

### The Scope of the Downline

From nine children, Joe Clark Sr. produced (at the third generation alone):
- At minimum 60+ documented grandchildren
- Hundreds of great-grandchildren, many of whom lived into the 20th century
- Surnames Clark, Tyree, Peters, Wood, Pultz, Sorrells, Johns, Southers — almost every name on Plecker's 1943 Rockbridge County target list flows through this tree

The endogamy is visible in the naming: multiple Clarks married other Clarks (Joseph Carter Clark Jr.'s daughter Dewey M. Clark married Lessie Ann Clark; James Franklin Clark married Aurilla Johns and then Virginia Frances Southers; Rachel Ann Tyree — Joe's granddaughter through Sarah — married Henry Givens Clark). The community circulated within itself, which is exactly what the 1946 Smithsonian anthropological record describes: "a group apart from both Whites and Negroes," with "their own churches, schools, and cemeteries."

## Part 7 — The Folk Song: "Old Joe Clark"

The American folk song "Old Joe Clark" is one of the most widely collected pieces in the Appalachian and southern folk repertoire. It has been recorded by hundreds of artists across more than a century and appears in virtually every major folk song anthology. The question of whether it is connected to Joseph Carter Clark Sr. of Irish Creek requires careful handling — because the musicological record is more complicated than the family tradition suggests.

### The competing claims

There are three documented competing theories for the song's origin. They are not equally supported.

**Claim A — Joe Clark of Clay County, Kentucky (strongest documented claim)**

Joseph Clark, born 18 September 1839 in Clay County, Kentucky. Married Elizabeth "Betty" Sandlin in 1857. Civil War veteran (discharged 1862 for disability). Farmer and general store operator. Shot and killed in 1886 near the back porch of his store. Kentucky State Historical Marker #1382 has been erected at Sexton's Creek, Clay County, specifically commemorating this man as the subject of the song. The Clay County claim is cited by multiple genealogical and historical sources. The earliest documented reference to the song in print is the *Journal of American Folklore*, Vol. 25, p. 152 (1905), which identifies it as "sung by East Tennessee whites" — geographically closer to Clay County, Kentucky than to Rockbridge County, Virginia.

**Claim B — Joe Clark of Irish Creek, Rockbridge County, Virginia (local oral tradition)**

The Virginia claim rests primarily on two sources: the 1956 Penick letter (described below) and a 1983 account by musicologist Mike Seeger documenting local oral tradition — that Joe Clark's father settled around Irish Creek near South River, Rockbridge County, in the early 1800s, and that a jilted suitor wrote the song in the late 1800s. Seeger also notes: "The Clarks have been family-style string musicians right down through the years." This is a local oral tradition, not independently verified by historians. It is consistent with the 1797–1859 Joe Clark Sr. of Irish Creek, but it does not establish him as the song's subject.

**Claim C — A Maryland murder (fringe, poorly documented)**

A theory connecting the song to a post-Civil War murder in Maryland involving a man named Joe Clark, his wife Betsy, and a traveling salesman named Herbert Brown. Poorly documented; not treated as credible by folk music scholars.

### The 1956 Penick letter

A letter to the editor of the *Lexington County News*, written by Mary Monroe Penick on 22 October 1956, is attached as a source memory on Joseph Carter Clark Sr.'s FamilySearch profile. It records Irish Creek community tradition: Sam Downey, an illiterate teamster for the **South River Lumber Company**, was in love with Joe Clark's daughter **Betty Brown** (Betsy Brown in some song versions). While driving his six-horse log team along mountain roads, he composed verses to existing tunes; "Old Joe Clark" was one. The letter was prompted by a Roanoke Symphony performance of Dr. Charles Vardell's "Joe Clark Steps Out." At the time, a grandson of "the original Joe Clark" was reportedly still living on Irish Creek in his eighties.

This letter is genuine. It documents a real community tradition. But it is a secondary account written 97 years after Joe Clark Sr.'s death, and it does not engage with or rebut the Kentucky claim — because Penick was writing a local-interest piece, not a musicological argument.

### What the musicological record actually says

Library of Congress musicologist Alan Jabbour found **no 19th-century printed examples** of the song and assessed it as "likely no older than the late 19th or early 20th century." Folklorist Samuel Bayard (1981) concluded the melody was possibly derived from the minstrel tune "Lucy Long," with lyrics assembled from floating verses borrowed across folk traditions — *Shady Grove*, *Cindy*, *Ida Red*, and others. The first commercial recording was Fiddlin' John Carson's "Fare You Well Old Joe Clark" (OKeh 40038, 1923), followed by Cowan Powers and Family (3rd best-selling country record of 1924). Cecil Sharp first notated the song in 1918 from a Virginia singer, Margaret Dodd — not from an Irish Creek source.

**No musicological consensus exists that the song originated in Rockbridge County.** The honest scholarly position is that the song's ultimate origin is ambiguous, with the Kentucky claim having the strongest external documentary evidence (a state historical marker is a formal public recognition, not genealogical speculation) and the Virginia claim resting on local oral tradition documented in the 1950s–1980s.

### What this means for the family record

The connection between Joseph Carter Clark Sr. and the folk song "Old Joe Clark" is a genuine part of Irish Creek community identity and should be documented as such. It is not fabricated. The Clarks of Irish Creek understood themselves as the family behind the song, and that self-understanding persisted for at least a century after Joe's death.

But it should not be stated as fact that Joseph Carter Clark Sr. is the subject of the song. The more defensible claim is: **the Irish Creek Clark community maintained a tradition that the song referenced their patriarch, and that tradition is documented in community memory going back at least to 1956.** Whether or not Joe Clark Sr. is the song's origin, the song became part of how his community remembered him — and that is itself a historical fact.

**"Betty Brown" / "Betsy Brown."** This name does not appear among Joe Clark Sr.'s nine documented children (who are all named in his 1859 will). Whether she was a daughter who died young before the will, a grandchild, a neighbor's daughter who worked at the sawmill, or a name that Downey altered for the song is not established. The overlap with the "Brown People" ethnographic label for the Irish Creek Indian community is noted but not conclusive.

**Donna Huffer's book.** *Fare Thee Well Old Joe Clark: History of the Clark Family of Rockbridge County, Virginia* uses the song's title as its frame. The book content has not been obtained; it is not digitized and not available online. Whether Huffer argues for the Irish Creek origin of the song itself, or simply uses the song title because it invokes the family name, is unknown pending acquisition. This book remains the highest-priority unread source in the research.

**(Credibility: 1/5 for the claim that this Joe Clark Sr. is the definitive subject of the folk song — the Kentucky claim is better externally documented. 3/5 for the claim that the Irish Creek Clark community maintained a tradition connecting the song to their family — documented in community memory by Penick 1956 and Seeger 1983. The song's title as a frame for Huffer's Rockbridge County Clark genealogy is a real connection; its musicological implications are unresolved.)**

**Mary Clark** — his wife Polly

**Wm. A. Clark** — son William Albert

## Part 9 — The Legal Chain He Left Behind

Joe Clark Sr. did not intend to leave a legal legacy. He left a documented life. But a documented life, in the hands of his descendants, became exactly the legal evidence that protected the Clark family from Plecker's paper genocide.

Here is the sequence:

**1. Joe Clark Sr. (1797–1859)** accumulates 2,561 acres, files deeds, executes a will, appears in three federal censuses. He is Mulatto in the record — a recognized legal person with property and a documented chain of descendants.

**2. James Clark** (identity not yet precisely fixed — likely a son of Joseph Carter Clark Jr., born ~1820s–1840s) applies for a marriage license in December **1876** to marry Cerinda Robison. Rockbridge County Clerk J. P. Moore refuses, calling him "a black man." James Clark files for writ of mandamus. The court rules in his favor. The decision goes into Rockbridge County Court Order Book, 1876, pages 137 and 174. The family tree connecting James Clark back to Joe Clark Sr. is now a matter of record in a court file (Library of Virginia accession 1140710).

**3. Atha Sorrells** (born 1904), great-granddaughter of Joe Clark Jr. and Julia Sorrells Clark — herself a Clark descendant — applies for a marriage license in November **1924**. Rockbridge County Clerk A. T. Shields refuses, invoking the Racial Integrity Act passed that same year. Atha files for writ of mandamus. Her attorneys submit a **family tree tracing back to Joe Clark Sr. (born 1797)** as evidence, along with the 1876 James Clark precedent. Walter Plecker testifies against her. Judge Henry W. Holt rules for Atha, holding that the evidence covering "130 years" showed no blood "other than white, except Indian, and there is not enough of that to come within the statute." Atha and Robert Painter marry May 31, 1925. (Library of Virginia accessions 1140744 / 1164679.)

**4. Claude Clark** (grandson of Joseph Anderson Clark, Brady's cousin line), age 12, seeks priesthood ordination in the LDS Buena Vista Branch in **1951**. Branch President Eugene Larsen finds a "colored" notation on the courthouse records. He writes to Salt Lake City. LDS Church Genealogical Society Elder William S. Tanner responds — bad news first, then keeps digging. Genealogical missionaries are dispatched to Virginia. The Clark family's Indian identity is established, the priesthood ban is lifted, and Claude Clark, Alvin Coleman, and Garvis Wheeler are ordained. The evidentiary chain used in this fight traces back to the same documented family tree that Atha Sorrells used in 1924 — which itself traces back to Joe Clark Sr.

**Three fights. One chain. The 1797 patriarch at the root of all of it.**

Joe Clark Sr. did not create this chain intentionally. He simply lived — filing deeds, appearing in censuses, writing a will, being recorded as Mulatto by the federal government. That record, accumulated over 62 years of documented life, became an anchor that his descendants could grasp when the state tried to erase them. You cannot erase someone who already wrote 2,561 acres of deed transfers into the Rockbridge County Courthouse.

## Part 10 — The Tombstone

"Ole Joe Clark" — that is what they carved. Not "Joseph Carter Clark Sr." Not "Beloved Father and Husband." Not a scripture verse or a birth location or a list of offices held.

Ole Joe Clark.

The nickname tells you something. In a tight mountain community, the patriarch accumulates a name that his people use. "Old Joe Clark" — *ole* as the mountain pronunciation — is a term of familiarity and affection. The folk song used it. The tombstone used it. The 1956 newspaper letter used it. The family identity ran through this name for a century.

The stone is at Rocky Mountain Cemetery, Rockbridge County, Virginia. The cemetery's location is consistent with the Irish Creek / South River corridor where the Clark family land holdings were concentrated. Polly Clark, who died in 1882, is presumably buried there as well — she lived 23 years after her husband, long enough to see most of her grandchildren born.

The stone survives. It has survived 165 years of Appalachian weather, the Civil War, Reconstruction, the Racial Integrity Act, Plecker's campaign, and the LDS church's racial ban. Ole Joe Clark is still in that ground, and his name is still on the stone.

## Part 11 — What We Don't Know Yet

The record on Joe Clark Sr. is stronger than for almost any other figure in this research at this generational depth. But several important questions remain open:

**His parents' origins.** James Clark Sr. (1770–1840) and Anna Moseley (1752–1804) — the Moseley name is notably not in the canonical Monacan surname cluster. Whether Anna Moseley was a white woman, a Monacan woman operating under an Anglicized name, or a free woman of color with a different background is not established. She died in 1804, leaving few records. The 1800 and 1810 censuses (before her death) are where she might appear. Tracking James Clark Sr.'s 1800, 1810, and 1820 census records — in Henrico County and then in Rockbridge — is the next research step to push the line back past 1797.

**Pre-1842 land history.** The documented Irish Creek land acquisitions begin in 1842, when Joe was 45. What he was doing on Irish Creek between 1819 (marriage) and 1842 (first deed purchase) is not in the available record. He almost certainly was farming, probably as a tenant or squatter on land owned by others before he began purchasing. The 1830 and 1840 census households are listed under his name, which implies some residential stability, but the land tenure before 1842 is unconfirmed.

**Peter Curry.** The enslaved man Peter Curry (1823–1862, FamilySearch KCBT-JMF) is documented as connected to Joe Clark Sr.'s household. The formal "enslaver" relationship was noted on FamilySearch and then deleted in January 2026. The underlying question — whether Peter Curry was legally enslaved by Joe Clark Sr. and what happened to him and his descendants — is not resolved. This is a moral question as much as a genealogical one. Peter Curry's descendants, if any survive, have a connection to this family story.

**The Betty Brown / Betsy Brown identity.** The folk song claims Joe had a daughter named Betty (or Betsy) Brown. His nine documented children do not include a daughter by that name. Whether "Betty Brown" is a daughter who died young (and thus doesn't appear in the will), a daughter by a different name who acquired the nickname, or a fictional/composite character invented by Sam Downey for the song is not established. The "Brown" surname — in the context of the "Brown People" of Irish Creek — adds another layer of interpretive possibility.

**The James Clark of the 1876 case.** This figure is documented in Library of Virginia accession 1140710 as a Clark family member who won a marriage license fight in Rockbridge in 1876. His exact generational relationship to Joe Clark Sr. — whether he is a son of Joseph Carter Clark Jr., a nephew through another line, or something else — requires pulling the full 1876 case file. That file will also contain the earliest surviving Clark family tree document, predating the 1924 Sorrells exhibit.

## Part 12 — Brady's Line Through Joe Clark Sr.

The documented chain from Joseph Carter Clark Sr. to Brady Smallwood runs through **Lee Harrison Clark**, the seventh of Joe's nine children:

```
Joseph Carter Clark Sr. (1797–1859)
    └── Lee Harrison Clark (1837–1870)
            └── Joseph Anderson Clark (1869–1963)
                    └── [Brady's great-grandmother Lutie Fannie Clark]
                            └── [Brady's grandmother]
                                    └── [Brady's parent]
                                            └── Brady Smallwood (1982–)
```

Lee Harrison Clark was thirteen years old in the 1850 census, living in his father's Mulatto household on Irish Creek. He died in 1870 at 33, the year his son Joseph Anderson Clark was born. He is present in the 1850 household but absent from all subsequent records — an early death that erased him from most of the documentation that later generations used to tell this story.

Joseph Anderson Clark (1869–1963) lived 94 years. He and Mary Susan Clark raised eleven children in Alto, Buena Vista, Vesuvius, and Cornwall — the Irish Creek corridor that Joe Clark Sr. had assembled into a lumber operation six decades earlier. In 1906, Joseph Anderson Clark married Mary Susan Clark — and that marriage appears in the joepayne.org index of the Monacan Rolls as "Joseph Clark married Mary S. Clark (1906)." If that identification is correct, Brady is three generations from a man who appears on the Monacan Nation's own compiled rolls.

Joe Clark Sr. is four generations further back. The chain from Brady to Joe Clark Sr. spans six generations and 165 years. Every generation in that chain lived in the same Irish Creek / Rockbridge County corridor that Joe Clark Sr. walked in 1819 when he married Polly Clark and started building toward what would become 2,561 acres of mountain timber land.

## Part 13 — The Meaning of "Ole Joe Clark"

The tombstone uses the nickname. That is not nothing.

In American folk culture, nicknames on tombstones are rare. They appear when the gap between the formal name and the real person is wide enough that the people who loved him wanted to close it — when "Joseph Carter Clark Sr." says nothing, and "Ole Joe Clark" says everything. The community that buried him, the children who mourned him, the sawmill hands who had worked for him, the neighbors who had bought his timber and sold him land parcels — they knew him as Ole Joe. That is the name they carved.

He was born in Amherst County in 1797, which means he was alive during the War of 1812, through the entire antebellum period, through the era of Nat Turner's rebellion (1831), through the Mexican War, through the national debate over slavery that would finally erupt two months after his death when John Brown raided Harpers Ferry in October 1859. He spent his adult life accumulating land and timber in a mountain community that Virginia's law classified as Mulatto and Colored — people without a legal category that matched who they actually were.

He didn't leave any written record of what he thought about any of it. He left deeds, a census household, a will, a tombstone, and nine children.

That is enough. The deeds proved the land. The census proved the family. The will proved his intent. The children carried the rest.

His son Lee Harrison Clark stood at a clerk's window in January 1859 and told them his father was dead. Eleven years later, Lee Harrison was dead too, and his infant son Joseph Anderson Clark was alive. Sixty-five years after that, Atha Sorrells used the family tree that traced back to Lee Harrison's father to win a courtroom fight against Walter Plecker's racial machinery.

The chain from Ole Joe Clark to the 2018 federal recognition of the Monacan Indian Nation is not a straight line. It runs through generations of ordinary decisions — marriages, land purchases, courtroom fights, a twelve-year-old boy asking to be ordained, a twenty-year-old pregnant woman suing a county clerk. But the chain begins at Rocky Mountain Cemetery, Rockbridge County, Virginia, where there is a stone with a name on it.

**Ole Joe Clark. 1797–1859.**

That is where the documented story of this family starts.
