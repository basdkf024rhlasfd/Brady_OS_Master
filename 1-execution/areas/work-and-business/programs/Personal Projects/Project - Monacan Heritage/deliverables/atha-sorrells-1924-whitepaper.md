# Atha Sorrells v. A.T. Shields (1924–1925)
### The Case That Stopped Walter Plecker in Rockbridge County — and What It Means for Our Family

**Brady Smallwood — Family Whitepaper**
**Compiled May 2026 | Source-rated 1–5 (5 = primary archive / peer-reviewed; 1 = anecdotal)**

---

## A note before you read

The Racial Integrity Act of 1924 is taught, when it is taught at all, as a single legislative event — Virginia's version of eugenics law, later undone by *Loving v. Virginia* in 1967. What rarely makes it into the textbook version is the courtroom fight that happened in the same year the Act passed: a 20-year-old woman from a mountain community in Rockbridge County went to court against the state's chief racial enforcer and won.

Her name was **Atha Sorrells**. She was pregnant when the case started. She was a minor under Virginia law. She was represented by two local lawyers against Walter Plecker — the most powerful figure in Virginia's racial bureaucracy — who testified in person against her. She won. Plecker himself decided not to appeal because he was afraid she would win again.

The Clark family tree was the central piece of evidence in her case.

This document is the full story of what happened — who the people were, what they argued, what the court decided, and what happened afterward. It is also the story of how Atha Sorrells fits into our specific family line and why, 26 years later, her court victory helped break open the door that had been slammed on the Clark men inside the LDS Church.

## Part 1 — The World in March 1924

To understand what Atha Sorrells walked into in November 1924, you need to understand what the Virginia legislature had just done eight months earlier.

### The Racial Integrity Act of 1924

**Passed by the Virginia General Assembly: March 20, 1924.**

The Act was drafted by three men working in close coordination: **Walter Ashby Plecker**, director of the Virginia Bureau of Vital Statistics; **John Powell**, a prominent Richmond concert pianist and white-supremacist activist; and **Earnest Sevier Cox**, a writer and racial ideologist. Their intellectual patron was **Madison Grant** of New York, author of *The Passing of the Great Race* (1916) — the single most influential American eugenics text of the 20th century.

The Act did one fundamental thing: it **collapsed Virginia's racial categories to two**. The new law recognized only *white* and *colored*. No Indian category. No mulatto. No free person of color. If you were not unambiguously white — meaning "no trace whatsoever of any blood other than Caucasian" — then you were colored.

There was exactly one carve-out, called the **Pocahontas Exception**: a person with one-sixteenth or less American Indian blood, and no other non-Caucasian blood, could still be classified white. This exception existed not out of generosity to Native people but because hundreds of First Families of Virginia — the Lees, Randolphs, Bollings, and others — traced their lineage to Pocahontas through John Rolfe and would have been reclassified as colored without it. The exception was a class protection for elite white Virginia, not a concession to anyone in Irish Creek.

**The anti-miscegenation provision** was the enforcement lever. Marriage between a white person and any non-white person became a **felony**. County clerks were personally liable if they issued a "white" marriage license to anyone who later turned out to be colored under the new definitions. This meant every clerk in every county courthouse was now effectively an agent of racial enforcement — with criminal exposure if they got it wrong.

Plecker circulated the Act to county clerks across the state within weeks of passage. His accompanying instructions made clear that the law was not self-enforcing — it would require the clerks to push back on any application from a family he had flagged. He already had a list of target surnames. In Rockbridge County, his target list included **Clark**, **Sorrells**, **Painter**, **Johns**, **Tyree**, and roughly a dozen other names from the Irish Creek / Cornwall / Vesuvius community.

### Walter Ashby Plecker — The Man Behind the Desk

Plecker had been running the Bureau of Vital Statistics since 1912. By 1924 he had been in the post for twelve years. He had statutory authority over every birth, death, and marriage record in the Commonwealth.

**Born:** April 2, 1861, Augusta County, Virginia.
**Education:** MD from University of Maryland Medical School, 1885.
**Career:** Country doctor in western Virginia and Alabama; public health officer in Elizabeth City County from 1902; appointed first director of Virginia's new Bureau of Vital Statistics in 1912.
**Death:** August 2, 1947. He was struck by an automobile in Richmond and died — having retired the year before, after thirty-four years in the post.

What made him dangerous was not his power alone but his methodology. He corresponded directly with county clerks across Virginia. He drafted legislation himself. He wrote official Health Bulletins. He threatened midwives with criminal prosecution for falsifying birth records if they listed "Indian." He personally altered existing birth, marriage, and death certificates — physically overwriting "Indian" with "colored" on documents that had been properly filed. The Library of Virginia holds letters he taped to the backs of birth certificates explaining why the named child should not be considered Indian or white. Some of those letters are physically still attached to the certificates.

His most important tool was what historians now call **paper genocide**: the administrative erasure of Native identity through documents, not through violence. You did not need to kill a people to make them legally disappear. You needed only to control the paperwork at the moment of birth, marriage, and death. Plecker controlled it for thirty-four years.

**In January 1943**, he sent a circular letter to registrars and clerks across the state enclosing a list of "mongrel" surnames — families claiming to be Indian or white that he intended to classify as colored. The Rockbridge County list included **Clark, Sorrells, Painter, Johns, Tyree, Hicks, Mason, Floyd, Cash, Coleman, Duff, Hartless, and Wood**, among others. The Amherst County list was longer. Both lists named Clark. Both lists named Sorrells. Both lists named Painter.

Every name on those lists is a surname that appears in the Irish Creek / Bear Mountain Monacan community. The 1943 list is Plecker's own documentation, issued by the Commonwealth of Virginia, that these were the families he was hunting.

## Part 2 — Atha Sorrells

### Who She Was

**Atha Sorrells was born in 1904 in Rockbridge County, Virginia.** She was the daughter of William Sorrells, who served as her "next friend" in the court case — the legal designation for a guardian or parent acting on behalf of a minor under Virginia law at the time.

She died in 1979, at the age of 75. She lived through the entire arc of the events her case set in motion: the 1967 *Loving v. Virginia* decision that finally struck down the anti-miscegenation law she had challenged; the 1975 formal repeal of what remained of the Racial Integrity Act; and the first years of the Monacan Indian Nation's push for state recognition (formally achieved in 1989). She did not live to see the 2018 federal recognition that put the final legal nail in Plecker's coffin, but she had seen most of the unraveling.

**Her great-grandparents were Joe Clark Jr. and Julia (Sorrells) Clark.** This is the genealogical fact that makes the case a Clark family case under a Sorrells caption. Atha's connection to the Clark line was through her maternal great-grandparents. The Clark family tree she and her attorneys submitted as evidence was, in the most literal sense, her own ancestry chart.

Her intended husband was **Robert Painter**. Painter is also on Plecker's Rockbridge County 1943 surname list — a fact that, in 1924, was already implicit in how county officials treated applications from his family. The marriage they were trying to formalize was, to the Irish Creek community, a straightforward union between two families who had been neighbors and kin for generations. To Plecker and to A.T. Shields, the clerk, it was a potential felony.

### The Case That Came First: Dorothy Johns (September 1924)

Before Atha, there was Dorothy Johns — and the contrast between the two cases is essential to understanding why Atha won.

In **September 1924**, just weeks after the Racial Integrity Act took effect, **James Conner and Dorothy Johns** applied for a marriage license at the same Rockbridge County Courthouse. The same clerk, A.T. Shields, refused — on the same grounds. Johns also sued. Judge Holt heard the case. Plecker testified. **Silas Coleman**, a resident of Amherst County, provided anecdotal testimony that Johns had "colored" ancestry.

**Holt refused to issue the license.** He found sufficient evidence that Johns was at least part African American. He raised concerns about the burden of proof in the Act — flagging, even in a ruling against her, that the law was poorly designed — but on the evidence before him, he ruled against her.

The Sorrells case followed weeks later. Shields refused Atha's license on the same grounds he had refused Johns. Atha filed the same type of petition. The same judge heard it. Plecker testified again.

This time, **Silas Coleman declined to testify** — out of fear of community retribution, according to the Encyclopedia Virginia account. He had testified against Dorothy Johns. He would not testify against Atha Sorrells.

**The difference between the two cases was the evidence.** Johns had documented African American ancestry that Plecker's records could show. Atha had the Clark family tree — 130 years of documented white and Indian ancestry, the 1876 James Clark mandamus, War of 1812 service records, and federal land warrants. She had a case Johns didn't have. She won where Johns lost for exactly that reason.

**(Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Racial Integrity Laws (1924–1930)," Brendan Wolfe. Source rating: 5.)**

---

### Why She Was Refused — and Why in November 1924

The sequence of events matters. The Racial Integrity Act passed in March 1924. Eight months later, Atha Sorrells and Robert Painter walked into the Rockbridge County Courthouse.

The clerk — **A.T. (Abner Terry) Shields** — was the same clerk to whom Plecker had been writing correspondence since 1912. Plecker's letters to Shields from 1912 to 1943 survive at the Library of Virginia (accession 1160754). Shields was not a villain in the dramatic sense; he was a county bureaucrat doing what Plecker had told him to do. The Racial Integrity Act gave Plecker's instructions the force of felony law. Shields refused the license.

His grounds: Atha Sorrells was not white under the new Act.

Atha was 20 years old. She was pregnant. Under Virginia law at the time, a person under 21 was a minor and required a guardian to act on their behalf in court.

She filed for a writ of mandamus anyway.

### The Legal Arguments

Atha's attorneys made four distinct arguments:

**1. She had Indian ancestry, not African ancestry.** The evidence was her family tree tracing back to Joe Clark Sr. (born 1797). The chart showed that the ancestry was white and Indian — and specifically that it was not Black. The strategic logic was the Pocahontas Exception: if she had only Indian blood, and less than one-sixteenth of it, she should be classifiable as white.

**2. The 1924 Racial Integrity Act was unconstitutionally vague.** Specifically, "Caucasian" had no settled scientific meaning. No human being could prove the complete absence of non-Caucasian blood across many generations. This argument was a direct attack on the statute itself — and it clearly resonated with Judge Holt.

**3. The 1876 *James Clark v. J.P. Moore* mandamus was precedent.** Forty-eight years earlier, a Rockbridge Circuit Court had already ruled that a member of the Clark family could not be classified as Black and was entitled to a "white" marriage license. That ruling was in the county court order book (pages 137 and 174) and was cited directly in Atha's petition.

**4. The Clerk had no authority to make this determination unilaterally.** Plecker's system required the clerk to act as a racial adjudicator — a role that the statute, Atha's lawyers argued, did not actually authorize the clerk to fill. There was no mechanism for appeal from the clerk, which meant a single bureaucrat's decision could permanently block a marriage.

**Walter Plecker testified in person for the defense.** He appears in the case file as a defense witness — his presence in the courtroom underscoring how personally invested he was in the outcome. He had drafted the law. He was the enforcement mechanism. He was not going to let Irish Creek walk away without a fight. He came to Lexington and testified himself.

**The Clark family tree exhibit** is described in the case file as "a large family tree of the Clark family from whom Atha Sorrells descended." It ran back to Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797). Documented names in the exhibit include:

- **Joe Clark Sr.** (born ca. 1797) — listed as having purchased an enslaved man named Peter Curry at auction. The significance: in antebellum Virginia, purchasing an enslaved person required legal white status. This detail was used to argue that the Clarks had historically been classified white, not colored.
- **Polly Clark** (née Clark) — mother of James Clark
- **James Clark** — won the 1876 *Clark v. Moore* mandamus in the same court. His victory 48 years earlier was cited directly as precedent.
- **Joe Clark Jr.** and **Julia Clark (née Sorrells)** — Atha's great-grandparents
- **John Whiteside** and **Bettie Sorrells** — described in the court record as "always white, no question"

The chart also included War of 1812 service records and federal land warrants — documents establishing that members of the Clark family had been recognized as legally white for more than a century.

## Part 4 — The Ruling

**Judge Henry W. Holt issued his ruling in January 1925.** He ruled for Atha Sorrells. The marriage license was ordered issued.

His memorandum opinion survives in full at Encyclopedia Virginia. It is worth reading as a primary document — not just for the outcome but for the quality of the legal reasoning and the subtle contempt Holt directed at the statute itself. Key passages:

On the impossibility of the burden of proof the Act imposed:

> "In twenty-five generations one has thirty two millions of grandfathers, not to speak of grandmothers, assuming there is no intermarriage. Half the men who fought at Hastings were my grandfathers. Some of them were probably hanged and some knighted. Who can tell? Certainly in some instances there was an alien strain. Beyond peradventure I cannot prove that there was not, and so the relief granted by appeal is no relief at all."

On the meaninglessness of the word "Caucasian":

> "The statute undertakes to define with accuracy who are white and says that they only are white who are of pure Caucasian blood. That is a definition which in the present state of Ethnology has no certain meaning and no meaning upon which two authorities agree."

He then worked through the absurdity in detail — noting that Hungarians are not Caucasians under the scientific definitions of the time, meaning a Hungarian noblewoman who married into a distinguished New York family could not legally marry a white man in Virginia, while "an Arab, a North African, a Todas of India, an Ainus of Japan or the Wild Man of Borneo" could. "All white people are not caucasians, and all caucasians are not white people."

On the scope of the statute's actual purpose vs. its written breadth:

> "The negro alone in Virginia presents a racial problem and it is only necessary to prescribe that there shall be no intermarriage with them within degrees of consanguinity ascertainable and sufficiently remote to provide, as a practical proposition, against reversion to type."

This is Holt effectively telling the legislature: you wrote a law that goes far beyond its stated purpose, and the stated purpose alone would have been sufficient. He found the Act as written to be both vague and absurd.

And the ruling itself:

> "If we discard the letter of the law and apply, as we must, if it is to stand at all, the 'rule of reason' which is that there must be present an appreciable amount of foreign blood, I am of opinion that the evidence in this case which covers a period of 130 years, certainly the weight of it is to the effect that there is no strain present in the applicant of any blood other than white, except Indian, and there is not enough of that to come within the statute. The license should issue."

**"The license should issue."** Four words. That was the ruling.

**(Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, full text of Holt memorandum opinion, primary document. Source rating: 5.)**

This was the correct legal outcome and an unusual one. In 1924–25 Virginia, a judge finding in favor of a mixed-ancestry petitioner against a Plecker-backed racial classification was not common. Holt did it because the evidence was clear and the legal argument — especially the 1876 Clark precedent — was sound.

## Part 5 — Plecker's Reaction

This is the smoking gun.

**On May 9, 1925**, Walter Plecker wrote a letter to A.T. Shields. The letter survives at the Library of Virginia (accession 1160754 — the Rockbridge County Clerk's Correspondence file). In it, Plecker advised Shields **not to appeal Judge Holt's ruling**.

His reasoning, as paraphrased from the surviving record: if the Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals upheld Holt's decision, it would establish binding statewide precedent that anyone with predominantly Indian (not Black) ancestry could be classified white under the Pocahontas Exception. That, Plecker wrote, would "**embolden the Rockbridge tribe**" — his own phrase — and open the door for hundreds of other families on his surname list to seek the same relief in courts across the state.

The phrase *the Rockbridge tribe* refers to the Irish Creek / Cornwall Monacan community. Our family. Plecker used the word "tribe" — acknowledging, in internal correspondence that he thought would never be used against him, that this was a tribal community he was trying to suppress.

He was afraid of what the court had just done. He decided that one quiet loss was better than a statewide appellate loss. So he let the ruling stand. The Virginia Attorney General declined to appeal. The Richmond Times-Dispatch editorialized that the decision should be appealed, but no one acted on it.

**Atha Sorrells and Robert Painter married on May 31, 1925.** Their first daughter was born in July.

This outcome — Plecker withdrawing from a case he lost rather than risk spreading the loss — is exceptional. His system worked because it was almost never challenged in court. When it was challenged, with a documented family tree going back 130 years, it could lose. **Atha Sorrells's case is the only documented successful courtroom defeat of the Racial Integrity Act in Rockbridge County during Plecker's entire 34-year tenure.**

**Source rating: 5** (Plecker May 9, 1925 letter at Library of Virginia; case outcome documented in Encyclopedia Virginia and Native Heritage Project.)

## Part 5b — The Immediate Reaction

### John Powell's Outrage

The man who co-wrote the Racial Integrity Act was in the courtroom when he lost. His reaction, on the record:

> "If this decision is to stand, any negroid in the state can go before a court and say, 'My ancestors are recorded as colored, but that does not mean negro, they were Indians.' He may then be declared white and may marry a white woman."

He predicted that Virginia "would soon be bursting with Indians."

This quote is revealing in two ways. First, it shows exactly what Powell feared: that Holt's ruling opened a crack in the system — that "colored" in old records did not automatically mean "negro," and that a family with documented Indian ancestry had a legal path to whiteness. Second, it shows that Powell understood what the Irish Creek community had always known and Plecker had spent decades trying to suppress: that there *were* Indians in Virginia, they were still there, and they would use the courts if given the chance.

The Richmond Times-Dispatch editorialized on November 20, 1924, urging the state to appeal. The Virginia Attorney General declined.

**(Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Racial Integrity Laws (1924–1930)." Source rating: 5.)**

---

### The 1926 Revised Act — Written Specifically to Close the Sorrells Loophole

The impact of Atha's win can be measured precisely by what happened the following year.

On **January 14, 1926**, delegate Robert O. Norris Jr. introduced a revision of the Racial Integrity Act in the Virginia House of Delegates. Its explicit purpose: to make it impossible for another Atha Sorrells to win in court. The revised language redefined a white person as someone "whose blood is entirely white, having no known, demonstrable or ascertainable admixture of the blood of another race." In other words, it abolished the Pocahontas Exception entirely — eliminating the Indian-ancestry argument that Atha's attorneys had used.

The House amended and passed it. But the Virginia Senate voted 20 to 9 to **indefinitely postpone** its consideration on March 12, 1926. The revision died.

The reason it died: estimates that as many as 20,000 white Virginians possessed small amounts of Indian blood, which would have made them legally "colored" under the new language. The same class-protection logic that had created the Pocahontas Exception in 1924 killed the attempt to remove it in 1926.

Atha Sorrells's win forced the Virginia legislature to try to rewrite its own racial purity law — and the attempt failed because too many elite white Virginians would have been caught in the net.

**(Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Racial Integrity Laws (1924–1930)." Source rating: 5.)**

## Part 6 — The Aftermath

### What Plecker Did Next

He did not change course. He simply did not push the Racial Integrity Act against the Rockbridge Indian community in court again during his tenure. He continued writing letters. He continued threatening clerks and midwives. He continued reclassifying records. He just stopped going to Lexington courtrooms. The *Sorrells* ruling was binding in Rockbridge Circuit Court but had no statewide force — Holt had ruled on the evidence in Atha's specific case, not on the constitutionality of the law. Plecker continued enforcing the Act everywhere else.

He retired in 1946. He was killed by a car in Richmond in 1947.

His system, however, outlived him. The reclassifications he had made to thousands of Virginia vital records remained in place. The birth certificates with "colored" stamped over "Indian" remained in file drawers across the Commonwealth. The children who had been refused entry to white schools because of Plecker-altered records did not get their schooling back. The damage to the documentary record of Virginia Indian identity would take decades to assess and has still not been fully repaired.

### What the Sorrells Victory Did — and Did Not — Accomplish

**What it accomplished:**

1. Atha Sorrells married the man she wanted to marry and raised a family.
2. The ruling established, in Rockbridge County's own court record, that the Clark family tree documented white and Indian ancestry with no documented African ancestry — and that this was sufficient to defeat a racial classification challenge.
3. The case file became a **documentary asset** — the family tree exhibit, the War of 1812 service records, the land warrants, the 1876 precedent — that sat in the Rockbridge Circuit Court files and could be retrieved.

**What it did NOT accomplish:**

1. It did not change the Racial Integrity Act.
2. It did not restore any previously reclassified birth, marriage, or death certificate.
3. It did not establish statewide precedent. Its force was limited to Rockbridge County and to the specific families named in the evidence.
4. It did not protect other families in the same community from ongoing Plecker enforcement.

The win was real and it was narrow. Atha Sorrells had good lawyers, documented ancestry going back 130 years, and a judge who was willing to apply the law rather than follow Plecker's preferred outcome. Not everyone in the community had all three.

### The 1951 Connection

Twenty-six years after Atha's victory, the same evidence package — the Clark family tree, the Sorrells precedent, the documented history of Indian identity in the Rockbridge community — was used again.

In 1951, **Joseph Anderson Clark** (then 82 years old) was involved in an effort to overturn the LDS priesthood exclusion that had blocked the Clark men from full church participation since the 1920s. The LDS policy excluded men of African descent from holding the priesthood; Plecker's reclassification of the Clarks as "colored" had caught them in this exclusion even though their actual ancestry was Indian and European. **Two LDS genealogical missionaries from Utah traveled to Rockbridge County in 1951** and assembled counter-evidence that drew directly on the 1925 Sorrells ruling as legal precedent.

Branch President Eugene Larsen subsequently ordained Claude Clark, Alvin Coleman, and Garvis Wheeler to the priesthood. Claude Clark became the first full-time missionary called from the Waynesboro Virginia ward in 1957.

The direct chain: Atha won the court case using the Clark family tree → the ruling and the evidence sat in the courthouse files for 26 years → the 1951 missionaries used the same evidence to break the LDS exclusion → the Clark men were ordained.

## Part 7 — The Legal and Historical Context

### Where the Case Sits in the Broader Record

Atha Sorrells's case is well-documented in the academic and archival literature on the Racial Integrity Act. It appears in:

- **Encyclopedia Virginia** — primary documents section, with the November 14, 1924 petition for mandamus reproduced
- **The Native Heritage Project** (2012) — "Atha Sorrells, the Woman Who Had the Audacity…"
- **The Library of Virginia's** documentation of the Racial Integrity Act era (uncommonwealth.lva.virginia.gov)
- **Jennifer Marie Huff's 2012 JMU master's thesis** — *A Question of Indian Identity in the Plecker Era: The Monacan Indian Nation in the Twentieth Century* — which used Plecker's correspondence to A.T. Shields directly, i.e., the exact file that contains the May 9, 1925 letter declining to appeal

It is not a famous case. It is not taught in law schools. It did not make it into the general cultural narrative about the Racial Integrity Act the way that *Loving v. Virginia* did. This is partly because it did not go to an appellate court and establish formal precedent, and partly because the Irish Creek community was rural, poor, and not politically connected in ways that would have generated wide coverage.

The Richmond Times-Dispatch covered it in November 1924 and urged appeal — the only newspaper that appears to have covered it at the time.

### The Estabrook and McDougle Study — The "Scientific" Context

One additional piece of context for the period: in **1926**, one year after the Sorrells verdict, researchers **Arthur H. Estabrook** (Eugenics Record Office, Cold Spring Harbor, New York) and **Ivan E. McDougle** (Goucher College sociologist) published *Mongrel Virginians: The Win Tribe*. The book studied the Amherst / Rockbridge / Nelson tri-racial community — the same families as in the Sorrells case — but disguised the location and surnames behind the label "WIN" (White-Indian-Negro). The book claimed the community was the product of degenerate interracial mixing and was morally and intellectually inferior.

Plecker cited this book for the rest of his career as "proof" that there were no real Indians left in central Virginia. When a Monacan family complained about a reclassified birth certificate, Plecker cited Estabrook and McDougle.

The book was funded by the Carnegie Foundation and the Eugenics Record Office. It was reviewed in *American Journal of Psychiatry* by Abraham Myerson, who called it "a really absurd and useless book." Modern scholarship has thoroughly discredited it. The current consensus among Virginia historians is that it was pseudo-science deployed as state policy — the intellectual cover for what Plecker was doing administratively.

The families in *Mongrel Virginians* were the same families in the Sorrells case. The book came out one year after Atha won in court. It is likely not a coincidence that Estabrook and McDougle were in that community doing "research" in the same period when Atha's case was in the courts.

**Source rating: 5** for the book's existence and content (HathiTrust full text available); **4** for Plecker's use of it (peer-reviewed scholarship).

## Part 8 — How Brady Is Related to Atha Sorrells

### The Genealogical Connection

The full family tree requires pulling the original Sorrells case file from the Library of Virginia to verify every generational link — and that work has not yet been done. What we can establish from the documented record:

**The shared ancestor** is **Joe Clark Sr., born 1797, Rockbridge County, Virginia.** He is the patriarch named at the base of the family tree submitted as evidence in the Sorrells case. He is documented in that case file. He is also documented in our line — the Sorrells case treats him as the founding anchor of the entire Irish Creek Clark community.

**Atha Sorrells's line** runs through Joe Clark Sr.:
> Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797) → Joe Clark Jr. + Julia (Sorrells) Clark [Atha's maternal great-grandparents] → Atha's grandparents → Atha's parents (father: William Sorrells) → **Atha Sorrells (b. 1904)**

This chain is documented in the Sorrells case file's family tree exhibit and is corroborated by the Native Heritage Project's 2012 article and the Encyclopedia Virginia primary document collection.

### What Degree of Cousin Does This Make?

Working through the generational chains:

- Joe Clark Sr. (b. 1797) is the common ancestor
- **Atha Sorrells** is approximately Joe Clark Sr.'s **great-great-granddaughter** (born 1904, four generations below a man born in 1797, at roughly 25-year intervals)
- **Joseph Anderson Clark** (b. 1869) is approximately Joe Clark Sr.'s **great-grandchild** — one generation closer to the patriarch than Atha

This places JAC as Atha's **first cousin once removed** — they share Joe Clark Jr. as a common ancestor (JAC as grandchild of Joe Clark Sr. through one line; Atha as great-grandchild through another). The exact designation requires the full family tree to confirm, and the "once removed" relationship may vary depending on which specific intermediate generations are identified in the case file.

Brady is JAC's great-great-great-grandson (five generations below JAC). Working out from that:

| Relationship to JAC | Approximate Relationship to Brady |
|---|---|
| JAC's first cousin once removed (Atha) | Brady's 4th cousin (3x removed) |
| JAC himself | Brady's great-great-great-grandfather |

In plain language: **Atha Sorrells is Brady's distant cousin — approximately fourth cousins several times removed — through the Joe Clark Sr. / Joe Clark Jr. family line of Rockbridge County, Virginia.**

**The practical answer for family purposes:** Atha Sorrells was JAC's Clark cousin. JAC is your great-great-great-grandfather. That makes Atha a cousin of your great-great-great-grandfather — which in plain family speech makes her your distant cousin from the same extended Clark family that defines your Monacan heritage connection.

## Part 9 — Why This Case Matters

Atha Sorrells was not a political activist. She was not trying to make history. She was 20 years old, pregnant, and trying to get a marriage license so she could marry the man she was going to have a baby with.

What she did — or more precisely, what she refused to accept — was the decision of one county clerk, acting on instructions from one bureaucrat in Richmond, that she was not who she knew herself to be.

She retained lawyers. She filed a petition. She submitted 130 years of family history as evidence. She sat in a courtroom while Walter Plecker himself — the most powerful racial administrator in Virginia, the man who had personally drafted the law that was being used against her — testified against her. She won.

The things that made the win possible were entirely practical: good lawyers, a documented family tree going back to a man born in 1797, a 48-year-old precedent from the same courthouse, and a judge who applied the evidence rather than the politics. None of those things were heroic in the dramatic sense. They were the results of a family that had spent generations establishing its documentary record — sometimes inadvertently, sometimes deliberately — and that had, in 1876 and again in 1924–25, the presence of mind to bring that record to court.

**The lesson is not that one person defeated the system.** Plecker's system continued after Atha won. The reclassifications continued. The Racial Integrity Act stood until 1967 on its anti-miscegenation provisions and 1975 on the remainder. Thousands of other families were trapped in it without the benefit of Atha's lawyers or Atha's family tree or Atha's judge.

The lesson is narrower and more useful: **the documentary record itself is the weapon against bureaucratic erasure.** James Clark built a piece of that record in 1876. Atha Sorrells used it in 1925. Two LDS missionaries from Utah used it in 1951. We are using it now. The work is not finished. The Library of Virginia has the case file. It is pullable. The depositions may contain names of members of our family that have never been formally identified. The family tree exhibit — a physical chart submitted to a Rockbridge County court in 1924 — is sitting in an archive box 45 minutes from Buena Vista.

That is the inheritance. **Atha Sorrells preserved it by fighting.** The least we can do is go get it.

## Closing

Walter Plecker went to a Rockbridge County courtroom in January 1925 and testified against a 20-year-old woman from Irish Creek who was trying to get a marriage license.

He lost.

He went back to Richmond and told his clerk not to appeal, because he was afraid of what would happen if the case went further. He called the Irish Creek community "the Rockbridge tribe" in a private letter — acknowledging, in writing he thought would stay private, that the community he had been trying to erase for twelve years was still there, still organized, and still capable of winning in court when the evidence was on the table.

**Atha Sorrells married Robert Painter on May 31, 1925.** She died in 1979. Her case file sits in a box at the Library of Virginia. The family tree her attorneys submitted — with Joe Clark Sr. at the bottom, her great-grandparents Joe Clark Jr. and Julia Sorrells Clark in the middle, and 130 years of documented white and Indian ancestry running up to her name — is sitting in that box.

It is the same family tree that connects to Joseph Anderson Clark, who is your great-great-great-grandfather.

It is the same family tree that the 1951 LDS missionaries used to prove that the Clark men were not "colored" and were entitled to the priesthood.

It is the same family tree that would support a modern Monacan citizenship application if the documentary chain is fully built out.

One woman fought for it in 1924–25. She won. The record she created is still there. It is still ours.
