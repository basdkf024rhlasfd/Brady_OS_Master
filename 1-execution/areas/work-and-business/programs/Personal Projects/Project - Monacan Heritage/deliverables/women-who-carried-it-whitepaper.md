# The Women Who Carried It
## The Matriarchs of the Line — What the Record Shows When You Read It for the Women

**Brady Smallwood — Family Whitepaper, Companion Document**
**Compiled August 2026 | Source-rated 1–5 (5 = primary archive / peer-reviewed; 1 = anecdotal)**

---

## The premise

Records follow men. Land deeds, tax rolls, court dockets, military files — the paper trail of early Virginia is a trail of men's names, because the law made it so: a married woman's legal existence merged into her husband's. Every whitepaper in this project so far has, faithfully following that record, been mostly about men — Joe Clark Sr., James Clark the mandamus winner, Peter Mason, Joseph Anderson Clark, and now the Smallwood patriline.

But read the same record sideways and a different pattern appears. At nearly every point where this family's story bent — where the line could have broken, where the identity could have dissolved, where the name could have vanished — **the person standing at the hinge is a woman.** The court case that beat Walter Plecker was brought by a twenty-year-old woman. The correction that rewrote this project's own family tree was the recovery of a forgotten first wife. The Smallwood name itself survived the 1950s because a widow kept it on her son. The best genealogy anyone in the family ever compiled was compiled by a woman.

This paper reads the record for the women. It makes no claims beyond the sources — several of these women left barely three dates behind — but it assembles what is known, says clearly what is not, and shows that the known part is more than anyone has ever put in one place.

---

## 1. The slot that held three women

Start with this project's own hardest-won lesson, because it is fundamentally a story about how women disappear from records — and how they can be recovered.

When the master whitepaper was first drafted in May 2026, Brady's 6x-great-grandmother — the wife of Nelson Harrison Clark (1793–1871), mother of Preston Harrison Clark — was identified as **Frances Elizabeth Mason**, the woman on the Monacan Rolls, daughter of the Pedlar Creek Mason family, granddaughter of Branhams. It was an attractive identification: it put a documented Monacan woman directly in Brady's bloodline.

It was wrong, and the project caught it within a day *(research file 10, 2026-05-06; 4–5)*. The FamilySearch record shows Frances married Nelson in 1840 — nineteen years *after* Preston was born. Preston and four siblings are explicitly tagged as her step-children. Frances was Nelson's **second** wife. Brady's actual ancestor was the first wife, who at that moment was a blank: name unknown, dates unknown, existence inferred only from her children.

For about a day, Brady's 6x-great-grandmother was literally "UNKNOWN FIRST WIFE" — which is precisely how millions of women stand in millions of family trees forever.

Then the project did the work, and she came back *(research file 13, 2026-05-07; 4)*. She was **Edith Wane**.

### Edith Wane (1798–1835)

What the record holds: born 1798 in Rockbridge County. Married Nelson Harrison Clark on **6 July 1819** in Rockbridge — the marriage record survives ("Edy Wane," *Virginia Marriages 1785–1940*). Five documented children: Andrew Jackson (1817), Sarah J (1820), **Preston Harrison (1821) — Brady's line**, Mary Jane (1831), Margaret (1835). Died 1835, at thirty-seven — **the same year her last daughter was born.** The record does not state that she died from that childbirth; the two dates in one row say what they say, and 1830s Virginia obstetrics fills in the probabilities. Sixteen sources are attached to her FamilySearch profile. *(File 13; 4.)*

Sixteen sources — and yet she was still forgotten, for a while, in her own descendant's tree, displaced by the second wife whose name the community remembered better. Edith carried five children, died young with an infant, and came within one careless merge of permanent erasure. She is in this paper first because recovering her is the single best thing this project has done for any woman in the line.

### Sally Wane of Aylesford, Kent (b. 24 September 1784)

Edith's mother — and the only woman in the direct line with a documented English birthplace: **Aylesford, Kent**, christened in the parish record system (*England Births and Christenings, 1538–1975*), daughter of Nicolas Wane (b. 1754) and Martha Burren (b. 1753), one of at least seven children. Sometime between 1784 and 1798 she crossed the Atlantic and appears next as a mother in Rockbridge County, Virginia. *(File 13; 4.)*

Sit with the arithmetic, which the research file records without comment: Sally was born in 1784; her daughter Edith was born in 1798. **Fourteen years.** And Edith's father, Harrison Wane (b. 1773), is a near-total blank — zero sources, no birthplace, no parents, no other children. The file names him the most likely candidate for the paternal line's Native entry point precisely because such total absence fits Virginia's marginal-community pattern. This paper adds only the observation the arithmetic forces: whatever happened around Edith's birth — a very young mother, or an error in one of the two birth years, or a record conflation not yet untangled — **the answer runs through Sally**, and the Aylesford parish registers that could resolve her birth year are digitized and one research session away. A girl christened in Kent, standing at the exact point where the family's Native-ancestry question goes dark: she may be the most consequential unexamined woman in the tree. *(Analysis; the 14-year gap flagged here as an open problem, 3.)*

### Frances Elizabeth Mason (b. 1820) — the step-matriarch

The correction removed Frances from Brady's bloodline. It should not remove her from the family's story, and here is why.

Frances was **on the Monacan Rolls as Indian** — her own documented identity. Her mother was Polly Ann Branham (1792–1828); her great-grandfather Benjamin Branham (1728–1795) sits in the documented Bear Mountain lineage; Branhams led the Monacan Nation into federal recognition in 2018. Her father Jesse Mason appears at age 80 *living in Nelson Harrison Clark's household* in the 1860 census — the same enumeration that recorded Nelson as Mulatto. *(Files 10, 12, ANALYSIS; 4.)*

From 1840 until Nelson's death in 1871, Frances was the matriarch of the household in which Brady's ancestor Preston had grown up and around which his family orbited. She raised nine biological children whose marriages wove the Clarks into Beverly, Holmes, and Johns families — the core Bear Mountain surnames, all later named on Plecker's 1943 list. In other words: **Frances is not Brady's blood, but she is a large part of why the Clark family's world was the Monacan world.** Kinship in that community — as the Sorrells case itself demonstrated — was a web of marriage and household, not only a chain of descent. Frances sat at the center of the web for thirty years. The bloodline correction and her importance are both true at once. *(Synthesis of files 10 and 12; 4.)*

## 2. Mary Susan Clark (1881–1954) — the woman the state couldn't make up its mind about

No individual in the entire project embodies the Plecker-era documentary war more completely than Brady's 3x-great-grandmother — not in a courtroom, but in her own paperwork, across four decades of ordinary life in the same place.

The record, in her own documents' order *(ANALYSIS §§1.3, 2.1, 3.3; census transcriptions preserved in the DeYoung write-up; 4)*:

- **1906, Amherst County marriage register:** Joseph Anderson Clark marries Mary Susan Clark. The register classifies the couple **"colored."**
- **1920, federal census, South River, Rockbridge:** Mary S. Clark, wife, age 38 — **"White."** Household: Joseph (50), sons Walter (21), **James W. (16 — Brady's 2x-great-grandfather)**, Levi, Elijah, Mark, Paul, daughter Francis.
- **1930, federal census, same place:** wife of Joseph A. Clark (60) — **"White."** Children at home: Fannie (23), Levi (19), Elijah (17), Mark (15), Paul (12), Silas (8), Beulah (5).
- **1940, federal census, Road 603, South River:** **"White."** Highest education recorded: **third grade.**

Same woman. Same husband. Same stretch of the South River. In 1906 the state wrote her down as colored; by 1920 the state wrote her down as white; in 1943 the state — in the person of Walter Plecker — circulated a list demanding that Rockbridge Clarks be re-reclassified back. **Mary Susan Clark did not change. Virginia's paperwork changed around her, three times, in her adult lifetime.** In 1951, it was the rediscovery of her 1906 "colored" marriage record by an LDS genealogist that detonated the priesthood case around her husband's family — the paperwork of her wedding day reached forty-five years into the future to put her descendants' church standing on trial. *(4–5 across the cited files.)*

And underneath the classification war, the life the same records sketch: married at about twenty-five, at least nine children across nearly three decades of childbearing, a third-grade education, a farm household on a numbered road — and she outlived the entire Plecker era. Plecker retired in 1946; Mary Susan died in 1954, at some seventy-three years old, having spent her whole married life as the untitled center of a ten-person household that the Commonwealth of Virginia could label but never actually describe.

Her own maternal line runs back through **Mary Elizabeth Sorrells (1840–1902)** — the Sorrells web again *(ANALYSIS §1.1; sheet data cross-checked, 3–4)* — which makes Mary Susan the bridge through which the Sorrells kinship, the one Atha's court case defended, flows into Brady's direct line.

## 3. Atha Sorrells (1904–1979) — the one who sued

Atha has her own whitepaper in this project, and this paper will not retell it. She stands in this lineup for one sentence of context the family should never lose: **when this community finally beat Walter Plecker in a courtroom — the man who had the entire apparatus of the Commonwealth behind him — the plaintiff was a twenty-year-old pregnant woman from the same Clark-Sorrells kinship as Brady's ancestors, who walked into the Rockbridge County courthouse in 1924 and refused to accept what the clerk had written about her.** Judge Holt ruled for her in January 1925; Plecker himself had testified against her and lost; his private letter afterward warned that the ruling would "embolden the Rockbridge tribe." It did. *(Files 02, 08, and the Atha whitepaper; 5 — Library of Virginia case file 1140744/1164679.)*

Among the men in this project's papers are patriarchs, converts, and litigants. The outright courtroom victory over the architect of the Racial Integrity Act belongs to a woman.

## 4. The widows' bridge, 1948–1955 — Ethel, Ida, and Lutie

The companion Smallwood paper documents the fracture decade from the men's side: grandmother, mother, and son dead within seven years. Read it now from the women's side, because the women are how the family got across.

**Ida Florence Johnson Smallwood (1869–1948)** — Hamilton's wife, forty-plus years married, died at seventy-nine having outlived her husband by a decade. **Ethel May Knicely Smallwood (1904–1949)** — the delta of the entire Palatine current (see companion paper), dead at forty-five, a year before her grandson Gene was born; with her died the living connection to the German Valley families, which is very likely why that whole stream vanished from the family's told story. *(Sheet data, 2.)*

And then the one the family remembers — the woman in the master whitepaper's own subtitle:

### Lutie Fannie Clark (1926–2001)

The record as held: born 1926 — granddaughter of Joseph Anderson and Mary Susan Clark through their son James William Clark (1904–1980) *(ANALYSIS §1.1; 3–4)*. Married Nelson Roudnal Smallwood (1925–1955). Mother of Dennis Eugene Smallwood, born 1950. **Widowed in 1955, at about twenty-nine, with a five-year-old.** Remarried — her later children carry the surnames Simmons, Weyerman, Pugh, Lee *(ANALYSIS §1.5; 2–3)*. Died 2001, having lived long enough to know her great-grandchildren.

Every stream this project has mapped converges in what Lutie did next, and the record states it with brutal economy: she was a Clark — carrying the Monacan-descended, Plecker-listed, court-tested bloodline of Irish Creek — raising a Smallwood boy alone, inside remarriage households full of other men's names. **The Smallwood surname survived to reach Brady and his children because Lutie Fannie Clark kept it on her son.** The blood-history came through her; the name came through her keeping; she is the single person in whom this project's every thread — Clark and Smallwood, Monacan and English and Palatine — is physically one woman holding one child. The master whitepaper already knew it when it addressed itself to "the children of Gene Smallwood, **grandchildren of Lutie Clark**." This paper just says out loud what that subtitle was already saying.

She died in 2001. Brady knew her. Her stories — of Nelson, of the remarriage years, of what her grandfather Joseph Anderson Clark's house was like — either live in the memories of people alive right now, or nowhere. See the research plan.

## 5. The women who wrote it down

One more pattern, running right up to the present: in this family, **the record-keeping itself has largely been women's work.**

The deepest genealogy anyone in the family produced before this project — a 210,000-character narrative plus a five-sheet pedigree out to fourteen generations, with clean census transcriptions this project still relies on — was compiled by **Marcene DeYoung** in 2022 *(preserved in full at `research/source/deyoung-research/`)*. This project's May 2026 analysis of her file found real gaps on the Native-ancestry side, and documented them plainly. It also found, and this paper repeats, that her work was serious, largely accurate, and generous — including her honest choice to preserve the Monacan Nation reference material even without connecting it to the family. The family's two genealogies disagree about emphasis; they agree about almost every name. Both should be said in the same breath.

Behind her, the pattern recurs: the 1978 Read family history in Marcene's files was a family-written memoir; the oral tradition the master whitepaper set out to test — "the brown people," the Cherokee story, the things the grandmothers said — was, by its nature, carried in kitchens more than in courthouses. The courthouse record kept the men. The kitchens kept the meaning. This project exists because both survived.

## 6. What is still open — the women's research plan

1. **Record Lutie's story while it can still be recorded.** Interview Gene's half-siblings (Simmons, Weyerman, Pugh, Lee) and any of Lutie's surviving kin — about Nelson, about the widow years, about her Clark grandparents. **The only expiring item in this entire project.** (Shared with the Smallwood paper's plan; listed first in both because it belongs first in both.)
2. **Resolve Sally Wane's arithmetic** — pull the Aylesford, Kent parish registers (digitized) for her christening and her siblings'; reconcile the 14-year gap to Edith's birth; then hunt Harrison Wane in Rockbridge records 1790s–1810s. The paternal Native-ancestry entry point runs through this couple.
3. **Find Edith Wane's grave and death record** (Rockbridge or Amherst, 1835) — confirm the death-in-childbirth inference or replace it with fact.
4. **Pull Mary Susan Clark's death record (1954)** and any obituary — the last documentary word on the woman the state kept relabeling, and a check on what race the Commonwealth assigned her at the end.
5. **Identify Nelson Harrison Clark's household composition in the 1820/1830 censuses** — the years Edith was alive and raising Preston — the closest thing to a document of her married life that exists.
6. **The unnamed women of the Palatine stream** — Anna Barbara Maurer's impossible 1745–1855 lifespan and the Limpens misattachment (companion paper §4) are both, notably, women's records broken in exactly the way women's records break: through inattention. Fixing them on the public tree is small, and it is the same act this whole paper is — putting the women back.
