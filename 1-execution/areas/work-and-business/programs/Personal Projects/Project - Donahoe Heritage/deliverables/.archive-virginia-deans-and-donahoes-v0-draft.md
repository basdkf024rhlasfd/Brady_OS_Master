## Executive Overview

Susan and Paulette descend, through their mother Mary Ellen Donahoe, from a line of Eastern Shore Virginia colonial families that goes back to the founding generation of English Virginia and Maryland, with documented immigrants on the *Bona Nova* (1624/5), the *Primrose* (1635), the *Rose* (1637), and a 1655 Eastern Shore arrival from Hampshire, England. Through their father Delbert Dean they descend from a New England line that reaches Connecticut by 1693 and a Famine-Irish line from County Cork and Pembrokeshire, Wales.

Their mother's line carries:

- A documented Revolutionary War officer — **Capt William H. Parker (~1717–1784)** of Accomack County, Virginia — who was sworn in as Captain of militia 30 July 1777 and administered the oath of allegiance to his company on 26 August 1777. Susan and Paulette's 6th great-grandfather.
- A documented Eastern Shore colonial-immigrant patriarch — **Richard Kellam II of Occahannock (1618–1703)** — who arrived from Aslackby, Lincolnshire on the *Primrose* in 1635 at age 16, bought a neck of land directly from "King Tabutiabum of Nandau" in 1647, and whose 1665 holdings were on the Accomack County tithables list with six tithes. Their 8th great-grandfather.
- A documented Eastern Shore Maryland patriarch — **Col William Coulbourne (1630–1689)** — who patented Pomfret Plantation at Annemessex on the lower Eastern Shore in 1679, sympathized with the Quakers, hosted George Fox at his house, and headed the 1687 Maryland commission that drew up the treaty with the Nanticoke Indians. Their 9th great-grandfather.
- A documented Welsh-origin Northampton County immigrant — **John Floyd I "of Hog Island" (~1640–1687)** — who arrived as one of twenty headrights of John Wilcocks on 30 July 1660 and was patented 2,200 acres for "Hogg Island alias Shooting Beach" in 1681. Their 7th great-grandfather.
- A documented family-tradition Native ancestor — **Mary Bays / Baze (died likely late 1775)** of Goochland County, Virginia — recorded in family memory as a Monacan Indian woman from the village of Mowhemencho on the falls of the James River near present-day Richmond. Their 7th great-grandmother. The tradition is plausible — Mowhemencho was a documented Monacan town on Captain John Smith's 1612 map, and the destruction described in the family Bible matches the colonial record of the village's overrunning around 1670 — but no primary source for her tribal status survives. The claim is family tradition and is presented in this paper as such.
- A documented Great Migration Pilgrim ancestor — **Anthony Fisher Sr. (1591–1671)** — who arrived at Boston aboard the ship *Rose* from Yarmouth on 26 June 1637 and signed the Dedham Covenant. Their 8th great-grandfather. The Pilgrim-era inheritance enters the line not through the Dean surname (which terminates in the surviving record at a 1693-born Connecticut individual whose parents are not linked) but through the Fisher line on the Dean side — Susan and Paulette's paternal grandmother Editha Read's mother-in-law's people.

Their father's line also carries a Famine-era Irish Catholic inheritance from the 1850s, with documented townlands of origin in **Ballincolly** and **Durrus, County Cork**, plus a Welsh-Irish surname (Arundel) that entered the family from **Pembrokeshire, Wales**.

The work that follows traces these threads in detail, sources every nontrivial claim, and is honest about the gaps and uncertainties. The 290-year continuity of family ground at Onley, Pennyville, and Hollybrook in Accomack County — from Richard Kellam II's 1665 tithables entry through the loss of the Bull family farm in the 1946–1955 window — is treated as the spine of the maternal narrative.

## Chapter 1. The Anchor — Mary Ellen Donahoe

Susan Caylor Dean (born to Mary Ellen and Del Dean) and Paulette Dean (her sister) were both born to **Mary Ellen Donahoe**, who lived a long American life across two of the country's most distinct regional cultures: the Eastern Shore of Virginia, where she was born and grew up, and the Piedmont Virginia tobacco-and-textile city of Danville, where she lived out her marriage and her later years.

She was born **9 April 1932 in Accomac, Accomack County, Virginia**, on the Bull family ground where her mother's people had lived continuously since at least 1665. She died **5 May 2023 at the Piney Forest Health and Rehab in Virginia**, and is buried in Danville. She lived ninety-one years.

Her parents were **Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe (1896–1979)**, born in Ripley, Jackson County, West Virginia, and **Fannye Rogers "Fannie" Bull (1906–1982)**, born in Onley, Accomack County, Virginia. They married 31 July 1926 in Pocomoke City, Worcester County, Maryland — just across the Eastern Shore line — and Mary Ellen was their second daughter. Her older siblings included **Joel Caylor Donahoe (1927–2009)** and **William "Billy" Rollyson Donahoe (1935–1995)**, both of whom were also born in Onley, Accomack County. The middle names "Caylor" and "Rollyson" both came from the Donahoe paternal-grandmother and paternal-paternal sides — Lucinda Caylor (1845–1881), Stephen Alexander Donahoe's wife's mother, gave the family its "Caylor"; the Rollyson surname runs through Leona Ellen Rollyson (1871–1942), Paul's mother, and is a West Virginia surname concentrated in Roane and Calhoun counties.

Mary Ellen's marriage to Delbert "Del" Allen Dean — a Michigan-born railroad and rail-yard man whose family roots ran to Connecticut and County Cork — moved her permanently off the Eastern Shore. Del was born **24 August 1930 in South Lyon, Oakland County, Michigan**. He died **18 January 1996 in Danville, Virginia**, and is buried there. Their marriage produced Susan and Paulette and centered the family in Danville, where both daughters grew up.

The full pedigree below works back from Mary Ellen and Del through both lines, anchored on a copy of Brady Smallwood's RootsMagic-synced FamilySearch tree exported 6 May 2026 (86,877 lines), with verifications run live against FamilySearch in May 2026.

## Chapter 2. The Bull-Kellam-Rogers Eastern Shore Continuum

The most striking structural feature of Susan and Paulette's maternal-side ancestry is its concentration: through Mary Ellen's mother Fannye Rogers Bull, the line reaches back across more than three centuries on a single piece of Eastern Shore Virginia ground in the southern half of Accomack County. The relevant places — **Onancock, Onley, Pennyville, Hollybrook, Melfa, Craddockville, Cashville, Hacks Neck, Chesconessex** — sit within a six-mile radius of one another along the railroad corridor that bisects the lower Shore. Multiple Brady-line surnames intermarried within that footprint over six or seven generations.

### Mary Ellen's mother and grandmother

**Fannye Rogers "Fannie" Bull (1906–1982)** was born 24 June 1906 at Onley. Her parents — Mary Ellen's maternal grandparents — were **Joseph Saples Bull (1850–1923)** of Pennyville and **Mary Eliza Rogers (1878–1946)** of Onancock. They married 18 March 1896. Joseph Saples Bull is buried at the Bull family burying ground; Mary Eliza Rogers was buried in 1946 at Onley. Joseph's father was **Edward S Bull (1820–1856)** of Accomack, who married **Susan Elizabeth Jacob Kellam (1825–1900)** on 26 December 1843 in Accomack. Edward died early — at thirty-six — leaving Joseph Saples a child of six. The Bull-Kellam intermarriage at this point is the structural one: Edward's wife brought into the family the Kellam surname, which at this point in the Eastern Shore record had already been continuously documented for nearly two centuries.

The Kellam line traces back, through Susan Elizabeth Jacob Kellam's father William Samuel Kellam (1817–1898), eventually to **Richard Kellam II "of Occahannock"** — about whom more in the next chapter.

### The Bull line (maternal-paternal)

The Bull line carries back through Edward S Bull's father **Thomas Southy Bull (1788–1860)**, who married Rachel Floyd (1792–1823) in Accomack. Thomas's father was **Elijah "Eli" Bull (born about 1748, Accomack)**, who married Nancy Fitzgerald in Accomack on 21 December 1785. Elijah was of military age during the Revolutionary War; no documented service has surfaced in surviving rosters, but his presence on the line at the right age and the right county is recorded.

Above Elijah Bull, the surviving FamilySearch tree does not link parents — the colonial Accomack patentee Bull ancestor who would have brought the surname into the county is not documented in the present collaborative tree. The Bull surname does appear in pre-1700 Eastern Shore records, and a deep-research pull of Accomack land patents would likely surface him, but as of v1 of this paper that work has not been done.

### The Rogers line (maternal-maternal)

The Rogers line carries back through Mary Eliza Rogers's father **William Frances Rogers (1847–1895)**, born and died in Accomack County, buried at Mt. Holly Cemetery, Onancock. His father was **John K. Rodgers (1823–1879)** of Cashville, who married Eliza Evins Boggs (1813–1880). Eliza Evins Boggs's father was **Francis Boggs III (1775–1823)**, who married Elizabeth D. Underhill; Francis III's grandfather was **Francis Boggs (1707–1782)** of Accomack, who married Anne Hook. Boggs is one of the longer-running Eastern Shore surnames; the family has its own colonial pedigree on the Shore back to the 1660s.

The intermarriage of Boggs into Rogers in the early-1800s generation is itself the entry point for the **Coulbourne and Parker** lines covered in Chapter 4 — Eliza Evins Boggs is a great-great-granddaughter of Frances Colburne (daughter of Robert Coulburne, son of Col William Coulbourne), through Frances's daughter Ruth Parker (daughter of Capt William H. Parker, the documented Revolutionary War officer in Susan and Paulette's line).

### The Floyd line (maternal-paternal-maternal)

The Floyd line enters through Edward S Bull's mother Rachel Floyd, who carries the surname back to **Berry Floyd (1714–1771)**, **Matthew Floyd Sr (~1684–1752)** of Northampton, and the Welsh-origin colonial immigrant **John Floyd I "of Hog Island"** (about whom more in Chapter 4). The Floyd line is sourced cleanly back to John Floyd I and his arrival; above John Floyd I, the FamilySearch profile carries an explicit "questionable parents" warning flagging the further-back graft as unverified, and that further-back claim is therefore not relied on in this paper.

### The Garrison line

Edward S Bull's wife Susan Elizabeth Jacob Kellam carried, through her grandmother Rachel Garrison, the **Garrison** surname — and through Rachel Garrison's mother **Rose Kellam (1650–1717)**, the bridge directly back to **Richard Kellam II of Occahannock**, the 1635 immigrant. The Kellam-Garrison-Floyd-Bull intermarriages over multiple generations are the consanguinity pattern that genealogists call "pedigree collapse" — the same families circulating around one another in a small geographic area for a long time. It is typical of Eastern Shore Virginia and reflects the fact that the local pool of marriageable Anglo families was small and stable.

### What ties this all together

Onley and Pennyville and Melfa and Hollybrook are not merely the places where Brady's mother's people happened to live. They are the places where a single mid-1660s land claim — the 1665 Accomack tithables-list entry showing **Richard Kellam II ("Richard Kellum") with six tithes** — gradually generated, across nearly three hundred years of intermarriage and inheritance, the Bull-Kellam-Rogers-Boggs-Floyd-Garrison ground that Mary Ellen's mother Fannye was born on in 1906. The land was lost in the 1946–1955 window — that story is in Chapter 6.

## Chapter 3. The Native Ancestor — Mary Bays / Baze of Goochland County

The most carefully-tended claim in Susan and Paulette's family memory is that one of their ancestors was a Native woman. The claim survives in an old transcribed form on a curated-genealogy website (genealogytrails.com), in three notes on FamilySearch, in a single image in the FamilySearch Memories tab attached to her profile, and — the original source — in family memory recorded in a Bible kept by one of her descendants in Tennessee. The woman herself is real and well-documented for the marriage and burial sides of her life. Her tribal identification is family tradition and is not verifiable in primary records.

The work to identify her required correcting a longstanding identity conflation in the FamilySearch tree.

### The conflation

For some years, the FamilySearch profile (LRKY-41Q) attached to Brady's line as the wife of John Woodrum II of Goochland County conflated two different colonial Virginia women named Mary:

1. **Mary Dorcas Kimbrough**, daughter of John Kimburrow Jr., christened 30 January 1702/3 at St. Peter's Parish, New Kent County, Virginia. This Mary married a Wilkins and died in 1747. She is **not** Brady's ancestor.
2. **Mary Bays / Baze** (also recorded as Bayse, Bayes), who married **John Woodrum** at St. James Northam Parish, Goochland County, Virginia, on **15 November 1761**. This Mary is Brady's actual 7th great-grandmother.

The conflation appears to have been driven by a desire on the part of some Woodrum descendants to assign the 1703 St. Peter's christening to their ancestor (which would have given her a documented colonial-VA birth record) and to anchor her with the surname "Kimburrow" (which appears nowhere in the marriage register of her actual marriage). The 1761 Goochland register names her — and the 1764–1774 baptism records of her six surviving children name her — as **Bays / Baze / Bayse / Bayes**. Six baptism records over a decade are difficult to dismiss as coincidence. The current surviving consensus among careful researchers (WikiTree's Mary "Bays" Woodrum profile is one example) is that the Bays/Baze identification is correct and the Kimburrow identification is the conflation.

This paper therefore identifies her as **Mary Bays / Baze**, with the understanding that the surname "Kimburrow / Kimborough / Kimbrough" attached to her in some current FamilySearch material reflects the unresolved conflation.

### What is documented

The primary-source record is, in compressed form:

- **Marriage:** "BAZE, Mary, & John Woodrum, both in this parish Nov: 15 1761" — *The Douglas Register* (1928 published transcription of the parish register kept by Reverend William Douglas of St. James Northam Parish, Goochland County, Virginia), digitized at archive.org. The page numbering in published indices is bays-side male p. 6 and female p. 4.
- **Children's baptisms 1764–1774:** Six baptism entries in the same Douglas Register naming John Woodrum and Mary Bays/Baze (variant spellings) as parents. The children's birth orders and names are consistent with the family group recorded elsewhere in the FamilySearch tree.
- **John Woodrum's remarriage 3 January 1776 to Mary Barker** — recorded in the same Douglas Register. The proximate dating implies Mary Bays/Baze died sometime in the months between her last documented activity and John's remarriage; a probable death date of **late 1775**.

### What is family tradition

The Native-ancestry claim survives in a single textual chain, summarized in a FamilySearch note (user TracieLeighGross, last edited 19 February 2023) sourced to the Genealogy Trails website at `/ill/washington/woodrome.html`:

> "One of John's great grandsons, Dr. Jacob Woodrum of Tennessee, had a bible that indicated that John was born in 1702. It was also written in the same bible that Mary (Molly) was born in 1704 and that she was an Indian. All the males in her tribe (Monacan) had been killed by the settlers. The Indian Village was called 'Mowhemencho'. They occupied 10,000 acres of land close to present day Richmond on the south side of the James River in Virginia, Mary (Molly) later became John's wife."

The same note continues with reference to a memo dated 27 July 1983 by **Marjorie Fluor Moore** that contained a copy of John Woodrum II's will and the sale of his property by his daughter Alice on 21 June 1779. The memo and the Bible itself have not been located in surviving public deposits.

Two cautions about the claim:

- A figure described as "Dr. Jacob Woodrum of Tennessee," John Woodrum II's "great-grandson," appears to be conflated with Jacob E. Woodrum (1767–1842) of Wilson County, Tennessee — who was John Woodrum II's **grandson**, not great-grandson, and who was not a doctor. The "Dr." may have migrated from the byline of the Genealogy Trails state coordinator who hosts the page.
- The 1727 marriage date that appears in the genealogytrails note is unsupported in primary record. The Douglas Register's 1761 marriage date — between Mary Bays/Baze and John Woodrum — is the verifiable one. If the 1727 date is in the Bible, it is in conflict with the parish register and likely refers to a different person or a different event.

Despite these cautions, the geography of the claim — Mowhemencho on the south side of the James, near present-day Richmond — is real. Mowhemencho was a documented **Monacan town** that appeared on Captain John Smith's 1612 *Map of Virginia* as one of five named Monacan settlements. The Virginia Department of Historic Resources has placed roadside Marker O-3 ("Mowhemcho-Manakin Town") on the location, which today sits within the Manakin community in Goochland County. The colonial-era destruction described in the family Bible — that "all the males in her tribe had been killed by the settlers" — matches the documented overrunning of Mowhemencho around 1670, after which the surviving Monacan population dispersed westward toward what would become Bear Mountain in Amherst County. Mary Bays/Baze, born around 1737–1740 if her marriage in 1761 was at the typical age for the era, would have been a generation removed from the actual destruction, born in or near the diaspora. The family-tradition framing is consistent with the colonial record.

### The recommended citation

This paper presents Mary Bays/Baze as Susan and Paulette's 7th great-grandmother and notes the family tradition of her Monacan ancestry as the clean, single oral source it is — neither inflating it to primary-source-verified status nor dismissing it. The companion Bible, if it survives in private hands in Tennessee or has been deposited in a genealogical society, is the single most valuable next-step source for any future v2 of this paper. Susan and Paulette may know whether anyone in the family has a lead on it.

A side note: a current FamilySearch entry attaches a "Cherokee Guion Miller Roll number 9614" reference to Mary Bays/Baze. The Guion Miller Roll is a real federal record (Eastern Cherokee Applications under the Act of 28 June 1906), but the 9614 number is a fraud-pattern signature: the same number was attached to a now-deleted "Solomon John Rappahannock Kimbrough" entry that was part of a fictional ancestor cluster derived from Don Greene and Noel Swartz's *Shawnee Heritage* book series — denounced by the Cherokee Nation, the Shawnee chief, professional genealogists, and removed or merged from FamilySearch. The 9614 attribution should be discarded; the underlying Bible-tradition Monacan claim is independent of and unrelated to the Cherokee fraud cluster.

## Chapter 4. The Eastern Shore Colonial Immigrants

The arrival generations on the Eastern Shore are the deepest verified part of Susan and Paulette's ancestry. Four named ancestors arrived between 1624 and 1660 and patented land that descended through their families into the lines that produced Mary Ellen Donahoe.

### Nathaniel Floyd of the *Bona Nova* (Wales → Virginia, 1624/5)

The earliest documented immigrant in the line. Nathaniel Floyd was born around 1601 in Wales and arrived at Blaney's Plant, James City, on the ship *Bona Nova* before 4 February 1624/5. He patented 850 acres in Isle of Wight County on 20 November 1637. He died sometime before 1674, in Isle of Wight County. His Welsh origin is consistent with the surname's earliest form on the Eastern Shore — which appears in some Hog Island land documents as "Lloyd" rather than "Floyd."

A 1922 fire destroyed the original parents listed in current FamilySearch trees ("Sir John Theophelus Floyd" and "Lady Mary Veriar of Queen Elizabeth's Court") flag as bogus on the profile itself: the alleged parents reportedly married seven years after Nathaniel's birth, and the alleged father is shown with a son born sixteen years after the father's death. This is a Shawnee-Heritage-style aspirational-nobility graft. The walked-line in this paper terminates at Nathaniel Floyd himself, who is real and well-sourced. Whatever his actual Welsh parentage, it is not currently recoverable from the surviving record.

### Richard Kellam II "of Occahannock" (Lincolnshire → Virginia, 1635)

The keystone immigrant for the maternal line. Christened **30 August 1618 at St. James Parish, Aslackby, Lincolnshire, England**, Richard Kellam II departed Gravesend, England on the ship *Primrose* on 27 July 1635 — at age sixteen — under indenture to Nicholas Waddelowe, a Quaker farmer. He survived the Atlantic crossing and the indenture, and on **6 May 1647** he purchased a neck of land on the north side of Occahannock Creek called "Quinduit" by deed directly from **King Tabutiabum of Nandau** — a Native land transaction recorded 2 August 1649. He patented 300 acres for transporting six persons (including his future wife Sarah Ainsley) on 28 July 1649; over his lifetime he accumulated approximately 2,400 acres of patents on the Eastern Shore plus 500 acres of "Kellam's Folly" on the Pocomoke River in Somerset County, Maryland. The 1655 Act of Assembly designated his land as the Eastern Shore court, church, prison, and public-mart site at Hungars Creek divide — a remarkable institutional concentration of colonial functions on a single landholding. Tithables lists from 1662 through 1695 are recorded annually on his FamilySearch profile, including the 1665 "Richard Kellum 6 tithes" entry that anchors the 290-year continuity through to the 1950s. His will was signed **1 June 1703**. He died sometime between 1 June and 6 October 1703.

He has 69 sources on his FamilySearch profile — the most heavily-documented person in the entire pedigree.

A note on his record that the paper reproduces honestly: on 8 March 1654, Richard Kellam II was "committed for affronting justice" during a court suit brought by "Andyaman and other Indyans," reportedly saying — per the surviving order — that "if he had the Indyan at ye door he would cut his throat." Seven years after he had bought land directly from a Native king. Both records are real. The paper does not flatten them: the colonial Eastern Shore was a place where the legal forms of land purchase from Native polities existed alongside violent personal contempt for individual Native litigants.

### John Parker Sr. "of Mattapony" (Hampshire → Virginia, 1655)

Born **25 May 1634**, christened at Southampton, Hampshire, England. Came to Virginia by March 1655 — transported by his older brother George Parker — and patented 600 acres called "Mattapany" on the Eastern Shore in 1660. Married Amy Anderson, daughter of Garrett Anderson I. Died at Cashville, Accomack County, on 19 September 1695. Well documented in Whitelaw's *Virginia's Eastern Shore* and in the MILESFILES Eastern Shore genealogical compilation.

His grandson **Capt William H. Parker (~1717–1784)** is the line's documented Revolutionary War officer; the Parker chapter is in Chapter 5.

### Col William Coulbourne (London → Virginia → Maryland, 1652)

Born **19 January 1630, Westminster, City of Westminster, Greater London** (christened that same day at St Martin-in-the-Fields). His father, **Charles Coulbourne (1603–1662)**, also born in St Martin-in-the-Fields, immigrated to the Eastern Shore in his own right and died in Somerset County, Maryland.

William Coulbourne arrived in Northampton County, Virginia, by 25 March 1652, on which day he signed (with 116 others) the "Engagement tendered to ye Inhabitants of Northampton County" pledging fidelity to the Commonwealth of England without King or House of Lords — a striking republican gesture from a 22-year-old Eastern Shore newcomer. He patented 350 acres in Northampton on 4 March 1652/3 and another 350 in 1662/3. Between October 1663 and January 1663/4 he relocated south across the Maryland line to Annemessex on the lower Eastern Shore of Maryland. There he patented 1,400 acres in June 1679 named **"Pomfret"** between Jones' Creek and Coulbourne's Creek. The Coulbourne family owned Pomfret continuously through nine generations until a sale in 1921; the property was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984.

He served as Sheriff of Somerset twice (1673, 1675), held a Lord Proprietor's commission as a justice of the peace, rose through the militia from Lieutenant (1664) to Colonel of foot raised in Somerset and Dorchester counties (1681), and on **July 1687 was designated by the Lord Proprietor of Maryland to head the commission to draw up a treaty with the Nanticoke Indians** — the treaty was signed 11 August 1687. He took the loyalty oath to King William and Queen Mary on 28 November 1689 and died 21 January 1689 (the Old Style year ending in March creates a year discrepancy in some records).

A particular note on his religious life: on 12 April 1661 the Northampton court recorded an order of recantation against him for entertaining Quakers, but the side note in the order book reads, simply, "This refused to bee subscribed by Wm. Colborne." He was reportedly arrested with Henry White, Thomas Leatherbury, and Ambrose Dixon for giving succor to Quakers. Eleven years later — by then settled at Pomfret on the Maryland side — **George Fox, the founder of the Society of Friends, called on Coulbourne and held a service at his house**. The paper trail of his Quaker sympathy is documented in Torrence's *Old Somerset on the Eastern Shore of Maryland: A Study in Foundations and Founders*.

He is Susan and Paulette's 9th great-grandfather. The line of descent runs:

> William Coulbourne → his son Robert Coulburne (1667–1698) → his daughter Frances Colburne (1696–1755) → Frances's husband Capt William H. Parker → their daughter Ruth Parker (1742–1815) → her son Francis Boggs III (1775–1823) → his daughter Eliza Evins Boggs (1813–1880) → her husband John K. Rodgers / their son William Frances Rogers (1847–1895) → his daughter Mary Eliza Rogers (1878–1946) → her daughter Fannye Rogers Bull (1906–1982) → her daughter Mary Ellen Donahoe (1932–2023) → Susan and Paulette.

A side note on the deeper Coulbourne pedigree: Charles Coulbourne's 1623 Visitation of Somerset entry records a four-generation pedigree (William Colborne of Wythehill → John Colborne → Thomas Colborne → William Colborne of Braton, married Mary Topp) — but the FamilySearch profile itself notes that this visitation chain is "not unlikely" but explicitly **not proven** as the Maryland immigrant's own ancestry. The paper presents the visitation pedigree as English tradition rather than verified ancestry.

### John Floyd I "of Hog Island" (Wales → Northampton, 1660)

Distinct from Nathaniel Floyd (1601), this John Floyd I was born around 1640. The relationship between the two is not clearly documented; Nathaniel's profile lists John as a possible son but the link is not source-cited and a Hog Island deed records the surname spelled "Lloyd."

John Floyd I "of Hog Island" arrived in Northampton County on 30 July 1660 as one of twenty headrights transported by John Wilcocks. He was patented 100 acres of tract N37 in 1668 and, in 1681, jointly with three partners, the 2,200-acre patent for "Hogg Island alias Shooting Beach." He served as Constable of Northampton County in 1669 and as a Justice of the Peace. He died in Northampton between his will of 2 April 1687 and its probate of 28 July 1687.

His FamilySearch profile states cleanly: "He was presumably born in Wales, although nothing of his parentage or early life is known." This paper accepts the Welsh-origin attribution and the 1660 arrival; the further-back claims are not recoverable from current sources and are not relied on.

He is Susan and Paulette's 7th great-grandfather through Berry Floyd → Matthew Floyd Sr → John Floyd I.

## Chapter 5. The Revolutionary War Patriot — Capt William H. Parker

The single strongest documented Revolutionary War service in Susan and Paulette's direct line is that of **Capt William H. Parker** of Accomack County, Virginia.

Born around 1717 to **John Parker III "Sr." (1685–1755)** — son of John Parker II of Cashville, son of John Parker Sr. "of Mattapony," the 1655 immigrant — and to John III's wife. He died in Accomack County in 1784. He married **Frances Colburne**, daughter of **Robert Coulburne** (granddaughter of Col William Coulbourne), and through this marriage united the Coulbourne and Parker lines that produced Susan and Paulette's documented Revolutionary-war ancestor and, simultaneously, their Pomfret-Plantation ancestor.

The primary source for his Revolutionary service is *Accomack County Court Order Abstracts Vol. 28* (Marshall Steere McKey, 2017 abstracts of original microfilm; cited at McKey p. 30 et seq.). The relevant entries:

- **30 July 1777** — William Parker was recommended to the Governor of Virginia and **sworn in as Captain of militia** alongside ten other officers at the Accomack County Court.
- **26 August 1777** — Justice Arbuckle was ordered by the court to administer the oath of allegiance to "the companies under Captains William Parker, Young, Pettigrew and Burton."
- A later 1778 entry confirms his continuing rank as Captain through at least 1778; his service is documented through 1781.

This is Daughters of the American Revolution application-grade evidence. Susan and Paulette's documented descent runs:

> Capt William H. Parker → his daughter Ruth Parker (1742–1815) → her son Francis Boggs III (1775–1823) → his daughter Eliza Evins Boggs (1813–1880) → her son William Frances Rogers (1847–1895) → his daughter Mary Eliza Rogers (1878–1946) → her daughter Fannye Rogers Bull (1906–1982) → her daughter Mary Ellen Donahoe (1932–2023) → Susan / Paulette.

## Chapter 6. The Bull Farm — A 290-Year Continuity Lost in the 1950s

The longest single line of family memory for Susan and Paulette is that the family lost a farm in the mid-1950s in Accomack County. The farm was the Bull family ground, which had been held continuously by the Bull-Kellam-Rogers-Boggs intermarried network since at least 1665.

### What was lost

The lost ground sits in a small triangle of Accomack County between Onley, Pennyville, and Melfa — about four miles south of Onancock along the railroad corridor. The relevant family burial sites in this triangle, mapped in *Graven Stones: Inscriptions from Lower Accomack County, Virginia*, include:

- **Bull Burial Ground / Bull Farm at Melfa (GS-199)** — about four miles south of Onancock
- **Bull-Pennyville cemetery (GS-88)** — recorded in the same compilation as: *"A fairly large plot was destroyed; stones hauled away."*
- **Bull-Coard House at Greenbush** (HABS-documented; about four miles east of Onley)
- **Hollybrook Farm at Craddockville** — burial site of **Shepherd Turlington Bull**

The "stones hauled away" notation on the Bull-Pennyville cemetery is direct physical evidence that the family parcel left Bull-family hands during this period: family burying grounds were, by Eastern Shore custom, retained by inheritance even when the surrounding farmland was sold; their dismantling marks a hard breach in family ownership.

### The trigger and the window

Mary Eliza Rogers Bull — Joseph Saples Bull's widow — died at Onley on **17 November 1946**. Her death triggered the heir distribution of the Pennyville parcel to the children of Joseph Saples and Mary Eliza Bull (Fannye Rogers Bull and her surviving siblings). Within nine years, the heirs sold; the loss appears to have been a private sale rather than a foreclosure or sheriff's auction.

The agricultural context shapes the story. The Eastern Shore of Virginia in the late 1940s and early 1950s underwent the largest agricultural collapse in its history. Accomack County alone lost **2,349 farms — 69% of its 1925 base — by 1954**, as truck farming consolidated, the Eastern Shore Produce Exchange dissolved, and chicken-house construction and mechanized soybean farming reshaped the land use. The closing public marker of the era was the **1955 dissolution of the Eastern Shore Produce Exchange at Onley**, purchased on dissolution by **Carroll R. Bull** — an Onley/Florida produce operator who appears to be a member of a different surviving Bull family branch. He was one of the family's economic survivors.

The Bull family parcel at Pennyville was likely lost in this 1946–1955 window; the subsequent destruction of the family burial ground, with stones hauled away, is consistent with conversion of the land to chicken houses or mechanized agriculture. Specific deed-book references are at the Accomack County Courthouse and have not been pulled for this paper.

### What this means

The structural fact is striking: **a 290-year continuity of the same intermarried-Eastern-Shore-family network on the same patch of southern Accomack County ground, from Richard Kellam II's 1665 tithables entry through to the loss of the Bull farm in the 1946–1955 window**, ended with Mary Eliza Rogers Bull's death and the post-war agricultural collapse.

Mary Ellen Donahoe — born on that ground in 1932 — was thirteen at her grandmother's death in 1946 and twenty-three at the closing-of-era dissolution at Onley in 1955. Whatever specific family memories Susan and Paulette have of stories Mary Ellen told about losing the farm would be the highest-leverage v2 source for this chapter.

A 90-minute desk-research session at the Accomack County Courthouse — pulling Mary Eliza Rogers Bull's 1946 probate file and the Accomack grantor index 1946–1958 for any Bull-named transaction — would close most of the remaining open questions in this chapter. A Newspapers.com search of *Eastern Shore News* and *Peninsula Enterprise* 1950–1958 (free at the Bentonville Public Library) would surface any specific notice or social-page mention.

## Chapter 7. The Civil War Under Occupation

The Eastern Shore of Virginia had a Civil War unlike any other part of the Commonwealth. Accomack and Northampton counties voted with Virginia to secede in May 1861. On **17–18 November 1861**, six months later, **Brigadier General Henry H. Lockwood** of the Union Army marched south from Maryland into Accomack and Eastville without a shot being fired, and the Eastern Shore remained under federal occupation for the rest of the war. Residents who wished to retain property took the United States oath of allegiance; those who wished to fight for the Confederacy crossed the Chesapeake Bay to enlist with mainland units, principally the 39th Virginia Cavalry Battalion and the 46th Virginia Infantry's "Eastern Shore Refugees" Company F under Captain John H. White.

### Susan and Paulette's military-age ancestors

Across the war years, only two named ancestors in the maternal line were both alive and within the conscription-eligible age range:

- **John K. Rodgers (1823–1879)** of Cashville, Accomack — 38 at war's start, 41 at Lee's surrender; a married man with children.
- **William Samuel Kellam (1817–1898)** of Accomack — 44 at war's start; older than typical conscription-age but plausible for home-defense militia roles.

Other potentially-eligible kin:

- **William Frances Rogers (1847–1895)** — 14 at war's start, conscription-eligible by 1864 if he traveled to enlist on the mainland.
- **Joseph Saples Bull (1850–1923)** and **William Edward Bull (1854–1915)** — both too young.

A free-text search of the **National Park Service Civil War Soldiers and Sailors System** (a comprehensive index of Union and Confederate service records) returned **no enlistment records** for any Bull, Kellam, Rogers, Rodgers, Boggs, Floyd, or Garrison from Accomack County matching the male Brady-line ancestors of military age. A similar negative on the published roster of the **1st Loyal East Virginia Volunteers** — the only Eastern Shore Union infantry company that mustered, Company A at Cobbs Island in 1864.

No documented Civil War service for Susan and Paulette's direct Eastern Shore VA ancestors has surfaced in the records to date.

### The slaveholding question

Before the war, Accomack County's enslaved population was 24% of the total county population; Northampton's was 49%. The 1860 US Federal Slave Schedule for both counties is a public record at the National Archives and partially indexed at FamilySearch. It is honest expectation that at least one slaveholding direct ancestor will surface in a focused line-by-line check of the Slave Schedule for the surnames Bull, Kellam, Rogers/Rodgers, Boggs, Floyd, and Garrison in 1860 Accomack and Northampton Virginia. That check is pending and has not been completed for this v1.

The fact that Edward S Bull (1820–1856), Joseph Saples Bull's father, died five years before the war and his widow Susan Elizabeth Jacob Kellam Bull (who lived to 1900) was a long-term Accomack resident through the occupation, makes their household the primary candidate for slaveholding in the line in 1860. The family ground at Pennyville and Hollybrook in 1860 was almost certainly worked at least in part by enslaved labor, given the regional pattern. The paper acknowledges this rather than flatten it.

### What the war meant

The Civil War on the Eastern Shore was, for most of the people on the line, a story of **occupation, not enlistment**. Their farms were not burned. Their men did not die in battle. They took an oath of allegiance to a country they had voted to leave and watched their bank accounts depreciate as Confederate currency died in their wallets. The economic disruption was substantial — particularly the disruption of the regional oyster, salt, and produce trades — but the physical destruction was minimal compared to the Virginia counties on the mainland that saw multiple campaigns. The end of slavery, in 1865, did not require a battle on the Eastern Shore; it required a federal proclamation. That story — of how the descendants of mid-1800s Eastern Shore slaveholders lived through emancipation without combat — is the proper Civil War story for this line.

A note on regional published history: the canonical regional Civil War treatment is **Alton Brooks Parker Barnes, *Pungoteague to Petersburg* (3 volumes, 1988–1994)**, available at the Eastern Shore of Virginia Historical Society in Eastville. Earlier project notes attributed the regional treatment to Floyd L. Stallings and to a "99th NY Infantry recruited from the Eastern Shore" — both incorrect. The 99th NY was recruited in New York City and merely stationed on the Shore. The actual Eastern Shore Union recruiting unit was the 1st Loyal East Virginia Volunteers.

## Chapter 8. The Donahoe Ulster Line

The Donahoe surname in Susan and Paulette's line traces back, through nine documented generations, to **James Donaghe**, born **1635 at Kilkeel, County Down, in what is now Northern Ireland**. The Kilkeel location, in the Mourne Mountains region of southeast County Down, is in the Ulster Plantation area. It is the strongest single piece of evidence in support of Plantation-Scots, rather than anglicized-Catholic-Irish, ancestry for this line.

### The Plantation-Scots reading

Five lines of evidence converge on a Plantation-Scots Ulster origin:

- **Kilkeel was, by 1635, in one of the densest Plantation-Scots zones of Ulster.** The Plantation of Ulster (1606+) brought Scottish Presbyterian settlers to the land confiscated from the displaced Gaelic Irish chieftains after the 1607 Flight of the Earls. County Down was particularly heavy with Plantation Scots, especially in the lowland coastal areas and the southeastern townlands.
- **Hugh Casslin Donaghe Sr. (1728–1809)** — the immigrant or son-of-immigrant generation in America — settled in Augusta County, Virginia, where he held 1,227 acres on Naked Creek through multiple deeds 1765–1799. His neighbors were uniformly Ulster-Scots Presbyterian families: Baskin, Ritchey, McCutcheon, Frame, Chambers. The neighborhood is the canonical settlement pattern of Ulster-Scots-Presbyterian Augusta-County migration of the period.
- **The migration route from Ulster to America 1720s–1750s** is the well-documented Ulster→Pennsylvania (via Philadelphia or Newcastle, Delaware)→Great Wagon Road south down the Shenandoah Valley→Augusta County, Virginia route. Hugh Casslin Donaghe's Augusta County settlement fits this pattern exactly.
- **The terminal-syllable spelling drift** in 18th-century Virginia records for Brady's surname (Donaghe / Donahoo / Donaho / Donahee / Donahue / Donahoe) is consistent with rural-frontier orthography for an oral surname being written down by clerks unfamiliar with the standard spelling — typical of the Scots-Irish frontier of the period.
- **Surname distribution data**: forebears.io shows only 2 "Donaghe"-spelled bearers in 1880 Ireland versus 353 in North America — suggesting the spelling itself is an American frontier orthographic invention, not an Irish one. The Scottish original would have been Donachie or Donaghy; the Gaelic-Catholic original would have been Ó Donnchadha (anglicized "O'Donoghue" or "Donohoe").

### What is unverifiable

Above James Donaghe (1635) in the FamilySearch tree, every individual has zero sources. No Ulster Plantation muster-roll record, no Scottish-origin parish entry, no Catholic-Irish anglicization document. The ancestry is plausible but documentary support has not been recovered.

A relevant explanatory caveat: **the 1660s Hearth Money Rolls for County Down — which would be the canonical record for a 1635-born Down-County family — do not survive.** They were destroyed in the 1922 fire at the Public Record Office of Ireland in Dublin's Four Courts, along with most pre-1900 Irish census records and large numbers of parish records. The absence of records is therefore not evidence of fabrication; it is evidence of what was lost in 1922.

A definitive resolution of the Plantation-Scots-versus-Catholic-Irish question would come from a **Big-Y-700 Y-DNA test on a male-line Donahoe descendant** (currently $449 at FamilyTreeDNA, approximately 6 weeks for results). Brady's existing AncestryDNA results show Y-DNA haplogroup R-M269 (Western European, consistent with both Scottish and Irish origins). The Big-Y-700 would resolve the haplogroup at deep-clade level — distinguishing a Plantation-Scots haplogroup pattern (R-L21 → R-M222 cluster, characteristic of northwestern Scotland and Ulster Plantation Scots) from a Gaelic-Catholic haplogroup pattern (R-M222 alone, or DF63, or other Ulster-Gaelic-specific subclades).

### The wife — Elizabeth Currie / Curry

James Donaghe married **Elizabeth Currie or Curry** (1637–1687) of Ardee, County Louth, in Leinster — south of Ulster, in a region of mixed Plantation and surviving Catholic-Irish populations. Her FamilySearch profile carries zero sources. The mother attributed to her ("Gordana Gordia") is not a name that appears in any genealogical record outside the FamilySearch tree, which is a fraud-pattern signature. The Currie/Curry surname has both Scottish (Currie of Currie, near Edinburgh) and Irish-Gaelic (Ó Comhraidhe, of Munster) origins; either is possible. The line above Elizabeth Currie does not survive cleanly.

A possible cross-reference exists to a separate research project on Brady's paternal Clark/Monacan line, in which a Peter Curry (free Black taxpayer in Rockbridge County, Virginia in 1849–1850) appears in the Clark-family-related Sorrells case. The surnames coincide; whether the lines actually share an ancestor is unverifiable on current evidence and the connection is not pursued in this paper.

### The migration south

The Donahoe surname's path from Augusta County, Virginia (Hugh Casslin Donaghe, settled 1765+) to Ripley, Jackson County, West Virginia (where Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe was born in 1896) follows the Appalachian-spine settlement corridor — Augusta → Greenbrier → Kanawha River Valley → Jackson County. This is a typical Scots-Irish-Appalachian migration pattern of the late 18th and 19th centuries. The line did not reach the Eastern Shore of Virginia until **Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe's 1926 marriage to Fannye Rogers Bull** — at which point a West Virginia Donahoe man and an Eastern Shore Bull woman united their two completely different American family histories, and Mary Ellen Donahoe inherited both.

## Chapter 9. The Dean Pilgrim-Stock and Famine-Irish Inheritance

Susan and Paulette's father Delbert "Del" Allen Dean was born in **South Lyon, Oakland County, Michigan** in 1930. His parents — their Dean grandparents — were **Roy Franklin Dean (1886–1964)** and **Editha "Editha Addie" Read (1894–1980)**. Both were Michigan natives, but their ancestors had come to Michigan via two very distinct paths.

### The Pilgrim line — through Fisher, not Dean

A common family understanding is that the Dean line itself is Pilgrim-stock — a New England family with Mayflower-era ancestry. This is half right and half misattributed.

The **Dean** surname in the line traces back to **Nathaniel Dean (1693–1727)** of Connecticut, who married **Johanna Fisher** in the Massachusetts Bay Colony on 17 May 1716. Above Nathaniel Dean, the surviving FamilySearch tree carries no parents; his line dead-ends at his own birth in Connecticut. There is no documented Mayflower or Plymouth-Bay or Massachusetts-Bay 1620–1640 immigration on the Dean surname proper. The "Pilgrim Dean" is not currently a recoverable claim.

The **Pilgrim-era Great Migration ancestry that does survive in the line enters not through the Dean surname but through the Fisher surname** — specifically through Nathaniel Dean's wife Johanna Fisher, whose father and grandfather both immigrated to Massachusetts Bay on the ship *Rose*.

**Anthony Fisher Sr. (1591–1671)** was christened 23 April 1591 at Stradbroke, Suffolk, England, and baptized at St. Botolph's. He arrived in Boston aboard the **ship *Rose* from Yarmouth on 26 June 1637**. He took the Freeman's Oath in 1646 and signed the Dedham Covenant, the founding compact of the town of Dedham, Massachusetts (signature #36). He died 18 April 1671 in Dorchester, Massachusetts.

His son **Anthony Fisher Jr. (~1623–1670)**, born and christened at Syleham, Suffolk, England (christening 7 August 1623), arrived on the **same voyage of the *Rose* on 26 June 1637** with his father at age fourteen. He joined the Dedham Church on 20 July 1645 and took the Freeman's Oath on 6 May 1646. He died 13 February 1670 at Dedham.

Both arrivals are documented in the New England Historic Genealogical Register, Volume 151, pages 171–183 (1997: Hyde and Plummer, "The English Ancestry of New England Settlers Joshua and Anthony Fisher") and in *The Ancestry of Eva Belle Kempton 1878–1908* (NEHGS, 1996–2008), Volume 4, pages 239–244.

The Fisher line above Anthony Sr. is documented further back in Suffolk parish records — through **Anthony Fisher (1558–1640)** of Stradbroke, Suffolk; **William Fisher (1527–1591)** of Syleham, Suffolk (a literate yeoman who owned armor and whose 1591 will was probated at the Consistory Court of Norwich); and **Robert Fisher (~1495–1564)** of Wingfield, Suffolk — to **John Fisher of Wingfield (1465–1523)**, the deepest documented Fisher ancestor in the line. John Fisher of Wingfield's will is dated 27 February 1522/3 and was proved 15 April 1523; he appears in the 1524 lay subsidy of Syleham. He is Susan and Paulette's 13th great-grandfather.

This is the cleanest deep European chain in the entire pedigree. It is well-sourced through the standard New England genealogical literature.

### The Famine-Irish line — Daly, Donovan, and Arundel-from-Wales

The other major line in the Dean side is Famine-era Irish Catholic. Roy Franklin Dean's mother — Susan and Paulette's paternal great-grandmother — was **Helena Daly (1859–1948)**, daughter of two Irish-born parents who emigrated during the Famine years.

**John Daly** was born in 1841 or 1842 in Ireland; his father was **Timothy Daly (1816–1871)**, born at **Ballincolly, County Cork, Ireland**, and died at Cork. **Ellen Donovan** was born around 1844 in Ireland; her father was **Daniel Donovan (1812–1880)**, born at **Durrus, County Cork, Ireland**, and died in Pennsylvania. Daniel Donovan's emigration to Pennsylvania is documented; his death in Pennsylvania confirms the Famine-era Irish-Catholic-American pattern.

John Daly and Ellen Donovan married **19 September 1859 at Minane Bridge, County Cork, Ireland** — and emigrated to America together shortly afterward. By 1870 and 1880 they were residing in Grass Lake, Jackson County, Michigan. Helena Daly, their daughter, was born there in 1859.

A surprise on the line: Ellen Donovan's mother **Catherine Arundel (1811–1892)** was born in Durrus, County Cork — but Catherine's father **William Arundel (born 1775)** was born in **Pembrokeshire, Wales**, not in Ireland. The Arundel surname entered the Donovan line via this Welsh immigrant. Pembrokeshire and southern County Cork share an ancient maritime relationship — Welsh-Irish migration across St. George's Channel was a continuous medieval and early-modern pattern, and the Arundel line's entry from Wales into Catholic-Irish Cork society is consistent with that maritime corridor.

### What the Dean side gives the line

The Dean side gives Susan and Paulette: a verified Great Migration arrival on the Fisher line; a verified Famine-Irish-Catholic immigration on the Daly-Donovan line with documented County Cork townlands; a Welsh-Pembrokeshire maritime migration into Ireland one generation before that; and a five-generation Michigan family settled in Oakland and Livingston counties. It also gives the line a crucial geographic counterpoint to the Eastern Shore Virginia continuity — a Northern, industrial, immigrant inheritance balancing the Southern, agricultural, multi-generational rootedness on the maternal side.

The marriage of Mary Ellen Donahoe (Eastern Shore Virginia → Danville Virginia) and Delbert "Del" Allen Dean (South Lyon Michigan → Danville Virginia) was, in microcosm, a marriage of those two American inheritances.

## Chapter 10. The Maternal Bloodline — Susan Caylor Dean

Susan was born to Mary Ellen Donahoe and Del Dean, and given the middle name **Caylor** — a name that runs through the maternal line. **Lucinda Caylor (1845–1881)**, who married William Penn (or Patrick) Donahoe, was Mary Ellen's paternal-paternal-paternal great-grandmother. The Caylor surname is a German-Palatinate-derived name (originally Köhler or Kayler, an Anglicization of which is Caylor) that came to Pennsylvania in the colonial-era German immigration of the early 1700s. It was the name preserved as a middle name in Susan, and also in **Joel Caylor Donahoe (1927–2009)** — Mary Ellen's older brother — who carried it forward in his generation.

The middle name is one of the small artifacts of family memory that connects the line of Susan and Paulette to the deep migration patterns this paper has been tracing. A German-Palatinate name preserved in a Virginia family of Eastern Shore Anglo and Appalachian Scots-Irish ancestry, given by parents who themselves were one generation removed from the Eastern Shore farms and one generation removed from the West Virginia coal-country towns, is a small piece of the larger continental story of how American families combine.

Susan's full name — **Susan Caylor Dean** — reads, when its parts are separated, as a compressed family inheritance: *Susan* the given name; *Caylor* the German-Palatinate middle name carried through five generations of the Donahoe paternal line; *Dean* the Connecticut-and-Michigan paternal surname now firmly anchored in Danville, Virginia. Each part of her name is a different chapter of this paper.

Paulette carries the Dean surname directly. Both daughters lived their childhoods in Danville and grew up on stories of the Eastern Shore, the Bull farm, Mary Ellen's parents and grandparents. They are, between them, the living memory of the line.

This paper has tried to be honest about what the documents say and what they do not. What follows in the appendices catalogues the sources, the integrity ledger of what is verified versus what is family tradition versus what has been retracted, and the specific things Susan and Paulette could most help with in a future v2.
