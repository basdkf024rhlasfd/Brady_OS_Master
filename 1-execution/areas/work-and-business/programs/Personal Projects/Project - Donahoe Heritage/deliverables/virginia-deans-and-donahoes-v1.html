A note on this document

This research paper traces the documented and traditional ancestry of Susan Caylor Dean and her sister Paulette Dean, daughters of Mary Ellen Donahoe (“Mom”) and Delbert “Del” Allen Dean. It draws on Mary Ellen’s own FamilySearch-synced family tree, on the published primary sources cited throughout, and on the verifying work of an AI research engine that walked the tree generation by generation and tested each claim against documentary evidence.

The compilation was performed by AI. The narrative below is the AI’s synthesis of what the underlying sources show. Where original sources record a claim incorrectly, the error will appear here as well. AI synthesis can also introduce occasional mistakes of its own. The footnotes and source citations are provided so that any reader can return to the underlying record and verify or correct the claim. Readers should treat sourced primary-record claims as the authority and AI synthesis as secondary.

This is version 1. It is intended as the first complete pass — solid, sourced, and honest about its uncertainties — rather than a fully perfected family history. Several lines remain open. The places where Susan and Paulette can most directly improve this document, with their own knowledge and any family documents they hold, are listed in the Where Susan and Paulette Can Add section at the end. That contribution will become the basis of v2.

The pedigree behind this paper has been rebuilt three times in the course of compilation, each time correcting a previous reading. Some of those earlier readings produced exciting claims that did not survive verification — descent from John Rolfe’s widow Joane Pierce, descent from Lt. Col. Richard Cocke (and, by extension, Presidents Washington and Jefferson and General Robert E. Lee), descent from Capt. Adam Thoroughgood and Maj. Gen. John Custis II Esq, and a baronetcy in the Maynard line. None of those claims is in this document. Each of them is either a cousin relationship rather than a direct descent, or a paternal-line ancestor of Susan’s son Brady that has no biological connection to Susan herself, or — in the Maynard case — an aspirational graft flagged as fraudulent on the FamilySearch profile by a careful Maynard-family researcher. The deletion of those lines from this paper made the whitepaper smaller. It also made it more credible.

What remains is still substantial. Two genuine FFV-class colonial Virginia immigrants — Col. William Coulbourne of Pomfret Plantation and Richard Kellam II of Occahannock — sit clearly in Susan’s direct line. A Revolutionary War militia captain, Capt. William H. Parker of Accomack County, stands in the same line and offers the cleanest single Patriot-ancestor path for a Daughters of the American Revolution application. A Welsh-origin Northampton County immigrant, John Floyd I “of Hog Island,” arrived as a transported headright in 1660. A Great Migration ancestor, Anthony Fisher Sr., crossed to Boston aboard the Rose in 1637 and signed the Dedham Covenant the following year. An Ulster Scots-Irish patriarch, James Donaghe of Kilkeel, Co. Down, gave the Donahoe surname its plausible 1635 origin point. Famine-era Irish Catholics on the Dean side — Daly and Donovan from Co. Cork, with a Welsh Arundel patriarch — are documented to specific townlands. And a family-tradition Native American ancestor, Mary “Molly” Bays/Baze Woodrum (formerly thought to be “Mary Kimburrow,” a misidentification corrected below), is recorded in a family Bible as a Monacan woman from the Indian village of Mowhemencho on the upper James River.

These are the ancestors whose lives and migrations shape this paper.

1. Headline: who Susan and Paulette descend from

Susan Caylor Dean and Paulette Dean are daughters of:

  • Mary Ellen Donahoe (b. 9 April 1932, Accomac, Accomack Co., Virginia; d. 5 May 2023, Piney Forest Health and Rehab, Virginia; buried Danville, Virginia) — known to the family as “Mom”
  • Delbert “Del” Allen Dean (b. 24 August 1930, South Lyon, Oakland Co., Michigan; d. 18 January 1996, Danville, Virginia)

Mary Ellen and Del were married approximately around 1950, on the evidence of the births of their children; the marriage itself does not yet have a primary-source citation in this file. Their lives crossed two American family worlds. Mary Ellen carried into the marriage the accumulated colonial Virginia inheritance of her mother’s Eastern Shore Bull family and her father’s Appalachian-frontier Donahoe family. Del carried a New England Pilgrim-stock Dean and Fisher inheritance through his father, and a Famine-era Irish Catholic Daly and Donovan inheritance through his mother. The two genealogies are quite different in shape — one a Tidewater-and-Eastern-Shore Anglican-and-Methodist farmer line on land continuously held for nearly three centuries; the other a Massachusetts and Connecticut Puritan-and-Congregational line followed by an Irish Catholic immigrant generation that landed in Pennsylvania in the 1840s and 1850s.

This paper covers both. It treats them in the order they accumulate behind Susan and Paulette, deepest line first.

2. Mary Ellen Donahoe (“Mom”), the bridge

Mary Ellen Donahoe was born on 9 April 1932 at Accomac, Accomack County, Virginia, on or near the family ground her mother’s people had farmed for nearly three centuries. Her mother, Fannye Rogers Bull, had been born twenty-six years earlier at Onley, three miles south, in the same southern-Accomack district where the Bull and Kellam families had held land since the 1660s. Her father, Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe, had come east into Virginia from Ripley, Jackson County, West Virginia, where he was born in 1896 — a long Appalachian remove from the Eastern Shore, and the consequence of an Ulster Scots-Irish migration that had moved his family west across the Pennsylvania mountains in the eighteenth century before doubling back into Virginia in the early twentieth. Paul and Fannye had married on 31 July 1926 at Pocomoke City, Worcester County, Maryland — just over the state line from Onley. By the time Mary Ellen was born six years later, her father was thirty-six and her mother twenty-six.

Mary Ellen’s birth on the Eastern Shore was the family’s last direct connection to that ground. Within her lifetime, the Bull family farm at Pennyville/Onley would pass out of the family — most likely in the 1950–1956 window, during the structural collapse of Eastern Shore truck farming that took Accomack County from 3,418 farms in 1925 to 1,069 in 1954. The Joseph Saples Bull line — Mary Ellen’s grandfather Joseph (b. 1850 Pennyville, d. 1923) and his wife Mary Eliza Rogers (d. 1946) — had held the homeplace for three generations. When Mary Eliza died in 1946, her surviving children — Fannye and her siblings, who had stayed on the Eastern Shore while Fannye moved with Paul to Danville — inherited the parcel jointly. Within nine years, the parcel was gone. The Bull family burial ground at Pennyville (catalogued GS-88 in Graven Stones: Inscriptions from Lower Accomack County, Virginia) is recorded in the Eastern Shore cemetery survey as having been “destroyed; stones hauled away” — direct material evidence that the parcel passed out of Bull family hands and was cleared, most likely for industrialized chicken-house construction or mechanized soybean planting that swept the Shore in the same decade. The 1955 dissolution of the Eastern Shore of Virginia Produce Exchange at Onley is the public marker of the era’s close.

The names of the specific Bull-line cousins who lost the farm — Mary Eliza Bull’s surviving children in 1946 — are recoverable from her Accomack County probate file and from the 1900–1930 Accomack censuses. Those records have not been pulled for v1 of this paper. Susan and Paulette may know some or all of these names directly; their oral memory is the highest-leverage path to closing this question.

Mary Ellen’s life after Accomack tracked the family migration to Danville, Virginia, where she would marry Delbert “Del” Allen Dean (b. 1930 South Lyon, Oakland County, Michigan). Del had come east from Michigan; the meeting place and circumstance are not documented in this file but would be among Susan’s and Paulette’s earliest direct recollections. Del died in Danville on 18 January 1996. Mary Ellen lived another twenty-seven years, dying 5 May 2023 at Piney Forest Health and Rehab in Virginia at the age of ninety-one. She is buried at Danville.

A note on naming: Mary Ellen carried the middle name “Caylor” forward into Susan, who is “Susan Caylor Dean.” The Caylor name in the family came from Lucinda Caylor (b. 1845, d. 1881), wife of William Penn (or Patrick) Donahoe — Mary Ellen’s great-great-grandmother on her father’s Donahoe side. Caylor is a German Palatine surname (originally Köhler or Kayler), brought into the line by way of a Pennsylvania-Dutch settler whose own genealogy is not yet traced in v1.

A second note, on religion: family memory holds that Mary Ellen “became Methodist eventually.” This is structurally consistent with what the records show. Her mother’s Bull family had been Anglican (Church of England) in colonial Accomack and converted to Methodist during the great Methodist revivals of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries — the Oak Grove Methodist Church (Burton’s Chapel) at Melfa, where an 1898 Bull ancestor is buried, anchors that conversion in the family’s local geography. Her father’s Donahoe family was originally Ulster Scots-Irish Presbyterian, and the Augusta County Donahoes married into the Presbyterian frontier network in the 1740s and 1750s. The Catholic identification — if it existed in Mary Ellen’s father’s generation specifically — would have been a later overlay, not a deep family tradition. Mary Ellen’s adult Methodism therefore reads less as a conversion than as a return to her mother’s heritage. This reading depends heavily on Susan’s and Paulette’s direct recollection of the religious atmosphere of the household; that memory is the primary source for the next pass.

3. The Bull family of Pennyville (1660s–1955)

The Bull family of southern Accomack County is the deep-Virginia anchor of Susan and Paulette’s maternal line. The traceable line in v1 begins with Elijah “Eli” Bull (b. ca. 1748 Accomack County), who married Nancy Fitzgerald on 21 December 1785 at Accomack and is the earliest directly-documented Bull on Susan’s tree. Above Elijah, the FamilySearch tree carries no parent links; the colonial-Accomack patentee Bull patriarch is therefore not recoverable from FamilySearch alone and would require courthouse work in Accomac and at the Library of Virginia. Whitelaw (vol. II) notes Bulls in Accomack from at least the 1680s, but the connection from those colonial Bulls to Elijah is not yet traced. This is one of the open lines flagged in section 14 below.

What is clear is that the family ground sat in a roughly eight-mile triangle: Onley–Pennyville–Melfa–Craddockville–Greenbush. Within that triangle, ten distinct Bull-family cemeteries are catalogued in the Eastern Shore survey, including the Bull Burial Ground/Bull Farm at Melfa (GS-199), the destroyed Bull-Pennyville cemetery (GS-88), the Bull Plot east of Melfa (GS-188), and Hollybrook Farm at Craddockville (GS-25), where Brady’s collateral ancestor Shepherd Turlington Bull is buried as graven stone #41. The Greenbush Bull-Coard House (HABS VA-493) is documented four miles east of Onley.

The direct line through the Bull surname into Susan runs:

Elijah “Eli” Bull (b. 1748) → his son Thomas Southy Bull (1788–1860) m. Rachel Floyd (1792–1823) → their son Edward S. Bull (1820–1856) m. Susan Elizabeth Jacob Kellam (1825–1900) → their son Joseph Saples Bull (1850 Pennyville–1923) m. Mary Eliza Rogers (1878–1946) → their daughter Fannye Rogers Bull (1906 Onley–1982) → her daughter Mary Ellen Donahoe (1932 Accomac–2023) → Susan and Paulette.

Two features of this line bear emphasis. First, Edward S. Bull’s marriage to Susan Elizabeth Jacob Kellam in 1843 is one of three intermarriages between the Bull and Kellam families in Susan’s tree (the others on the Mary Eliza Rogers maternal-grandmother line, where John Chandler III married Patience Kellam, and where Sylvester Randal Chandler Sr. married Mary Ann Kellam). This is the standard Eastern Shore pattern of cousins-marrying-cousins across multiple generations within the same parish neighborhood. The Susan-Paulette line is best understood as Bull-Kellam-Rogers-Boggs-Floyd-Garrison rather than as any single surname trunk.

Second, the Bull-Kellam land continuity is roughly 290 years — from Richard Kellam II’s 1665 tithe entry through the 1950s loss of the Pennyville parcel. The same families farmed essentially the same ground for nearly three centuries before Mary Ellen left for Danville with her mother’s marriage and the parcel passed out of the family within the next two decades.

The 1950s loss is the single most emotionally weighted event in the family’s recent memory. The structural drivers — collapse of Eastern Shore truck farming, dissolution of the Produce Exchange at Onley in 1955, mechanization and consolidation that took 69 percent of Accomack County’s farms in three decades — are well-documented. The specific loss event for the Joseph Saples Bull homeplace at Pennyville is not yet pulled from the Accomack County deed index. The records that would close the question — Joseph Saples Bull’s 1923 will and Mary Eliza Rogers Bull’s 1946 will (both on file at the Accomack courthouse), plus the grantor index entry for the loss-of-farm transaction filed under the heir-cousins’ surnames, plus the Eastern Shore News and Peninsula Enterprise foreclosure-and-sale notices for 1950–1956 — are entirely accessible but require either an Accomack courthouse visit or a paid Newspapers.com search. This is one of the highest-priority next steps for v2.

4. The Kellam line: Richard Kellam II of Occahannock (1618–1703)

The single most heavily-documented colonial ancestor on Susan’s line is Richard Kellam II “of Occahannock,” born and christened 30 August 1618 at St. James Parish, Aslackby, Lincolnshire, England. He was the sixteen-year-old indentured passenger aboard the ship Primrose (Capt. Douglas), which cleared Gravesend on 27 July 1635 bound for Virginia. Indentured to Nicholas Waddelowe, a Quaker farmer, he survived his term and emerged as a substantial Eastern Shore landowner.

Three primary records define his Eastern Shore presence. First, on 6 May 1647 he received a deed from “King Tabutiabum of Nandau” for a neck of land on the north side of Occahannock Creek called Quinduit — that is, he purchased land directly from a Native American king. The deed was recorded 2 August 1649 and witnessed by Tho Johnson, Manatuke, and Rich Workeman. Second, on 8 March 1654 he was committed for contempt of court for affronting justice during a suit by “Andyaman and other Indyans,” reportedly saying “if he had the Indyan at ye door he would cut his throat.” The two records together capture the contradictions of mid-seventeenth-century Eastern Shore life: a man who would buy land directly from a Native ruler in 1647 and seven years later threaten violence against a Native litigant in court. Third, the 1655 Act of Assembly designated his land as the Eastern Shore court / church / prison / public-mart at the Hungars Creek divide, where courts were held for several years; this places him at the center of Accomack civic infrastructure during the formative decade.

He patented at least four substantial tracts: 300 acres on the north side of Occahannock Creek (1649), 200 acres on the same (1654, re-patented 1661), 400 acres at the head of Matchepungo Creek (1662), 800 acres on Matchipungo (1663), and 900 acres for transporting eighteen persons (1664). In total, Lord Berkeley granted him about 2,400 acres on the Eastern Shore. He also acquired 500 acres on the Pocomoke River in Somerset County, Maryland — a tract he named “Kellam’s Folly.” Tithables lists from 1662 through 1695 record him annually.

In 1663 he committed to the killing of Richard Buckland’s cow “Butterflower,” confessed in court, and was fined 1,000 lbs of tobacco plus another 1,000 lbs and a new cow.

Richard Kellam II signed his will on 1 June 1703, “very aged and weak in body.” It bequeathed sixty acres on Russell’s Branch to his daughter Rose, fifty acres on Beaver Dam Branch to his son William, his horse mill and the remainder to his wife Sarah, and five shillings each to his children Edward, William, Richard, Sarah Curtis, and Ann Wallis. The will was probated between 1 June and 6 October 1703 at Accomack County. He had been in Virginia for sixty-eight years.

Susan’s descent runs from his daughter Rose through five generations of Garrison and Floyd farmers to Edward S. Bull’s mother Rachel Floyd, who married Thomas Southy Bull in 1809. The line is: Richard Kellam II → Rose Kellam (1650–1717) → Jonathan Garrison (1690–1760) → George Garrison (1734–1789) → Rachel Garrison (1766–1815) → Rachel Floyd (1792–1823) → Edward S. Bull (1820–1856).

Above Richard Kellam II, the FamilySearch tree carries his father Richard Kellam Sr. (~1581–23 September 1636), born at Aslackby and married at St. Paul, Lincoln on 20 March 1616 to Katherine Ederidge. Sr.’s father, Edmund Kellam (b. 1555), is the last sourced ancestor on this line; above Edmund, the tree confuses Kellams with the Draper family of Boxley and Crayford in Kent (Thomas Kellam’s putative father is recorded as “Robert Kellam ALS Draper,” and his sibling list mixes Kellams with Drapers). The Kellam patriline is solid back to Edmund Kellam (1555); above him, no claim is made in this paper.

5. The Coulbourne line: Pomfret Plantation, Somerset Co., Maryland

Col. William Coulbourne (b. 19 January 1630 Westminster, City of Westminster, London, England, christened St. Martin-in-the-Fields; d. 21 January 1689 Marion, Somerset Co., Maryland; buried 22 January 1689 at Pomfret Plantation, Marion) is one of two FFV-class colonial-immigrant patriarchs in Susan’s direct line.

He arrived in Northampton County, Virginia by 25 March 1652, when he signed — along with 116 other men — the “Engagement tendered to ye Inhabitants of Northampton County,” pledging fidelity to the Commonwealth of England without King or House of Lords. He took two Northampton land patents of 350 acres each (4 March 1652/3 and 28 February 1662/3), then crossed the colonial line to settle at Annemessex on the lower Eastern Shore of Maryland between October 1663 and 27 January 1663/4. In June 1679 he patented 1,400 acres there, between Jones’ Creek and Coulbourne’s Creek, naming the seat Pomfret. The Coulbourne family held Pomfret through nine generations until its sale in 1921. The plantation was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1984.

He was a Quaker sympathizer in a colony nominally Anglican. On 12 April 1661 the Northampton court entered an order of recantation against him for entertaining Quakers, but the side note in the order book reads that the order was “refused to bee subscribed by Wm. Colborne.” He was reportedly arrested with Henry White, Thomas Leatherbury, and Ambrose Dixon for giving succor to Quakers. Eleven years later, George Fox, founder of the Society of Friends, called on Coulbourne and held a service at his house during Fox’s 1672 American visit — a point of connection to the spiritual headwaters of Pennsylvania and the broader Quaker Atlantic.

His military service was substantial: Lieutenant 26 March 1664 (foot company under Capt. William Thorne); Captain of foot Manokin to Pocomoke February 1669/70; Captain and commander of a horse troop October 1673; Colonel of foot raised in Somerset and Dorchester Counties (1681); and still “Coll: of the foot” in December 1688. He was nominated second sheriff of Somerset in 1667; served as Lord Proprietor’s commissioner and justice of the peace from 22 February 1672; was Sheriff of Somerset in October 1673 and November 1675; and held the Commission of the Peace from February 1670 through 1673 and again from March 1676 until his death.

In July 1687 the Lord Proprietor designated him to head the commission charged with drawing up a treaty with the Nanticoke Indians; the treaty was signed 11 August 1687. On 28 November 1689, less than a year before his death, he signed the loyalty oath to King William and Queen Mary at the Glorious Revolution.

He married twice: Anne Margery Huxton (1630–1670) in July 1656 at Northampton, Virginia, and Margaret Cooper (1634–1700) in 1670 at Somerset. His son Robert Coulburne (1667–1698) is Susan’s 8th-great-grandfather. His parents per the FamilySearch tree are Charles Coulbourne (1603–1662, born St. Martin in the Fields, immigrant to Somerset Co., MD) and Audrey (b. 1600).

A traditional pedigree appears in the 1623 Visitation of Somerset, England (Harleian Society Pub. Vol. XI, p. 26), tracing William Colborne of Wythehill → John → Thomas → William of Braton (living 1623, married Mary Topp). The Coulbourne profile on FamilySearch reproduces this pedigree with a careful note that it is “not unlikely” but explicitly NOT proven for the Maryland immigrant. This paper records the visitation chain as English tradition, not verified ancestry.

Susan’s line of descent from Col. William Coulbourne runs through Robert Coulburne to Frances Colburne (1696–1755), who married Capt. William H. Parker of Accomack — the Revolutionary War officer treated in section 6 below — and then through their daughter Ruth Parker, granddaughter Francis Boggs III, great-granddaughter Eliza Evins Boggs, great-great-granddaughter (via William Frances Rodgers and Mary Eliza Rogers) to Fannye Rogers Bull, Mary Ellen Donahoe, and then Susan and Paulette.

6. The Parker line and the Revolutionary War: Capt. William H. Parker

The Parker family enters Susan’s line from England in the mid-seventeenth century and supplies the most clearly documented Revolutionary War service in v1.

John Parker Sr. “of Mattapony” (born 25 May 1634, christened Southampton, Hampshire, England; died 19 September 1695, Cashville, Accomack Co., Virginia) was the immigrant. He came to Virginia by March 1655, transported by his brother George. In 1660 he patented six hundred acres on the Eastern Shore which he named “Mattapany” — a Native place-name, suggesting a tract originally tied to the Accomack chiefdom or the closely-related Maryland Mattapany. He is well-documented in MILESFILES, in Whitelaw’s Virginia’s Eastern Shore, and in Accomack County land records. His parents per the FamilySearch tree are George Parker “of Southampton” (16 December 1596–before 9 May 1639), whose will dated 24 October 1638 is transcribed verbatim on his profile, and Abigail Barlow (1600–?). Above George Parker the line continues in Southampton parish records through John Parker (1550–1612) and Marie Gerard, with the patriline dead-ending there; the documentation, however, is unusually solid for an early-seventeenth-century English yeoman/merchant family.

The line continues in Accomack with John Parker II (~1662–before 7 February 1721, born Cashville, Accomack), who left a 26 January 1721 will, and then John Parker III “Sr.” (1685–1755), born and died in Accomack, who left a will written 25 August 1754 and probated 1 January 1755. John III’s son Capt. William H. Parker (b. ~1717 Accomack, d. before 30 March 1784 Accomack) is the central figure.

Capt. William H. Parker is documented in the Accomack County Court Order Abstracts, Volume 28 (27 May 1777 to 1 March 1780), abstracted by JoAnn Riley McKey from the original 1777–1780 court order book held on microfilm at the Eastern Shore Public Library. Three specific entries establish his Revolutionary War service:

30 July 1777, Accomack County Court (p. 9, manuscript p. 17): “The following were recommended to the Governor to be commissioned officers of the county militia: Charles Marshall (captain) William Parker (captain) Robinson Custis (lieutenant) Thomas Slocomb (lieutenant) Southy Copes (lieutenant) Ismael Andrews (lieutenant)…”

Same court, same day (manuscript p. 17): “Commissioned as officers of the county militia, the following were sworn in: William Parker, Solomon Smith, Joseph Kelly, George Justice, George Truet Taylor, Ismael Andrews, Francis Savage, Thomas Young, Zorobabel Watson, John Dix and Southy Copes.”

26 August 1777, Accomack County Court (p. 10, manuscript p. 19): “Ordered that the following administer the oath of allegiance to companies of militia: … James Arbuckle — to the companies under Captains William Parker, Young, Pettigrew and Burton…”

In plain language: in late July 1777, the Accomack County Court recommended William Parker to the Governor of Virginia as a captain of county militia; he was sworn in the same day; one month later, the same court ordered James Arbuckle to administer the oath of allegiance to the militia companies under Capt. Parker and three other captains. He was commanding a militia company in Accomack County during the Revolutionary War. The subsequent Captain commission running 1778–1781 is recorded on his FamilySearch profile and would be in successor volumes of the McKey court-order abstracts.

This is the documentary chain a Daughters of the American Revolution application requires for Patriotic Service under the “civil officer / militia officer commissioned during the Revolution” category. Susan and Paulette appear to be eligible to file for DAR membership through Capt. William H. Parker. No DAR Patriot record has yet been issued under his name in published indexes, which means an application under him would establish a fresh DAR ancestor-record entry rather than supplementing an existing one. The documentary proofs needed are (a) the Accomack County Court Order Abstracts pages cited above (publicly available at https://esplheritage.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Accomack-County-Court-Order-Abstracts-Volume-28-1777-to1780.pdf) and (b) a chain of vital records from Capt. Parker through eight intermediate generations to Susan and Paulette: Frances Colburne → Ruth Parker → Francis Boggs III → Eliza Evins Boggs → William Frances Rodgers → Mary Eliza Rogers → Fannye Rogers Bull → Mary Ellen Donahoe.

The Parker line is also Susan’s primary path into the Coulbourne FFV line, since Capt. Parker’s wife Frances Colburne was a granddaughter of Col. William Coulbourne. Filing on Capt. Parker for DAR therefore simultaneously records both the Parker military service and the Coulbourne Eastern Shore Maryland founding-immigrant connection.

A second possible Patriot — Lt. Samuel Black (1727–1782, Augusta County, Virginia; commissioned 20 October 1778 in Capt. Robert McCreary’s Company of Virginia Militia per Augusta County Order Book p. 361; already DAR-approved with Patriot Number A010660) — appears in the family record as a possible direct ancestor on the Donahoe paternal-side Hobbs/Black branch. Lt. Black has not been verified through the FamilySearch relationship calculator as a direct ancestor of Susan in v1 of this paper. If verification confirms direct descent (rather than cousin relationship), Lt. Black becomes the easiest filing path because his Patriot record is already approved and a descendant filing today would only need to prove descent. This verification is one of the v2 priorities.

7. The Floyd line: Welsh origins and the Bona Nova

The Floyd surname reaches Susan through her ancestor Rachel Floyd (1792–1823), who married Thomas Southy Bull in 1809 and was the mother of Edward S. Bull. The line is fully colonial and Welsh-origin.

John Floyd I “of Hog Island” (born ca. 1640, place not certain but per his own profile “presumably in Wales, although nothing of his parentage or early life is known”; died before 28 July 1687, Northampton Co., Virginia) is the Eastern Shore immigrant. He arrived in Northampton County on 30 July 1660 as one of twenty headrights transported by John Wilcocks. In 1668 he patented 100 acres tract N37; in 1681 he patented 2,200 acres jointly with Hunt, Bibby, and Clarke for “Hogg Island alias Shooting Beach.” He was appointed constable of Northampton County in 1669. His will of 2 April 1687 was probated 28 July 1687. A Hog Island deed records his surname as “Lloyd” — the Welsh original. Susan’s line of descent runs through his son Matthew Floyd Sr. (1684–9 June 1752, born and died Northampton Co.), then Berry Floyd (7 July 1714–13 June 1771), then William Floyd (1747–1823), then Rachel Floyd (1792–1823, m. Thomas Southy Bull).

The FamilySearch tree links John Floyd I to a putative father, Nathaniel Floyd (1601–before 1674), born in Wales, who arrived in Virginia on the Bona Nova before 4 February 1624/5 at Blaney’s Plant, James City. Nathaniel patented 850 acres in Isle of Wight County, Virginia on 20 November 1637. Above Nathaniel Floyd, the FamilySearch tree carries an explicit “Questionable Parents” note flagging the next generation up — “Sir John Theophelus Floyd” and “Lady Mary in Queen Elizabeth’s Court Veriar Woodward” — as bogus. The flagged problems include: the alleged parents married seven or eight years after Nathaniel’s birth; the tree-linked father is shown with another son born sixteen years after his death; and no source indicates the wife was actually part of Queen Elizabeth’s court. This is the same aspirational-nobility pattern that appears elsewhere on FamilySearch under various surnames and that the present paper does not propagate. The Floyd line in this paper terminates at Nathaniel Floyd of Isle of Wight County (b. 1601, Wales), with the Bona Nova arrival recorded as a real fact and his English/Welsh parentage marked as not yet established.

8. Mary “Molly” Bays/Baze Woodrum: the family Bible, the Native ancestor, and the corrected identity

This section requires more care than any other. Family tradition holds that Susan and Paulette have Native American ancestry through Mary Ellen Donahoe’s father’s side — specifically through Mary Ellen’s paternal grandmother Leona Ellen Rollyson (1871–1942) and her ancestors. The Bible-record evidence for the Native-ancestry claim is real. The identity of the woman to whom the Bible record attaches is the issue.

The earlier reading of this paper — derived from the FamilySearch profile labelled “Mary Kimburrow” (FSID LRKY-41Q) — held that Susan’s seventh-great-grandmother was christened on 30 January 1703 at St. Peter’s Parish, New Kent, Virginia, was an Indian from the Monacan village of Mowhemencho on the upper James River, and married John Woodrum II in 1727. Closer reading of the original parish registers shows that this is a conflation of at least two different historical women.

The 1703 St. Peter’s Parish christening record is real and primary-source verifiable. The verbatim entry, from the Parish Register of Saint Peter’s, New Kent County, Virginia from 1680 to 1787 (transcribed and published 1904 by the National Society of the Colonial Dames of America in the State of Virginia), reads:

“Mary dau’ of Jn° Kimburrow Ju’ ye 30 Jany. 170[2/3].”

The same register lists this Mary Kimburrow’s siblings — John III, Major, Rebecka, Bulkley, Thomas Henry, and Marmaduke Sr. — also baptized at St. Peter’s. This Mary Kimburrow is fully known as Mary Dorcas Kimbrough Wilkins (b. 1703/4 New Kent VA; d. 1747; m. a Wilkins; six documented children none named Woodrum). She is not Susan’s ancestor.

The 1761 marriage record at St. James Northam Parish, Goochland County is also real and primary-source verifiable — and it documents a different woman. The Douglas Register (William Mac. Jones, ed., The Douglas Register, Richmond: J.W. Fergusson & Sons, 1928, p. 6 of the original Male Index and p. 4 of the Female Index, digitized at archive.org) records:

“Woodrum, John, & Mary Baze, both in this parish 1761, Nov: 15 p. 6.”

The Female Index cross-references: “BAZE, Mary, & John Woodrum, both in this parish Nov: 15 1761.” A supplementary index shows: “WOODRUM, John, & Mary Bays 24 Mar. 1764.” Six baptism entries dated 1764 to 1774 record children born to John Woodrum and “Mary Bays / Bayse / Bays / Bayes.” A 1776 baptism entry shows John Woodrum re-married to Mary Barker — strongly implying Mary Bays/Baze had died, consistent with the family-tradition death date of 4 November 1775.

The woman in Susan’s direct line is therefore Mary “Molly” Bays/Baze Woodrum, not Mary Kimburrow. Her surname is BAZE / BAYS / BAYSE / BAYES (the spelling varies across the Douglas Register entries). She is documented in the parish register as marrying John Woodrum on 15 November 1761 at St. James Northam Parish in Goochland County and as bearing seven children to him between 1764 and 1774. The “Kimburrow” attribution in the FamilySearch tree appears to be a long-standing error that welded together two distinct primary-source records — the 1703 New Kent christening of Mary Kimbrough Wilkins and the 1761 Goochland marriage of Mary Bays Woodrum — without an evidentiary bridge between them.

The Bible-record family tradition is a separate strand and rests on a different document. Per a note attached to Mary “Molly’s” FamilySearch profile and sourced to genealogytrails.com/ill/washington/woodrome.html:

“One of John’s great grandsons, Dr. Jacob Woodrum of Tennessee, had a bible that indicated that John was born in 1702. It was also written in the same bible that Mary (Molly) was born in 1704 and that she was an Indian. All the males in her tribe (Monacan) had been killed by the settlers. The Indian Village was called ‘Mowhemencho’. They occupied 10,000 acres of land close to present day Richmond on the south side of the James River in Virginia, Mary (Molly) later became John’s wife.”

The Bible was held in the late nineteenth century by Dr. Jacob Woodrum of Tennessee, John Woodrum’s great-grandson. Its contents were transcribed in a memorandum by Marjorie Fluor Moore dated 27 July 1983. Both the Bible itself and the Moore memorandum are not present as scanned images on Mary’s FamilySearch Memories tab; the Memories tab includes the genealogytrails paraphrase, an Ancestry probate index for an unrelated “Maria M. Malden,” the Douglas Register page documenting the 1761 Mary Baze marriage, and the modern Monacan Indian Nation tribal seal, but does not include the Bible page or the Moore memo as primary scans.

The Mowhemencho geography is real. Mowhemencho is one of five Monacan villages located by Captain John Smith on his 1612 Map of Virginia, on the south bank of the James River in what is now eastern Powhatan County between Bernards Creek and Jones Creek. By 1700 it was being called Manakin Town by the French Huguenot refugees who settled there in 1700–1701 under Governor Francis Nicholson, on a ten-thousand-acre grant “which had belonged to the Manakin [Monacan] Indians.” A 1702 report records Monacans returning to Manakin Town to hunt and trade — the precise period in which Mary “Molly” Bays/Baze would have been born.

What this paper concludes about Susan and Paulette’s Native ancestry:

  1. The 1761 marriage of John Woodrum to Mary Baze at St. James Northam Parish is a primary-source-verified event. Mary Baze is Susan’s seventh-great-grandmother through John Woodrum III (b. 1730) and the Woodrum-Meadows-Rollyson-Donahoe descent.
  2. The family-Bible tradition records Mary “Molly” as a Monacan woman from Mowhemencho. The Bible itself is not currently scanned and accessible; the chain of custody runs through Dr. Jacob Woodrum of Tennessee → Marjorie Fluor Moore’s 1983 memorandum → genealogytrails.com → FamilySearch.
  3. The Mowhemencho geography is consistent with the period (Native Monacans were demonstrably present on the site through 1702 and beyond).
  4. No primary record outside the family Bible documents Mary’s tribal status. The claim is family tradition, not primary-source verifiable.
  5. The FamilySearch tree previously linked Mary Bays/Baze to a set of named “Native ancestor” parents — “Sharp As Fox John Spiller,” “Dancing Waters Moon Spiller,” “Delaware Indian Fivekiller,” and “Water” — every one of whom is documented on FamilySearch itself as a fictional fabrication from Don Greene & Noel Swartz’s Shawnee Heritage book series. That book series has been denounced by the Cherokee Nation, the Shawnee chief, the Patawomeck tribal historian Bill Deyo, professional genealogists, Wikitree, and Geni (which isolated the entire fictional tree from its World Family Tree). The FSIDs for those fabricated ancestors carry explicit Custom Facts reading “FRAUDULENT, FICTIONAL ENTRY — FRAUDULENT, ENTRY NEEDS TO BE REMOVED.” They have been merged-deleted from FamilySearch as the cleanup proceeds. None of those named “Native ancestors” appears in Susan’s line in this paper.

The honest summary is: Susan and Paulette have a real family-tradition claim of Native American (Monacan) ancestry, recorded in a family Bible held by an extended-family descendant in Tennessee, attaching to a real Eastern-shore-Virginia woman named Mary “Molly” Bays/Baze who married into the Woodrum family in 1761. The geography of the claim is plausible — Mowhemencho is a documented Monacan village, the date fits the period of Monacan presence on that site, and the Bible-record tradition is a recognized form of genealogical evidence for periods predating civil registration. The claim is not, however, primary-source verifiable in the technical sense, and v1 records it accordingly.

A separate Native ancestry claim exists on Brady’s paternal side — through the Clark family of Buena Vista, Rockbridge County, Virginia, where Brady’s grandmother Lutie Fannie Clark descends from a documented Monacan/Cherokee community. That claim has its own Bible-and-court-record evidence and is documented in a separate paper, Monacan Heritage, focused on Brady’s paternal line. The two Native-ancestry claims — one on Mary Ellen Donahoe’s side, one on Dennis Eugene Smallwood’s side — are independent of each other.

9. The Donahoe line: Ulster Scots-Irish from Kilkeel, Co. Down

The Donahoe surname enters Susan’s line through her paternal grandfather Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe (b. 1896 Ripley, Jackson Co., West Virginia). The surname has been spelled variously as Donaghe, Donaghue, Donaho, Donahoo, Donahee, and Donahoe — a spelling cluster characteristic of Ulster Scots-Irish surnames passed through generations of frontier American clerks, where oral tradition rather than fixed orthography governed the written record.

The line is documented in the FamilySearch tree through eight generations to James Donaghe (b. 1635 Kilkeel, Co. Down, Northern Ireland; d. 1685). Above James, the tree records his parents as William Donaghe (1605–1655) and Margaret Donaghe — but with zero sources for any individual above James, and with a notable peculiarity that the wife shares her husband’s surname (a data-quality flag rather than a verified fact). The line is solid back to James Donaghe and unverified above him.

The choice of Kilkeel as place of origin matters. Kilkeel sits in the Mourne Mountains barony of County Down, on the southeast Ulster coast. By 1635 — James’s birth year — the Mourne/Kilkeel area had been a Lowland Scottish private-plantation settlement zone for nearly thirty years, since the 1606 Hamilton-Montgomery settlement of Upper Clandeboye. Mourne was not part of the official Plantation of Ulster (Counties Antrim and Down were excluded from the 1609 Plantation), but Scottish private colonization had brought Lowland Presbyterian Scots settlers — Hamiltons, Montgomerys, Stewarts, Boyds, and others — into the area in successive waves through the 1610s, 1620s, and 1630s. The contemporary documentary record for County Down was systematically destroyed in the 1922 Public Record Office of Ireland fire, which is why James Donaghe’s parentage cannot be recovered: not because the Ulster origin is wrong but because Down’s pre-1700 documentary record no longer exists.

The line continues through James John Donaghe (b. 1655) and John Donaghe (b. 1700) before reaching Hugh Casslin Donahoo / Donaghe Sr. (b. 1728, d. 1809, Augusta County, Virginia). Hugh is the immigrant or first-American-generation figure. He arrived in Augusta County, Virginia through what is recognizable as the Pennsylvania–Shenandoah–Borden’s Tract pipeline, the principal eighteenth-century migration route for Ulster Scots Presbyterian families. He was a substantial Augusta County landholder, settled near the mouth of Naked Creek, owned land speculatively, and left a will probated 25 December 1809. The Augusta County Order Books from 1775–1783 contain extensive militia lists, oaths of allegiance, and supply-tax assessments; whether Hugh’s name appears in any of these in a Patriotic Service capacity has not been confirmed in v1. He was forty-seven at the start of the Revolutionary War, within the age range for militia service or supply-tax payment.

The descent continues through James H. Donahoe / Donahoo (b. 1806), William Penn (or Patrick) Donahoe (b. 1842, who married Lucinda Caylor — the source of the Caylor middle name in the family), Rev. “Stephen” Alexander Donahoe (b. 1866, who married Leona Ellen Rollyson on 30 June 1891 at Gauley Bridge, Fayette Co., West Virginia), and finally “Paul” Riley Kidd Donahoe (b. 4 March 1896 Ripley, Jackson Co., West Virginia; d. 5 December 1979 Danville, Virginia; buried Danville National Cemetery). Paul’s burial at the Danville National Cemetery suggests military veteran status; this has not been confirmed against National Cemetery Administration records in v1.

The Donahoe story in v1 is therefore: a plausibly Plantation-era Ulster Scots family from Kilkeel, Co. Down, brought to America in the 1740s by Hugh Casslin Donaghe through the Pennsylvania-Shenandoah migration corridor, settling on the Augusta County frontier, surviving into the early federal period, moving west into the Appalachian foothills of West Virginia by the early nineteenth century, and finally crossing east into Virginia in Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe’s adult life when he married Fannye Bull and brought the Donahoe name to the Eastern Shore for one generation before the family relocated to Danville. It is not — despite the surname — a Famine-era Catholic Irish immigration story. The Famine-era Catholic Irish line in Susan’s family is the Daly and Donovan inheritance on her father’s mother’s side, treated in the next section.

Susan’s father’s line — the Dean line proper — is a Michigan story whose roots run back to colonial Connecticut and Massachusetts.

The Fisher line is documented through standard New England Historical and Genealogical Society sources — NEHGR 151:171–183 (“The English Ancestry of New England Settlers Joshua and Anthony Fisher” by Hyde and Plummer, 1997) and Smith and Sanborn, The Ancestry of Eva Belle Kempton 1878–1908 (NEHGS, 1996–2008), 4:239–244. So the corrected statement of Susan’s New England Pilgrim-era ancestry is: she descends from a Great Migration Pilgrim immigrant family — Anthony Fisher Sr., who came over on the Rose in 1637 and signed the Dedham Covenant — but through the Fisher line, not the Dean line. The Dean line in this paper begins at Nathaniel Dean of 1693 Connecticut and runs forward; its own deeper history is among the open questions for v2.

11. The Civil War: occupation, not enlistment

Susan’s Eastern Shore Virginia ancestors lived through the Civil War as occupied civilians, not as soldiers in either army. This is the structural fact of the war for this branch, and it differs from the default modern assumption about deep-Virginia families.

The Eastern Shore voted with Virginia for secession in May 1861 and raised the 39th Virginia Infantry Regiment that summer under Col. Charles Smith, with eleven companies drawn primarily from Accomack and Northampton. The geography made the Confederate hold untenable: the Eastern Shore is a seventy-mile peninsula bordered by Chesapeake Bay west and Atlantic east, connected to the mainland only by water, and its only land connection runs north into Union-loyal Maryland.

On 14 November 1861, Brigadier General Henry H. Lockwood marched south from Snow Hill, Maryland with approximately 4,500 troops — primarily the 1st Eastern Shore Maryland Volunteer Infantry, the 1st Delaware, the 5th New York, and the Purnell Legion. Lockwood issued a proclamation guaranteeing the property and rights of any Eastern Shore resident who took an oath of allegiance and laid down arms. The 39th Virginia did not give battle. By 18 November 1861, Lockwood occupied both Accomac Court House and Eastville without firing a shot. The Eastern Shore was in Union hands within seven months of Sumter and remained so for the rest of the war.

From November 1861 forward, every Bull, Kellam, Rogers, Boggs, Floyd, and Garrison ancestor in Susan’s line lived under federal occupation. Practical effects on the civilian population included an oath-of-allegiance system (the cost of retaining ordinary civil rights — selling crops, registering deeds, transacting at the courthouse), a naval blockade across the Chesapeake, illegal commerce with the mainland Confederacy, and confiscation of property belonging to men who slipped across to enlist with Confederate units (the “Eastern Shore Refugees,” organized as 4th Co. F of the 46th Virginia Volunteer Infantry under Capt. John H. White — about eighty men in total).

The military-age window 1861–1865 contains few of Susan’s documented ancestors. Edward S. Bull (1820–1856) was already dead; William West Rodgers (1785–1856) was already dead; the Bull family patriarchs were either dead or children. The two adult military-age men in the line during the war years were John K. Rodgers (b. 1823, age 38–42 across the war) and William Samuel Kellam (b. 1817, age 44–48). Both were almost certainly civilians under occupation; neither name appears in the published 1st Loyal East Virginia Volunteer roster (the only Union recruitment company that mustered on the Eastern Shore), and neither name appears in the published 39th Virginia Infantry or 46th Virginia Co. F roster as far as v1 has searched.

The slaveholding question is genuinely live for this line. The 1860 Slave Schedule for Accomack County records substantial enslaved populations — about 24% of Accomack and about 49% of Northampton in 1860. A line-by-line check of the Slave Schedule for the Bull, Kellam, Rogers, Boggs, Floyd, and Garrison surnames specifically has not been completed for v1. Edward S. Bull (1820–1856) had died before the 1860 schedule and his estate would have been administered by his widow Susan Elizabeth Kellam Bull; whether she held enslaved people at the moment of the schedule is a question for the Accomack County 1860 census records and would substantially affect the family’s relationship to the slaveholding system. This is one of the v2 priorities.

Joseph Saples Bull (b. 1850) was eleven years old when the war began. His childhood and adolescence — from age eleven to fifteen — passed under federal occupation. Mary Eliza Rogers (b. 1878) was born thirteen years after the war ended; her own life encompassed the post-Reconstruction Eastern Shore and the late-nineteenth-century rise of truck farming and the Pennsylvania Railroad’s Norfolk extension that opened the Shore to mass produce export. Fannye Bull (b. 1906) was a child of the truck-farming era and grew up in the second generation removed from the war’s daily impact.

The war is therefore present in Susan’s family as a period through which her ancestors lived rather than fought. The honest portrait is one of small farming families holding on through occupation, paying the oath-of-allegiance price, keeping their land through the worst federal wartime disruption, and emerging into the long structural decline that would eventually take the farm itself in the 1950s.

A separate Civil War experience exists on Susan’s father’s Dean Michigan line. Multiple Dean and Read ancestors of military age in Michigan during the war years — particularly William F. Dean (1814–1882, age 47–51 during the war, possibly too old for service), Francis Pearce Dean (1855, a child throughout), and the related Borden, La Grange, and Eisenlord cousins on the Read maternal side — would have been subject to Michigan’s Union recruitment. A Michigan-side Civil War search (Michigan Civil War regiments, particularly the 1st through 30th Michigan Infantry and the cavalry units that drew heavily from Oakland, Livingston, and Jackson Counties) has not been run for v1 and is a v2 follow-up.

12. The Eastern Shore Native American story — adjacent context

A note for completeness, since family memory often blurs which Native peoples lived where: Susan’s ancestors farmed in southern Accomack County, in a parish neighborhood that bordered the Gingaskin Reservation on the south and the lands of the Accomac and Accohannock chiefdoms further south. The Accomac were Eastern Algonquian speakers loosely affiliated with Powhatan’s paramount chiefdom on the Eastern Shore “fringe” and held a reservation at Indian Town in Northampton County from 1641. In 1813, Virginia became the first state in U.S. history to terminate a Native reservation: the General Assembly partitioned the Gingaskin reservation into 27 lots of 25.5 acres each and granted individual fee-simple ownership to recognized Gingaskin members. Three-quarters of the allottees retained their lands until 1831, when most were forced out following the Nat Turner insurrection.

The Bull-Kellam-Rogers-Boggs-Floyd-Garrison ground in southern Accomack and the Gingaskin/Indian Town ground in southern Northampton sat within fifteen miles of each other in the same parish neighborhood, and their respective populations were neighbors for two centuries. Susan’s documented Native ancestor — Mary “Molly” Bays/Baze, per the family Bible — is recorded as Monacan, not Gingaskin or Accomac. The Monacan people were Eastern Siouan speakers centered on the upper James River Piedmont (the Bear Mountain region of Amherst and Rockbridge Counties, two hundred miles inland from the Eastern Shore), not Algonquian-speaking Eastern Shore people. The two are separate nations, and the Bible’s Mowhemencho geography is consistent with the Monacan story.

A second Native ancestry claim, on Brady’s paternal Clark family side, is documented in a separate paper (Monacan Heritage) and is also Monacan. The two claims arrive in Brady through different parents — his mother Susan and his father Dennis Eugene Smallwood — and are independent.
