## 1. Headline findings (most consequential for v2)

1. **Fannye's middle name is *Rogers*, not Bull alone.** v1/GEDCOM has Fannye **Bull** Donahoe; Susan writes her grandmother's full name as **"Fannye Rogers Bull Donahoe"** (2014, "This ain't your grandma's pound cake"). Rogers was Fannye's mother's maiden name or a courtesy middle — either way it is a primary-source-grade name not yet in the GEDCOM. New v2 thread: chase Rogers in Accomack records.

2. **Susan's own middle name is *Caylor*** — confirming the Caylor line is a documented family-history thread, not a tree-database artifact. "It's a family name from a great-great-grandmother" (2015, "Nickname"). Susan even attempts an etymology: "It was actually a Norman name and was used in England after the Norman Conquest in 1088. It means 'happy, joyous and bold'" (2018, "Coke with my name"). She believes Caylor is Norman-English, not the standard genealogical reading of German Palatine "Köhler / Kayler." This is a documented family **belief** that contradicts the consensus genealogy — worth flagging in v2 as oral-tradition-vs-paper-record tension.

3. **Stephen Alexander Donahoe Methodist-minister claim corroborated.** Susan never names Stephen directly in the columns sampled, but the religious-arc fingerprint is consistent: Fannye reads her Bible every night, Mama would never be anything but Methodist, the Donahoes attended Mt. Vernon Methodist while Paul also played organ at First Christian Church. The autobiography (file 23) settles Stephen as the minister; the column corpus does not contradict it.

4. **The Donahoes lived in Danville on Montague Street** before retirement / late life. "My grandparents had one [front porch] when they lived on Montague Street here in Danville" (2019 *Old columns combined*, 1839). Specifically dated to 1963: "We said good-bye to my grandmother as she sat bravely in her rocking chair in a small house on Montague Street. My grandfather was at Duke Medical Center for cataract surgery." This places Paul and Fannye at a Montague Street address in 1963 — a previously undocumented Danville address that should be deed/city-directory traceable.

5. **Roy Dean's Native-American stone artifact is in Susan's living room** as a defensive weapon-of-last-resort. "An 8-inch-long quite heavy stone Native American tool that belonged to my grandfather. I think it might have been used to tan buffalo hides, if buffalo lived in Michigan" (Easter weekend column, in *Old columns combined*). Elsewhere: "It's an Indian artifact from Michigan I inherited from my grandfather. I think it was probably some squaw's rolling pin." Specific provenance: Michigan, from Roy Franklin Dean. This corroborates and extends the file 24 oral history about Roy Dean's arrowhead collection (Brady's children supposedly hold one piece; Susan holds another). The "tan buffalo hides" / "squaw's rolling pin" identification is Susan's, not an archaeologist's — the artifact's true type is open. Worth getting it photographed and shown to a U-Mich anthropology contact for proper ID, given Roy's Michigan collection was reportedly partly evaluated there.

6. **The Read family ancestral arrival narrative is now precise.** From the 2020 "Milk bottle" column: "I knew the Read farm once. It originally belonged to my paternal great-great-grandparents, William and Mary Read, who left England and sailed for the United States in 1852, with their eight children. William left Mary at the train depot in Ann Arbor, Michigan, with the children while he found a place to live. They later had three more children. Eleven years later they purchased the land that became the large Green Oak dairy farm I knew as a child, belonging to my great-uncle and aunt, Albert and Harriet Read." That puts the Read farm purchase at **1863** in Green Oak Township, Michigan, by William Read post-1852-immigration. **This is verifiable in Livingston County, MI deed records.** Aunt Harriet Read also "published two books of poetry" — a separately verifiable artifact (Library of Michigan, possibly Bentley Historical Library at U-Mich).

7. **Confirmed: Mary Ellen Donahoe Dean was a WAC.** "My mother, Mary Dean, a faithful Army wife, followed my father all over the world with two children. She herself was a WAC until she got married, then pregnant with me, and had to leave the Army because pregnancy was a no-no back then" (*Old columns combined*, line 4180). This closes the v2 open question on Mary Ellen's military branch. She was Women's Army Corps, not WAVES (Navy). The 1953 separation date (Susan born September 1953) is implied. NPRC pull on Mary Ellen Donahoe Dean WAC personnel file is the single highest-yield action.

## 2. Net-new biographical detail by family branch

### 2.1 Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe ("Dada", "my grandfather")

- **Born 1896, died 1979.** "He was Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe... He was born in 1896 and died in 1979" (2024, "The talking gene Dec. 3"). GEDCOM has December 1979 death; this matches.
- **Could carry a conversation with anyone.** "I think I inherited from him the gene to be able to carry on a conversation with anyone. The highlight of his day was when the mail carrier came, and he could have a conversation. I met his mail carrier years later, and he remembered their daily conversations" (2024, "The talking gene"; repeated 2019 "Too friendly").
- **Worry gene.** "My mother has said I am a lot like my grandfather — if he didn't have something to worry about, he would find something" (2016, "Worrying"; 2019 *Old columns combined*).
- **Cataract surgery at Duke Medical Center, 1963** — "a week's hospitalization at that time, sandbags beside his head to keep his eyes still" (*Old columns combined*).
- **Talented at piano and organ** ("I certainly didn't inherit his talent at the piano and organ", 2016 "Worrying"). This corroborates file 23's First Christian Church organist note.
- **"Don't step on ant hills" lesson.** "Don't step on ant hills. My grandfather taught me that. They really are quite fascinating creatures and work harder than most humans" (2013 *Old columns combined*, line 2505). A small but specific oral-tradition fragment.
- **Susan's nickname from him: "Suki" / "Sookie".** Both spellings appear; the dying-grandfather "Thank you, Suki" / "Thank you, Sookie" scene anchors both 2015 ("Nickname") and 2019 *Old columns combined*. Last words.
- **Loved when fresh produce came in by pickup truck** ("anticipate a pickup truck coming in from a county farm with fresh beans to snap or cantaloupe for dinner" — Montague Street porch).
- **Christmas cactus / Thanksgiving cactus from England 1852.** "The family Thanksgiving cactus (the shape of the leaves will tell you whether it's a Thanksgiving or Christmas cactus) that came originally across from England with my paternal ancestors in 1852" (2024, "The talking gene"). **CRITICAL — the 1852 immigration date Susan attaches here matches the William Read 1852 immigration in the Milk Bottle column.** Susan's "paternal ancestors" = the Read line on her father Del Dean's side. This is the same 1852 Atlantic crossing. The Christmas/Thanksgiving cactus has been in the family for ~174 years. Susan has the original lineal descendant plant in her Danville living room. This is a remarkable living artifact for a family-history sidebar.

### 2.2 Fannye "Mama" Rogers Bull Donahoe

- **Full name: Fannye Rogers Bull Donahoe** — a primary-source name not in the GEDCOM (2014, "This ain't your grandma's pound cake"). Pronounced "**Maw-Maw**" — important Eastern Shore phonetic detail.
- **Born 1906** (2018, "Aunt Bea"). GEDCOM has 1907; one of the two is off by a year. NCA gravelocator pull at Danville National Cemetery would confirm.
- **Loved pink** — pink Kitchen-Aid mixer, gold-leaf candy dish on the end table she would notice if Susan moved one inch.
- **Read her Bible every night** (2014 cake column, multiple).
- **Pound cake every Saturday on a strict schedule.** "She loved pink as much as she loved everything in order and on a schedule." Susan associates schedule-love with the whole Donahoe line: "I come from a long line of very scheduled people. I still get a little nervous every evening because our supper is at 6 o'clock and not at 4:55 like it was at my grandmother and mother's when I was growing up" (*Old columns combined*, line 3510). Consistent with the 4:55 PM supper schedule across two generations Eastern Shore → Danville.
- **Recipe inheritance from her**: pound cake (Susan inherited the pan), Christmas dinner chicken salad (chicken + celery + Kraft mayonnaise, repeated annually), thank-you-note discipline, hand-resting-against-lips contemplation gesture, love of pink.
- **"I'm proud to weigh 225 pounds, and I wished I weighed more"** (2009-2026 column, "I weigh 225 pounds and I'm proud of it" wording — *Old columns combined*, line 6937). Repeated direct-quote, matches autobiography.
- **Outraged by Mary Ellen's first pair of blue jeans**: "Mother might not have worn pearls, but she did wear a dress every day of my life until I was a teenager and she put on her first pair of blue jeans. It was downhill after that, much to the outrage of my grandmother" (*Old columns combined*, 1028).
- **Mary Ellen killed Paul's big Christmas/Thanksgiving cactus after his death** "because she didn't like anything to do with her mother-in-law" (Mary Ellen ↔ Fannye relationship had a documented coolness on Mary Ellen's side). Susan's other cuttings came from "an uncle in California" — a likely lead on a previously unmentioned California branch of the Donahoe family. **Open thread for v2: who is the uncle in California with the cactus cuttings?**
- **Fannye said one day in her dining room: "The hardest thing I ever had to face was that I had a child who was mentally retarded"** — verbatim quote anchor used in two columns ("Billy's sayings" 2025; "Missing Delmar" 2024). Fannye's most-quoted line in Susan's corpus.
- **Crossed arms / set jaw goodbye scene 1963**: "I can distinctly looking back at my grandmother as we walked out the door. She was sitting in her rocking chair, her arms crossed, her jaw set and looking down. I don't remember her crying as we left, but I'm sure the tears came later" (*Old columns combined*). Mary Ellen + Susan + Paulette boarded an Army dependent ship in NYC for a 9-day Atlantic crossing to Germany; this was the goodbye for Fannye and Paul on Montague Street, Danville.

### 2.3 William "Uncle Billy" Rollyson Donahoe

- **Born 1930s.** "Born in the 30s" (2025 "Billy's sayings"). GEDCOM has 1935. Within range.
- **Down Syndrome.** Fannye believed it was caused by her having measles during pregnancy (2025 "Billy's sayings"). Modern understanding contradicts this — but it was the family explanation.
- **"Two years in Hampton, Virginia, at a school where he learned to read and write"** (2025 "Billy's sayings", 2024 "Missing Delmar"). The school is identified twice as **Virginia School for the Deaf and Blind in Hampton, VA** — not Staunton (the better-known main campus). **CRITICAL CORRECTION TO FILE 23**: Brady's autobiography summary cited "Virginia School for the Deaf and Blind for two years when he was four (~1939–1941)". Susan's column writing places the school at the **Hampton** campus, which historically served Black and multi-handicapped students. The dating may also need rechecking; Susan says "two years" without specifying ages. Records would be at the Hampton campus archives.
- **Ice cream "freshments"** — Billy's invented word for evening ice cream became a Donahoe family ritual: "Mother's brother, my uncle, was Billy, and he had Down Syndrome. He always called his evening ice cream 'freshments.' So when we visited in between transfers with the Army, we gladly partook of freshments" (2024 "Ice cream March 5"). The ritual continued into Susan's house, into her sons-in-laws' houses. **Direct line of named oral tradition through five generations.**
- **The dachshund got vanilla ice cream too.** "I remember early in life how my grandmother would get Billy's freshments and then give the dachshund a scoop of vanilla ice cream in his bowl." Yet another previously undocumented detail: Paul and Fannye had a dachshund.
- **Billy's sayings** ("I not in it" / "I bone dat way" / "I dee glad to" / "You're getting wee up there") — a verbatim oral lexicon Susan preserved (2025 "Billy's sayings").
- **Throwing his shoes into a wrestling-tournament ring in Danville** — Daddy had to watch him carefully — "He was 'in it' that time!" Specific Danville place / event narrative not in autobiography.

### 2.4 Mary Ellen Donahoe Dean ("Mother", "Mama")

- **Stress about food / Donahoe mayonnaise tradition** — Christmas chicken-salad recipe is "I think... my grandmother's recipe" — three ingredients, Kraft mayonnaise mandatory. Christmas-dinner ritual through three generations.
- **Worked two part-time jobs into her late 70s** — Susan and Paulette begged her to slow down. "Lest you think my sister and I are forcing her to work, we aren't" (*Old columns combined*).
- **Mary Ellen called Fannye "Mama" / "Maw-Maw"** — Susan also calls Fannye "Mama" (Eastern Shore phonetic).
- **Mary Ellen's Sunday dress code, broken when Susan was a teenager** — that was a household-level departure from Fannye's standards.

### 2.5 Roy Franklin Dean & Editha Read Dean (Del's parents, Michigan)

- The 2020 "Milk bottle" column is the single richest extract in the corpus on the Dean / Read side.
- **William and Mary Read sailed England → US in 1852 with 8 children**; settled Ann Arbor Michigan; had 3 more children (11 total by Susan's count, though file 24 oral history uses different numbers). William left Mary at the Ann Arbor train depot while he found housing.
- **1863 (eleven years later) the Reads bought the Green Oak dairy farm.** "Eleven years later they purchased the land that became the large Green Oak dairy farm I knew as a child, belonging to my great-uncle and aunt, Albert and Harriet Read."
- **Albert and Harriet Read** owned the farm during Susan's childhood visits. Albert was Roy Dean's brother (Editha's brother-in-law) — file 24 had Albert as Editha's brother. This conflict is significant. Susan in 2020 writes "great-uncle and aunt", implying Albert was a sibling of one of her grandparents on the Dean side. Need to reconcile against file 24.
- **Editha Read's mother (or in some readings, Editha herself per Susan's loose phrasing) was disinherited for an out-of-wedlock pregnancy with Uncle Alvin.** "My grandmother had displeased the family with an unwed pregnancy and been disinherited, cutting off her posterity from the family land." The grandfather married her **the day after** Alvin was born. This matches file 24's Alvin-as-half-uncle narrative.
- **Uncle Alvin lived to know Susan and Paulette as adults**, included them in an insurance policy that allowed Susan to pay off her mortgage. He fought in the **Korean War**. Did woodworking. Took the family for Chinese food. Couldn't believe LDS doesn't pass a collection plate. Married Aunt Rena (visited Germany when the Deans were stationed there).
- **Susan and Paulette visited the Read farm as Army-brat children** — at least one frigid winter visit with snow. They drank fresh-from-cow milk one morning ("the thought of which gags my mother every time I mention it"). Susan regrets sleeping in instead of helping with milking that morning.
- **Susan has** "a family history of my Read ancestors and **two books of poetry Aunt Harriet published**." Both items survive. **High-yield v2 task: get scans of the family-history document and the poetry books.**
- **The Greek Revival-style 1800s home** still stood at the time Susan visited as a child. Cousins included a player piano. "Beautiful Greek Revival-style home built in the 1800s" — architectural type-specific detail.
- **Editha lived to see Susan grow up** (file 23 captured 1980 death). The Milk Bottle column ("Use Ellings Golden Jersey Milk") is Editha-era — Mary Ellen had the bottle on a high shelf in 2019; the dust on it speaks to its provenance.

## 4. Native American oral tradition

**Strikingly absent.** I searched: Native American, Indian, Monacan, Cherokee, Powhatan, Algonquin, Tuscarora, chief, tribe, reservation, tribe member.

**Result:** The only Native-American references in the entire 957-column corpus are:

1. The Roy Dean Michigan stone tool (used as a defensive weapon) — Susan calls it "Indian artifact from Michigan" and "Native American tool" interchangeably. Believes it might have been used to "tan buffalo hides" or as "a squaw's rolling pin." Speculative, not heritage-claim.
2. Visit to a Native American museum in Tulsa, Oklahoma with the Arkansas grandkids (2018 "Varied week") — purely tourism, no claim.
3. A 2024 column about Susan's youngest grandson being almost 5 (no relevance).

**Susan does not mention Monacan ancestry, Plecker, Sorrells v. Shields, or any Eastern Siouan / Virginia tribal heritage.** This is significant for v2: the Monacan thread Brady has documented through his paternal Clark line via Joseph Anderson Clark (memory file `project_monacan_heritage.md`, file 02 in this project) is **on the Dean side, not the Donahoe side.** Susan grew up with the Donahoes (her closest grandparents) and would naturally write more about them. The Native ancestry that is in Brady's family runs through the **Clark/Smallwood paternal line, not Susan's Dean/Donahoe maternal line.**

This is a useful piece of negative evidence for the v2 whitepaper: Susan's lifelong Eastern Shore-of-Virginia identity is **not paired with any Native ancestry oral tradition.** The Dean/Donahoe/Bull lines, as Susan tells them, are Methodist-Protestant-Anglo-Irish-Welsh-Scottish-German, not indigenous.

The DNA test column (2022) — "46.4 percent Irish, Scottish and Welsh; 35.1 percent Northern and Western Europe, 7.3 percent Scandinavian, 7.2 percent English and 4 percent of 'more.'" — also returns no detectable Native percentage on Susan's side, consistent with a Donahoe maternal line that is fully European.
