## Headline finding

**The Donahoe surname in Brady's line is best read as Plantation-era Ulster Scots, not anglicized Catholic Gaelic Irish.** Five lines of evidence point this direction, none of them individually conclusive but collectively strong:

1. **Place.** Kilkeel, Co. Down — specifically the Kingdom of Mourne — was one of the earliest and densest pockets of Lowland Scottish private-plantation settlement in Ulster, beginning 1606 under Hamilton and Montgomery, and continuing through the 17th century. By 1635 (James's birth year), Kilkeel and the surrounding Mourne fishing villages had been a Scots-settlement zone for nearly 30 years.
2. **Name behavior.** The "Donaghe / Donahoo / Donaho / Donahee" spelling cluster across Pennsylvania and Augusta County records (1750s–1800s) is consistent with an oral-tradition surname being phonetically rendered by frontier clerks — a hallmark of Scots-Irish Presbyterian migrants, not anglicized Catholic Gaelic Irish, who tended to retain stable spellings once anglicized.
3. **Migration route.** Hugh Casslin Donaghe (1728–1809), the immigrant or first-generation American, lands in **Augusta County, Virginia** through the Pennsylvania–Shenandoah–Borden's Tract pipeline that defined Scots-Irish Presbyterian migration in the 1740s. Not the Eastern Shore Anglican-English route. Not the Famine-era Catholic route (which is a century later anyway).
4. **Religious destination.** The Augusta County Presbyterian network into which Hugh Donaghe married, sued, owned land, and died was the Ulster-Scots Presbyterian frontier — not a Catholic settlement.
5. **The Donaghe surname today.** Forebears.io reports only **2 Donaghe-spelled bearers in Ireland in 1880**, vs. 353 in North America. The "Donaghe" spelling is a US-frontier orthographic variant of the Ulster surname Donaghy (Mac Donnchaidh), not a stable Irish surname that was anglicized in place.

**What it is NOT.** It is *not* a Catholic Gaelic Ó Donnchadha line from Glenflesk Kerry, Ross Kerry, Cavan, Tipperary, or Limerick — those traditional septs are nowhere near Co. Down, and a Catholic Munster-or-South-Leinster Donoghue family ending up Presbyterian in 1740s Augusta County would be vanishingly unusual. The simpler reading is that the Donaghe family is a Plantation-era Ulster Scots family whose surname is best understood as a Scottish-origin Donachie/Donachy or a Lowland phonetic rendering of an Ulster Mac Donnchaidh that became Donaghe in colonial America.

**What we cannot prove.** Without (a) Co. Down 17th-century church records (largely lost) and (b) a Y-DNA Big-Y test on a male-line Donahoe descendant, the surname's pre-1635 trajectory is unrecoverable. The whitepaper should claim "Plantation-era Ulster origin" as plausible-and-best-supported, not "verified."

## Kilkeel and Co. Down in the 1600s

### The Kingdom of Mourne

Kilkeel sits in the **Mourne Mountains barony of County Down**, on the southeast Ulster coast facing the Irish Sea. The town's Irish name is *Cill Chaoil* ("church of the narrow"), and its medieval pedigree predates the Plantation by centuries: the area was the territory of the *Múghdhorna* (the clan-name from which "Mourne" derives), and from the 12th century onward it sat within the Gaelic lordship of **Iveagh**, ruled by the **Magennis** family of Rathfriland. (Sources: Mourne Mountains Wikipedia; Britannica Kilkeel; Ring of Gullion / Iveagh history.)

Crucially: **Mourne was not part of the official Plantation of Ulster.** When James I and his commissioners parceled out the six escheated counties (Donegal, Londonderry, Tyrone, Fermanagh, Cavan, Armagh) for confiscation and English/Scottish redistribution between 1609–1613, Counties Antrim and Down were **explicitly excluded** because they had not been part of the 1607 Flight-of-the-Earls forfeitures. Down's Gaelic landlords — the Magennises chief among them — were technically allowed to keep their land.

### But Down was settled anyway, by Scots, starting 1606

What officially excluded Down from the Plantation didn't stop private Scottish colonisation. The **Hamilton and Montgomery settlement of 1606** — two Ayrshire Scots, Sir Hugh Montgomery and Sir James Hamilton, who acquired half of the Upper Clandeboye Gaelic lordship from Conn O'Neill in exchange for helping him escape English captivity — is recognized by Ulster historians as the *first* substantial Scottish colonisation of Ulster, predating the official Plantation by three years. Their settlements concentrated in north Down (Bangor, Newtownards, the Ards Peninsula). (Sources: Discover Ulster-Scots, "Antrim and Down"; Hamilton-Montgomery settlement; Wikipedia Plantation of Ulster.)

The Mourne / Kilkeel area to the south was settled later and from a different patron base. Two parallel processes brought Scots and English into Mourne through the 1610s–1640s:

- **Sir Nicholas Bagenal's grant at Newry (1552)** had introduced English settlement decades before. Bagenal recruited from his Welsh estates; Trevors and other English-Welsh families arrived in the early 17th century. The Newry-Mourne corridor became a mixed English-Welsh-Scottish settler zone.
- **The 1607 Iveagh division** broke up Magennis lordship into 15 freeholds, of which the Magennises retained 13. The remaining two went to incoming British settlers, planting the seed of mixed Gaelic-British landownership in southern Down.
- **The Earl of Antrim's MacDonnell estates** (Randal MacDonnell, 1st Earl of Antrim) recruited 300 Presbyterian Scots families to his Co. Antrim and northern Down estates in 1607 — explicitly Scottish-Lowland Presbyterian, brought in to provide a tenant base.

By the **1630s** — when James Donaghe was born in 1635 — the Mourne/Kilkeel area was one of the **fishing-village zones where Scottish settlement is most strongly evident**, alongside north Down, the Ards Peninsula, parts of Tyrone, and east Donegal. (Source: Discover Ulster-Scots; visitkilkeel.com history.)

### Why Co. Down records are mostly lost

A practical note that bears on what we can ever prove: **the 1660s Hearth Money Rolls — the closest thing Ireland has to a 17th-century census — survive for Antrim, Armagh, Donegal, Londonderry, Monaghan, and Tyrone, but not for County Down.** Down's Hearth Money Rolls were destroyed (most likely in the 1922 Public Record Office of Ireland fire, with earlier losses). The 1631 muster roll for Co. Down (D1759/3C/1) survives in PRONI, but covers only able-bodied males 16+. Pre-1700 parish records for Kilkeel (Catholic, Church of Ireland, and Presbyterian) are essentially unavailable. (Sources: ulstergenealogyandlocalhistoryblog Hearth Money Rolls; PRONI muster roll catalog.)

**This is why James Donaghe's parentage is unrecoverable.** It is not because the Ulster origin is wrong. It is because Co. Down's pre-1700 documentary record was systematically destroyed in 1922, and the Plantation-era settlement of small-tenant Scots farmers and fishermen along the Mourne coast was the kind of population least likely to leave records that survived elsewhere (no Bishop's Transcripts, no church-court depositions for a man who lived and died on a coastal smallholding).

## The Plantation of Ulster framework

### What the Plantation actually was

The Plantation of Ulster (1609–1630s) was the organized colonisation of the six escheated counties — Donegal, Londonderry, Tyrone, Fermanagh, Cavan, Armagh — after the 1607 Flight of the Earls (when the O'Neill and O'Donnell Gaelic chieftains fled to Spain) and the 1608 O'Doherty rebellion (which gave the Crown legal pretext for confiscation). Land was granted to "undertakers" (chiefly English, who undertook to plant English Protestants), "servitors" (Crown veterans of the Nine Years War), "deserving Irish" (cooperating Gaelic gentry, mostly Catholic), and the **City of London Companies** (who got most of Co. Coleraine, renamed Londonderry).

### The Scottish layer

Of these settlers, **the Scottish were overwhelmingly Lowland Presbyterian**, drawn from southwest Scotland (Ayrshire, Galloway, Dumfriesshire, Lanarkshire, the Borders). The North Channel between Scotland and Ulster is only 13 miles at its narrowest, making Scottish movement to Ulster cheap and reversible — a meaningful cultural fact, because Scottish settlers retained close family ties to Scotland for generations and continued to be reinforced by new Scottish migration through the 17th and into the 18th century. (Source: Discover Ulster-Scots, "When?".)

Estimates of total British settlement in Ulster by 1641 range from 40,000 to 100,000, with Scots outnumbering English in counties Down, Antrim, and Donegal, and English outnumbering Scots in counties Londonderry, Fermanagh, Cavan, and Armagh. By 1660 — a generation after James Donaghe's birth — Co. Down's settler population was overwhelmingly Scottish Presbyterian in the north and east, and a mix of Scottish, English, and surviving Gaelic Catholic in the southern Mourne / Newry corridor. (Sources: Ulster Historical Foundation, "Movement of British Settlers"; Wikipedia, Ulster Scots people.)

### What "Ulster Scots" means religiously and culturally

The Plantation Scots were Calvinist Presbyterians, distinct from both English Anglicans (the "Old English" or "Pale English" who predated them) and from the native Gaelic Catholic Irish. Their cultural baseline:

- **Scots dialect** (Lowland Scots English), not Gaelic
- **Presbyterian Kirk** with its own Church-Session governance, not the Anglican Church of Ireland
- **Lowland and Border Scottish surnames**: Hamilton, Montgomery, Stewart, Boyd, Crawford, Campbell, Hamilton, Currie, Fleming, Buchanan, Steward, Galbreath
- **Tenant-farmer / fisher / weaver** social structure, not landed gentry
- **A culture of legal literacy** — Presbyterian Scots maintained tight community records (Kirk Session books, baptism registers), but these survived patchily

This is the cultural type that James Donaghe of Kilkeel 1635 most plausibly fits, and the cultural type that his great-grandson Hugh Casslin Donaghe carried to Augusta County, Virginia in the 1740s.

## Penal Era and anglicization — relevant or not for this line

A common framing in Irish-American genealogy is that surnames lost their "O'" or "Mac" prefix during the **Penal Laws era (1695 onward)**, when Catholic Irish surnames were anglicized under social and legal pressure. The 1695 Penal Laws explicitly aimed to "lessen Irish Catholic power, dismantle their culture, and anglicize Ireland." Among many other things, this period saw widespread loss of O'/Mac prefixes from Catholic surnames. (Source: Irish Cultural Centre Greater Boston; Wikipedia Penal Laws.)

**This framing largely does not apply to a Plantation-Scots Donaghe.**

- Plantation-Scots families never had an "O'" or "Mac" prefix to drop — their surnames came in already anglicized from Scottish Lowlands forms (**Hamilton, Montgomery, Currie, Donachie, Boyd**).
- The Penal Laws targeted Catholics. Plantation Scots were Presbyterian Dissenters; while they faced their *own* legal disabilities (Test Act 1704 excluded them from public office; tithe obligations to the Church of Ireland; marriage and baptism non-recognition), they were not the primary target of the surname anglicization pressure, because their surnames were not Gaelic to begin with.
- The Donaghe surname spelling cluster Brady's line shows (Donaghe, Donahoo, Donaho, Donahee, Donaghee, Donahue) is **not** a Penal-Era anglicization pattern. It is an **18th-century American frontier oral-rendering pattern**, where a Scottish-origin surname brought to Pennsylvania and Virginia was written down by clerks with no standard spelling and limited literacy among the bearers themselves.

The exception that *would* matter: if the Donaghe family was in fact a Catholic Mac Donnchaidh from Tyrone or Derry that converted to Presbyterianism after migrating into Down, anglicized the surname under Penal pressure 1695–1720, and then emigrated to America. This is possible. But there is no evidence for it, and the simpler explanation — Plantation-era Lowland Scots Donachie/Donaghie family — fits the geography (Kilkeel 1635), the timing (peak Plantation tenure), and the destination religion (Presbyterian Augusta County Virginia 1740s).

## The Donaghe / Mac Donnchadha / Donachie etymology problem

Three distinct surname origins all collapse into "Donaghe" in American records, and we cannot tell which one James Donaghe's family came from:

### Option A — Irish Catholic Ó Donnchadha (the Munster/South-Leinster sept)

Gaelic *Ó Donnchadha* "descendant of Donnchadh (brown warrior)." Multiple distinct septs:
- Glenflesk and Ross (Co. Kerry) — the most famous Ó Donnchadha septs, anglicized to **O'Donoghue**, **Donoghue**, or **Donohue**
- Cavan, Tipperary, Limerick — smaller branches

These are **southern Irish Catholic** lines. **None of them are in Ulster, and none of them are in Co. Down.** A Donaghe family in Kilkeel 1635 deriving from a southern Ó Donnchadha sept would require a 17th-century migration from Munster to Ulster — not impossible but unusual, and inconsistent with the Plantation-era population flow which was overwhelmingly Scotland-to-Ulster, not South-Ireland-to-Ulster. (Source: O'Donoghue Wikipedia; libraryireland Ó Donnchadha.)

### Option B — Ulster Catholic Mac Donnchaidh (Tyrone/Derry)

In Ulster, a parallel patronymic **Mac Donnchaidh** ("son of Donnchadh") was common in **Counties Derry and Tyrone**, anglicized as **Donaghy**, **MacDonaghy**, or **Donachy**. This is a Northern Uí Néill sept zone, claiming descent from the Cenél nEógain branch of Niall of the Nine Hostages.

**Donaghy is an Ulster surname** today found "almost exclusively in Ulster, particularly in Antrim and Tyrone." (Source: johngrenham.com Donaghy; Ulster Ancestry surnames.)

But here's the geographic problem: **the Mac Donnchaidh sept is concentrated in north-central Ulster (Tyrone, Derry), not in southern Co. Down.** A Mac Donnchaidh family in Kilkeel 1635 would represent a migration from the Tyrone/Derry zone southeast into Mourne — possible, but with the same documentary opacity as Option A. It would also imply a Catholic Gaelic family in a Plantation-Scots fishing zone, which is sociologically unusual.

### Option C — Scottish Lowland Donachie / Donachy (Atholl, Perthshire)

Clan Donnachaidh (the Donaldsons / Robertsons of Atholl) is a Highland Perthshire clan, but the **Lowland anglicized variants Donachie / Donachy / Donachee** are common in southwest Scotland and the Borders — exactly the catchment area for the Plantation-era movement to Ulster. A Lowland Donachie family migrating to Co. Down between 1606 and 1635 fits the Plantation-Scots template perfectly: Hamilton-Montgomery patronage, Lowland Presbyterian, settling on the cheap-tenant Mourne coast.

The forebears.io distribution data is consistent with this reading: the **Donaghe spelling appears only in tiny numbers in Ireland (2 bearers in 1880, all Catholic) but in larger numbers in the United States (353)**, suggesting the spelling itself is an American frontier orthographic invention, not an Irish stable surname. The Catholic Irish bearers in 1880 are likely entirely unrelated post-Famine emigrants, not survivors of a hypothetical Mac Donnchaidh / Ó Donnchadha sept that stayed in place. (Source: forebears.io Donaghe.)

### Without DNA, we cannot adjudicate

Brady's line shows Y-DNA R-M269 Western European (per file 06 of the related Monacan/Clark project). This is the haplogroup of **the great majority of British Isles male lines** — Lowland Scots, English, Welsh, and Irish Gaelic. It is consistent with all three options A/B/C and discriminates between none of them.

**A Big-Y or Y-DNA-37 test on a Donahoe-male-line descendant** would almost certainly resolve the question by matching to either:
- A Scottish Lowland Donachie cluster (proving Option C, Plantation Scots)
- An Ulster Mac Donnchaidh cluster (proving Option B, Catholic Gaelic anglicized)
- A Munster Ó Donnchadha cluster (proving Option A, Catholic Munster migrant)

This is the single highest-leverage research action available for the Donahoe surname.

## The 1700s migration route to America

Brady's line: **James Donaghe (1635 Kilkeel) → ... → Hugh Casslin Donaghe (1728–1809) → ... → Mary Ellen Donahoe → Susan Caylor Dean.**

Hugh Casslin Donaghe is the immigrant or first-generation American. The documented record on him (FSID LZ2C-G97; corroborating land records in Augusta County and werelate.org genealogies) places him in **Augusta County, Virginia by 1750**, with his first child Elizabeth born in Virginia c. 1750, his name first appearing in Augusta court records in 1754. He died in Augusta County 5 December 1809. (Sources: werelate.org Person:Hugh Donaghe; ancestors.familysearch.org LZ2C-G97; genealogy.com forum 32; geni.com 6000000003883328136.)

**The arrival route is the canonical Scots-Irish Presbyterian migration corridor:**

1. **Embarkation from Ulster ports** — Belfast, Larne, Londonderry, Newry, Portrush — late 1740s. Hugh Casslin Donaghe was 18–22 years old when he likely arrived, consistent with the 1740s wave that brought 12,000+ Ulster Scots to American ports.
2. **Disembarkation at Philadelphia or New Castle, Delaware** — the two primary 18th-century Scots-Irish ports. "Most of the Scots-Irish opted to disembark at New Castle, Delaware, during the 1730s and 1740s and settled western Chester County." (Source: Pennsylvania Scotch-Irish Centre.)
3. **Pennsylvania interim residency** — Chester County / Lancaster County Donegal Township area, where the first Ulster Scots settled in the 1720s. Werelate cites a 1723/24 Hicklin marriage in Chester County Pennsylvania involving Donaghe family connections, suggesting at least a generation of Donaghe presence in PA before the southward push to Virginia.
4. **Down the Great Wagon Road into the Shenandoah Valley** — the canonical 1730s–1750s Scots-Irish corridor. **Beverley Manor (1736 grant to William Beverley, in northern Augusta) and Borden's Tract / "Irish Tract" (1739 grant to Benjamin Borden, in Rockbridge)** were the two major Scots-Irish recruitment grants. "Beverley and Benjamin Borden both recruited Scotch-Irish settlers from Pennsylvania and Ireland." (Source: Ulster-Scots in Virginia; virginiaplaces.org Beverley Manor.)
5. **Settlement in northern Augusta County Virginia 1750–1810** — Hugh Donaghe accumulated 1,227 acres on Naked Creek (a tributary of the Shenandoah's North Branch) and additional tracts on the North River of Shenandoah. He bought from and sold to fellow Ulster-Scots Presbyterians (Baskin, Ritchey, McCutcheon, Frame, Chambers).
6. **Subsequent generations down the Appalachian spine into the Ohio Valley and West Virginia** — Hugh Donaghe's land speculation extended "as far away as Ohio." His descendants moved west across the Alleghenies into the Ohio River Valley, and by the time of Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe (b. 1896 Ripley, Jackson Co WV), the family had settled in Jackson County, West Virginia for at least one generation. Jackson County had been first settled by the LaRue family in 1797 and was officially formed in 1831; Scots-Irish, German, and English descent dominated the early settler pool. (Source: e-WV Jackson County entry; Genealogy Trails Jackson WV.)

**This is a textbook Scots-Irish Presbyterian migration trajectory.** Ulster → Pennsylvania → Augusta → Ohio Valley / West Virginia, on a 150-year arc from 1745 to 1896. It is the same trajectory followed by the Lewis family (Augusta's first settler family, also Ulster Scots), the Crawford family, and tens of thousands of others. The route is so canonical that any deviation would itself need explanation — and Brady's line shows no deviation.

## What the spelling-variations tell us

In the Augusta County records of 1750–1810, Hugh Donaghe's surname appears as: **Donaghe, Donaho, Donahoe, Donahue, Donehge, Donagha, Donaghoe, Donohue, Donahoo, Donahee.** This is not a documentary defect — it is a consistent pattern that tells a real historical story.

**The pattern is diagnostic of Scots-Irish frontier orthography, not Catholic Irish anglicization.**

Three observations:

### 1. The bearer's literacy was probably oral, not textual

In Plantation-Scots Presbyterian culture, surnames were passed down orally by the family but written down by external clerks (county clerks, tax assessors, deed scribes) who had no standard reference. When Hugh Donaghe walked into the Augusta County court in 1754 to be sued, the clerk wrote what he heard: the family pronunciation, rendered phonetically. Different clerks heard the pronunciation slightly differently and rendered it differently — *Donaho* one year, *Donaghe* the next, *Donahoo* the next.

### 2. The variation is *terminal*, not initial

Note the consistent first syllable across all variants: *Don-* or *Donagh-*. The variation is in the *ending*: *-aghe / -aho / -ahoe / -ahue / -ahoo / -ahee / -aghoe*. This is exactly the kind of variation you get when an unstressed terminal syllable in oral speech is rendered by ear: the second syllable carries less phonetic information than the first, so different clerks disagreed about how to spell it.

This is the opposite of the pattern you see with Catholic Irish anglicization, where surname changes are often *initial* (the dropping of "O'" or "Mac"), not terminal.

### 3. The American spellings drift back from "Donaghe" to "Donahoo / Donahoe / Donahue" over generations

Hugh Casslin Donaghe (1728–1809) was the immigrant generation, and his name in the records leans toward *Donaghe*. By the time of Hugh Casslin Donaghe Jr. (1793–1856), *Donnahoe* and *Donaghe* alternate. By Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe (1896 WV), the family has settled on *Donahoe*. This drift from *-ghe* (closer to the Scots-Lowland *-chie* terminal) toward *-hoe / -hue* (more conventional American spelling) is consistent with the family's surname being **Scottish-origin and gradually Americanizing**, not Irish-origin and remaining stable.

**Caveat:** Modern American Scots-Irish surname studies are clear that this pattern alone cannot prove Plantation-Scots origin. Catholic Gaelic anglicized surnames also sometimes drifted in American records. But the combination of (a) Co. Down 1635 origin + (b) Augusta County Presbyterian frontier social network + (c) systematic terminal-syllable spelling drift is the most diagnostic signal short of DNA.

## The Currie / Curry wife — Scottish or Irish-Gaelic

James Donaghe's wife is recorded on FamilySearch as **Elizabeth Currie / Curry** (FSID G7H6-SRJ, 1637–1687) of Ardee, Co. Louth. Her parentage on FamilySearch is 0-source (the listed mother "Gordana Gordia" has no records anywhere outside FamilySearch and is almost certainly invented), but her surname and place of origin are worth contextualizing.

### The Currie / Curry surname problem

Curry / Currie has at least three possible origins:

1. **Scottish, from a Midlothian place-name** ("Currie" near Edinburgh) or a Dumfriesshire place-name ("Corrie"). Common in southwest Scotland — exactly the Plantation-Scots catchment area.
2. **Scottish-Gaelic, from MacMhuirich** — a hereditary bardic family. This was anglicized to "Currie" beginning in the 18th century, so likely too late to apply to a 1637 wife's surname.
3. **Irish-Gaelic, from Ó Comhraidhe (Munster)** — the Curry surname in Munster Ireland.

There is a rough **rule of thumb** in Irish-American genealogy: *Currie is Scottish, Curry is Irish*. But the spellings are so often interchanged in American records that the rule is weak. The FamilySearch entry uses both. (Sources: selectsurnames.com Currie/Curry; Wikipedia Currie surname; houseofnames Currie.)

### Ardee, Co. Louth — a meaningful clue

Ardee in **Co. Louth** is significant because of its specific geographic position: **Louth was the northernmost county of the medieval English Pale, and Ardee was a fortified Pale border town** facing into Ulster. The Pale was the strip of fortified English-controlled land "from Dalkey in Dublin to Dundalk in Louth." Ardee was *inside* the Pale.

**This means a Currie family in Ardee in the early-to-mid 1600s is most plausibly:**
- A **Plantation-era settler family** (English or Scottish) moved into the Pale border zone for security and economic opportunity, **OR**
- A **transplanted Plantation-Scots family from Ulster** who relocated south into the Pale, attracted by garrison employment, trade, or fortified-town economic conditions.

A Catholic Munster Ó Comhraidhe migrant ending up in 1630s Ardee is sociologically less likely — the Pale was governed by English-Anglican administration, and a Catholic Munster family would have stayed south.

The Currie / Curry surname history is explicit on this point: "the earliest known Currie ancestors come from Ulster in Ireland, the family probably having established itself there during the Plantations of the Seventeenth Century. The name is Scottish, Anglicised from the Gaelic clan Mac Mhuirich." (Source: Currie/Curry Surname Meaning, History & Origin, selectsurnames.com.) Note: this is a generalization about the surname's most-common origin path, not about Brady's specific Elizabeth Currie.

### Best reading

The simplest reading consistent with all evidence: **Elizabeth Currie of Ardee Co. Louth was the daughter of a Plantation-era Scottish or Anglo-Scottish family settled in the Pale border zone**, intermarrying with a Plantation-era Scottish or Mac Donnchaidh family in Co. Down. The marriage of James Donaghe (Co. Down) and Elizabeth Currie (Co. Louth) — across the Down/Louth boundary — is geographically modest (a one-day ride), and culturally consistent with the mid-17th-century settler-population marriage market. **Both surnames point to Plantation-Scots / Scottish-Lowland origins more than to native-Catholic-Gaelic origins.**

This does **not** rescue any specific pre-1635 ancestor. But it adds weight to the Plantation-Scots reading of the Donahoe surname overall.

A **targeted PRONI search** — either via paid researcher (Ulster Historical Foundation offers this) or via a research-trip — could produce a 17th-century Donaghe in Co. Down that would either anchor or refute the Plantation-Scots reading.

### Medium leverage: Augusta County Pennsylvania predecessor records

Hugh Casslin Donaghe was in Pennsylvania before 1750. **Chester County / Lancaster County, PA records 1720–1745** could show a Donaghe / Donaho / Donahoo family from which Hugh emerged. Donegal Presbyterian Church session books, Donegal Township tax lists, ship passenger lists arriving New Castle DE 1720s–1740s. Pennsylvania State Archives or the Genealogical Society of Pennsylvania.

### Medium leverage: Augusta County primary records

Hugh Donaghe's will (he died Augusta Co. 5 Dec 1809) should be in the Augusta County Will Books. His wife Elizabeth Walker's family records may name her parents and her own family origin. **Augusta County Court Records, Will Book 9 (1810-ish)** and the *Chronicles of the Scotch-Irish Settlement in Virginia* (Chalkley) can both be consulted.

### Lower leverage but cheap: Currie / Curry surname research in Ardee Co. Louth

PRONI / National Library of Ireland / Louth County Archives. If Elizabeth Currie's parentage can be anchored to a documented Plantation-era settler family in Louth or southern Ulster, it would corroborate the Plantation-Scots reading of the marriage.

## Summary table

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Was Kilkeel in 1635 a Scots Plantation zone? | **Yes** — Scottish Lowland Plantation settlement of Mourne began 1606–1620s under Hamilton/Montgomery (north Down) and continued through the Earl of Antrim's settlement of Presbyterian Scots families. By 1635, the Mourne fishing villages were one of the most strongly Scots-settled zones in Ulster. |
| Does the Donaghe surname's Ulster geography fit the Mac Donnchaidh sept? | **No** — Mac Donnchaidh is Tyrone/Derry, not Down. A Mac Donnchaidh family in Kilkeel 1635 would represent an unusual eastward migration. |
| Does the Donaghe surname fit a Lowland-Scots Donachie origin? | **Yes, plausibly** — Donachie / Donachy are Scottish Lowland anglicized variants of the Highland Donnachaidh, found in southwest Scotland and the Borders, exactly the Plantation-era catchment for Co. Down. |
| Does the Penal Era anglicization framework apply? | **No** — Plantation Scots brought already-anglicized surnames; the Penal-Era O'/Mac drop targeted Gaelic Catholic surnames, not Lowland-Scottish surnames. |
| Does the migration route to America match Scots-Irish Presbyterian? | **Yes, exactly** — Hugh Casslin Donaghe's 1740s arrival → Pennsylvania → Shenandoah → Augusta County is the canonical Ulster-Scots-Presbyterian corridor. |
| Are the surname spelling variations Penal-Era anglicization or American frontier orthography? | **American frontier orthography** — terminal-syllable variation (-ghe/-hoo/-hue/-hee) is the diagnostic pattern of an oral-tradition surname being rendered by clerks, not of Catholic-Irish anglicization. |
| Does the Currie wife support the reading? | **Weakly yes** — Currie/Curry has Plantation-era Scottish associations; Ardee Co. Louth was a Pale border town with Plantation-era settler activity. |
| Can we prove the Plantation-Scots reading? | **No, not without DNA.** But the case is the best-supported of the available readings. |
| What single action would resolve the question? | **Y-DNA Big-Y-700 test on a male-line Donahoe descendant.** ~$449, ~6 weeks. |
