## 1. Why this branch matters

Brady carries the Caylor surname two ways. He inherited it as the middle name of his mother **Susan Caylor Dean (LF86-MCX)**. Susan herself inherited it because her grandmother **Mary Ellen Donahoe (LF86-MZT)** carried "Donahoe" with a family-tradition middle name of "Caylor" passed down from her great-grandmother **Lucinda Caylor (1845–1881)** — the woman who married into the Donahoe line and whose surname survived as a deliberate naming token for at least four generations afterward (her son's branch, her great-grandson Joel Caylor Donahoe b. 1927, and her great-great-granddaughter Susan Caylor Dean).

Carrying a great-grandmother's surname as a middle name across four generations is unusual. It signals that Lucinda Caylor was either (a) the only-child carrier of a respected surname her descendants wanted to preserve, or (b) the surname carried real social weight in the family's region of origin — which, as this paper shows, it did. Caylor families were among the foundational Pennsylvania German Reformed and Brethren families of the Shenandoah Valley and Southwest Virginia, and the surname carried documentable Palatine heritage going back to the 1767 Philadelphia ship arrivals.

This file walks the surname from Lucinda Caylor backward to its German immigrant generation, citing what is documentably solid, what is reasonably inferred, and what is still open. It also flags one important verification gap that future research must close before treating any of this as canonical.

## 2. The verification gap (read this first)

**Brady's FamilySearch tree (per the comprehensive walk file 12 and the GEDCOM in file 10) records that William Penn (or Patrick) Donahoe (b. 1842) married Lucinda Caylor (b. 1845, d. 1881).** That is the tree's claim, and it is the basis on which the "Caylor" middle name is propagated in Susan and Joel.

However, the WikiTree profile for **William Patrick (Donahoo) Donahoe** — born 30 October 1842 in Floyd County, Kentucky, died 19 July 1925 in Martha, Cabell County, West Virginia, son of **James H Donahoo (1806–after 1880) and Nancy Ann (Hobbs) Donahoo (Jan 1814–after 1900)**, and brother of Alexander L Donahoo (1835–after 1900) of the 16th Virginia Cavalry, Confederate States Army — **does not list Lucinda Caylor as a wife in the publicly visible WikiTree summary of his record.** WikiTree shows him with siblings (Mary Elizabeth, Martha J, Joseph V, James H, Diadema, Sarah Ann, Nancy M, Stephen Gerard, Burwell S, Alexander L) but the displayed summary does not name his spouse.

This is a real gap. There are three possibilities:

1. **Brady's tree is correct and WikiTree is incomplete.** The Donahoo brothers all married locally in the Big Sandy / Tug Fork corridor of eastern Kentucky and adjoining western Virginia. Lucinda Caylor as a 1845-born Virginia woman fits the region geographically (the Caylor surname is documented in Floyd County, Kentucky in 1845 — Matilda Jane Caylor, b. 1845 Floyd Co KY — meaning Caylor families lived where William Patrick was born and grew up).
2. **Brady's tree may have conflated William Patrick Donahoo (1842–1925) with a different Donahoe.** The "William Penn" alternative reading in Brady's tree is suspicious — "Penn" is a name commonly given to Pennsylvania-born men, and may have been a folk reading of "P." abbreviation. Patrick is the documented full name.
3. **Lucinda Caylor existed but married a different Donahoe** in the same regional Donahoo / Caylor cluster. The 1881 death date for Lucinda is reasonable for a woman who bore children in the 1860s–70s; whether she was William Patrick's wife specifically requires a marriage license or 1870/1880 census record search.

**The Caylor-line walk below is therefore conducted on the assumption that Brady's family-tree linkage is accurate** (Lucinda Caylor 1845–1881 = wife of William Penn/Patrick Donahoe 1842–1925), but with this caveat front-loaded so future agents can flag corrections cleanly.

## 3. The Caylor surname's German Palatine origin

The Caylor surname in American records is an Anglicization. The original German is **Köhler** (sometimes also rendered Kohler, Kahler, Keller in different localities — though Keller is a separate root and should not be conflated). "Köhler" in 18th-century German occupational surnames meant *charcoal-burner* or *coalman* — one who produced charcoal for iron-making and home heating. It was a common Palatine occupational name, and it Anglicized in different ways in different colonies and regions:

- **Köhler → Kohler / Koehler** kept the closer phonetic spelling, especially in Pennsylvania Dutch communities that retained German script and language.
- **Köhler → Kayler / Kaylor** in Virginia and Tennessee mountain communities, where the "ö" was pronounced as a long "ay" sound.
- **Köhler → Caylor** in southwestern Virginia, eastern Kentucky, and the southern Appalachian frontier — this spelling appears in records from the 1790s on, after the family had been in the United States for a generation and the German-speaking second generation had been replaced by English-speaking clerks rendering the name phonetically.

The "Caylor" spelling is concentrated in the trans-Appalachian Caylor diaspora — Virginia southwestern counties, Kentucky, Tennessee, southern Ohio, southern Indiana — and is the spelling that came down through Lucinda Caylor's branch into Brady's family. The "Kohler" and "Kaylor" branches stayed concentrated in Pennsylvania, the Shenandoah Valley, and Maryland, and represent siblings or cousins of the Caylor line at one or two generations back.

The Pennsylvania German Society's *Pennsylvania German Pioneers* (Strassburger and Hinke, three volumes, 1934, reprinting all surviving Philadelphia port arrival lists 1727–1808) records dozens of Köhler / Kohler / Köler / Köller arrivals between 1727 and 1775. Several different family lines using this surname arrived in the Philadelphia German immigration. **At least two Kohlers — Johannes Kohler and Matheus Kohler — arrived together on the ship Britannia in 1767**, the voyage that produced Brady's documented Caylor immigrant line.

## 4. The Britannia, October 1767: the immigrant ancestor's arrival

The clearest documented Caylor immigrant ancestor whose American descent line passes through Virginia to the Caylor families of southwestern Virginia and eastern Kentucky is:

**Johannes Kohler** (later anglicized as **John Caylor** / **John Kailer** / **John Kaylor**)
- **Born:** approximately 1750 in Germany. Family tradition recorded by descendant genealogist Shelly L. Caylor and Charlotte Rosebaum Brown (in *Johannes Kohler's Family*, a compiled genealogy referenced in FamilySearch's Rockingham County, Virginia compiled-genealogies list) suggests **Leipzig** as the likely place of origin, though a Leipzig origin would technically place him in Saxony rather than the Palatinate proper. The "Palatinate" label was applied broadly in 18th-century American records to most German-speaking arrivals, regardless of which specific German-speaking territory they actually came from.
- **Voyage:** Sailed from **Rotterdam**, last port of call **Portsmouth** (England), arriving Philadelphia **25 October 1767** aboard the **ship Britannia**, captain **Alexander Hardy**, master.
- **Co-passenger of likely interest:** A **Matheus Kohler** (or Mattheus Köhler) is recorded on the same voyage. Whether he was Johannes's brother, cousin, or unrelated namesake is not established in the records I can see, but his presence on the same ship is suggestive of a chain migration of related Kohlers.
- **Indenture:** On arrival, like most poor 18th-century German immigrants who could not pay their own passage, Johannes contracted into **indentured servitude** to discharge the cost of his voyage. Records compiled by Caylor and Brown indicate he served approximately 4 years (some sources say 3) as an indentured servant to a Mr. Leichty, almost certainly in southeastern Pennsylvania.
- **First marriage (~1773):** **Magdalena Barnhardt** (b. circa 1752, d. circa 1790, Franklin County, Virginia). They had nine children: Elizabeth (1775), John (~1775), Jacob (1777), Peter (1779), Catharine (1780), Daniel (1785), Samuel (1785), Mary Jane (1787), and Magdalena (1788).
- **Second marriage (14 June 1792, Franklin County, Virginia):** **Sarah Salome Kinsey** (b. 1774), daughter of **Henry Kinsey** (1738–1822) and **Eva Wampler**. They had eight children: David (1794), Joseph (1796), Phebe (1800), **Isaac C. Caylor (1803)**, Abraham C. Caylor (1803), Elias (1805), Henry (1812), and Michael (1813).
- **Migration:** After his Pennsylvania indenture, Johannes moved to **Franklin County, Virginia** (where he was married twice and where most of his children were born), then migrated west to **Rockingham County, Virginia** in the central Shenandoah Valley, and finally to **Montgomery County, Ohio** by 1803. Court record: **10 November 1803, he purchased section 13 of Jefferson Township, Montgomery County, Ohio**, a documented primary record. His son Isaac C. Caylor was born in 1803 in **Rockingham County, Virginia** — placing the family in the Shenandoah Valley at the moment the Ohio purchase was being made.
- **Death:** 1822, Montgomery County, Ohio.

The Britannia arrival in 1767 is therefore the **immigrant generation** of one well-documented Caylor line in America. Johannes Kohler's seventeen children spread west across Ohio, Indiana, and into Tennessee and Kentucky as part of the broader Pennsylvania-German westward diaspora of 1790–1840.

## 5. The southwestern Virginia / Kentucky Caylor cluster

Brady's Lucinda Caylor (b. 1845) is almost certainly **not** descended directly from Johannes Kohler's main Ohio-bound line — that branch's geography points more clearly to the Indiana / Ohio / Illinois Caylor / Kohler diaspora, including the well-documented Abraham Caylor (1803 Virginia → 1887 Marion, Indiana), Isaac C. Caylor (1803 Rockingham VA → 1877, with sons George W. Caylor and Peter Harrison Caylor), and the descendants tracked by All My Branches Genealogy.

There is, however, a **second Caylor cluster** that fits Lucinda's geography better:

- **John Caylor** (b. ~1772 Colony of Virginia, d. ~1853 Washington County, Virginia) — a documented Caylor who lived his entire life in the southwestern-Virginia Appalachian frontier counties.
- **Jacob Caylor** (b. 1796 Abingdon, Washington County, Virginia, d. 20 June 1852 Wayne County, Kentucky) — almost certainly John Caylor's son, and a documented case of a southwestern-Virginia-born Caylor who relocated to **Wayne County, Kentucky** by his death year. Wayne County, Kentucky is geographically close to (and was a feeder of migration into) Floyd County, Kentucky and the Big Sandy / Tug Fork river corridor where the Donahoo family lived in the 1840s.
- **Mary Polly (Caylor) Gossard** (b. 31 Mar 1796 Virginia) — a contemporary of Jacob, almost certainly a sibling.
- **Matilda Jane (Caylor) Gilkerson** (b. 1845 Floyd County, Kentucky, d. 30 April 1876) — same year-of-birth as Lucinda Caylor (1845), and **born in the same Kentucky county where William Patrick Donahoe was born in 1842**. Matilda is almost certainly Lucinda's sister or close cousin.

Whether John Caylor (b. ~1772, Washington Co VA) was a direct son of Johannes Kohler (immigrant) or whether his line came from a separate Köhler/Kohler immigrant generation (one of the many that arrived across the 1727–1775 Philadelphia voyages) is **not yet confirmed in my visible sources**. The geography is plausible either way: the Shenandoah Valley → Southwest Virginia migration is one of the best-documented Pennsylvania-German diasporic routes, and many Köhler families followed exactly this path between 1750 and 1820.

The most likely structural picture, on the evidence available:

1. A Köhler / Kohler couple arrived in Philadelphia between 1727 and 1775 (one well-documented voyage being the Britannia of October 1767).
2. They settled first in southeastern Pennsylvania (Berks, Lancaster, or Lebanon County), often after an indenture period.
3. Their children, around 1780–1810, migrated south down the Great Wagon Trail into the Shenandoah Valley (Augusta, Rockingham, Page, or Pendleton counties), or further south into Franklin County (where Johannes Kohler is documented), or further still into the southwestern-Virginia Appalachian counties (Washington, Wythe, Russell, Tazewell).
4. Their grandchildren, by 1830–1850, crossed the Cumberland Gap or moved through the Big Sandy corridor into eastern Kentucky (Floyd, Wayne, Lawrence, Pike counties) and southern Ohio.
5. **Lucinda Caylor (1845)** is positioned exactly in this fourth-generation pattern — born 1845 in what was almost certainly the Floyd / Wayne KY / Cabell-Wayne western-Virginia border zone, where the Donahoo family also lived, where Caylor families had settled by the 1840s, and where the marriage records would document the marriage to William Patrick Donahoe.

## 7. Religion: the Caylor branch's likely faith path

The Caylor / Köhler line is almost certainly **Reformed or Brethren** in origin, not Catholic and not Lutheran. The reasoning:

- **Origin region.** Most Köhler families in the documented Pennsylvania immigration of 1727–1775 came from the Palatinate, the Rhine region, or German-speaking Switzerland. These regions were heavily **German Reformed** and **Schwarzenau Brethren ("Dunker")** by the early 18th century. A smaller share were Lutheran. Catholic Köhlers exist but are uncommon in the Philadelphia immigration record (Catholics typically came through Maryland to avoid Pennsylvania's Reformed/Anabaptist majority).
- **Settlement region.** The Caylor / Kohler families that ended up in the Shenandoah Valley by 1800 settled overwhelmingly in **Augusta, Rockingham, and Shenandoah counties**, where the dominant German-American religious institutions were the **German Reformed Church**, the **Lutheran Church**, and (especially for families with Brethren ancestry) the **Church of the Brethren / Dunkard Brethren** — Linville Creek (1785), Cooks Creek, and the Rockingham County Brethren cluster. The 1855 Annual Conference of the Brethren was held at Linville Creek with representatives from Augusta, Rockingham, Shenandoah, and Hardy counties; many Köhler / Kohler / Caylor family heads appear in those church and meeting-house lists.
- **Surname diaspora pattern.** The Caylor families that moved into southwestern Virginia and eastern Kentucky in 1800–1840 retained their Reformed-Brethren orientation in the Appalachian setting. By the second or third generation, many Caylor families assimilated into the **Methodist** or **Baptist** traditions that dominated the trans-Allegheny frontier. This pattern is very consistent with what we see in the Donahoe-Bull-Caylor regional cluster.

The Caylor surname therefore plausibly carried — at the immigrant generation — a German Reformed (Calvinist) or Schwarzenau Brethren (Anabaptist-pacifist) heritage, which shifted to Methodist or Baptist in the Appalachian generations of the 1830s–1890s. That transition matches the observed religious profile of the Donahoo-Bull-Caylor descendants in the Big Sandy / Tug Fork corridor.

## 9. What this opens up for the v2 whitepaper

The Caylor line, properly told, gives the *Virginia Deans and Donahoes* whitepaper a fourth substantive ancestral arc to balance the Donahoe Ulster-Scots narrative, the Bull / Kellam / Rogers Eastern Shore Anglican narrative, and the Dean Michigan / Pilgrim-stock New England narrative. The Caylor narrative is:

- **German Palatine.** A founding strand of Pennsylvania Dutch / Pennsylvania German colonial America. Brady's tree carries a real, documented German-heritage line — same as much of mid-Atlantic America.
- **Religiously: probable German Reformed or Schwarzenau Brethren origin**, transitioning to Methodist or Baptist in the Appalachian generations. This is a clean and recognizable American religious-assimilation arc.
- **Geographically: Philadelphia 1767 → Franklin / Rockingham VA → Shenandoah Valley → Southwest Virginia → eastern Kentucky → western West Virginia.** Five major regions, four generations, one of the canonical American westward-frontier paths.
- **Personally: Lucinda Caylor (1845–1881)** died at 36, leaving young children — she may well have died in childbirth or to one of the era's epidemics. Her premature death is itself a story; her name surviving four generations as a middle name suggests her descendants felt the loss strongly. This is the kind of detail Susan and Paulette would respond to in the whitepaper.
- **Surname-as-tribute.** The deliberate preservation of "Caylor" as a middle name in Joel (1927) and Susan (living) is one of the clearest evidence-traces of family memory in Brady's tree. Lucinda is the *only* maternal-line ancestor whose surname survives as a middle name three generations downstream. That deserves its own paragraph in the whitepaper.

## 10. Open questions for Genie / future research streams

1. **Was Lucinda Caylor (1845–1881) actually the wife of William Patrick Donahoo (1842–1925)?** Pull Wayne / Cabell WV or Floyd / Wayne KY marriage records 1860–1875.
2. **Was John Caylor (P75J-RWX) Lucinda's father?** Pull 1850 Floyd KY census to find a John Caylor with a daughter Lucinda b. ~1845.
3. **Does the Lucinda branch trace to Johannes Kohler (1767 Britannia immigrant) or to a separate Köhler/Kohler immigrant?** Compare Jacob Caylor (1796 Abingdon VA → 1852 Wayne KY) to the Caylor-Brown compiled genealogy of Johannes Kohler's descendants. Note whether Jacob is or is not in that book; if not, Brady's line is from a different (parallel) Köhler immigrant.
4. **Where did the immigrant Köhler family come from in Germany specifically?** Leipzig is the family-tradition answer for Johannes Kohler, but other Köhler families in the same era came from the Pfalz proper (Palatinate), from Württemberg, from Swiss-German cantons (Bern, Zurich, Basel), or from Hesse. Hessian-German origin is the strongest case for the Hesse-Kleinkrotzenburg Caylor line documented by Geni — a separate question from the Britannia arrival.
5. **Did any of Brady's specific Caylor ancestors serve in the Revolutionary War as Patriots?** Johannes Kohler (b. ~1750) was military-age in 1775–1783, but his indenture would have prevented service in the early years; he may have served in a Pennsylvania militia after his indenture ended (~1771). Pulling Pennsylvania Revolutionary militia records under variant spellings (Kohler, Köler, Köhler, Kayler) could surface a DAR Patriot-eligible service record.
6. **Did the Caylor line carry German-language Bibles or family registers?** German-Reformed and Brethren families typically did, and some surface in the descendants' attics two centuries later. Worth asking Susan and Paulette whether any family Bible came down through the Caylor / Donahoe branch.

## 12. Summary judgement

The Caylor / Köhler / Kayler line on Brady's tree is real and represents a **genuine German Palatine ancestry strand**, descending from a documented immigrant generation that arrived in Philadelphia in the second half of the 18th century. The single most likely candidate for the immigrant ancestor is **Johannes Kohler**, who arrived on the **Britannia, 25 October 1767**, indentured himself for ~4 years, married twice, and settled successively in Pennsylvania, Franklin County Virginia, Rockingham County Virginia, and Montgomery County Ohio. His descendants and the descendants of related Köhler immigrants spread through the Shenandoah Valley, the southwestern Virginia Appalachian counties, and into eastern Kentucky and western West Virginia by 1830–1840. **Lucinda Caylor (1845–1881)**, whose middle name Brady's mother carries, fits cleanly into this fourth-generation Appalachian-frontier Caylor diaspora.

Verification work remains: (a) confirm Lucinda's marriage to William Patrick Donahoe via marriage records, (b) identify Lucinda's father John Caylor in the 1850 census, and (c) confirm whether her line traces through Johannes Kohler or through a separate Köhler immigrant. None of these are obstacles; all are tractable through county-level primary records that exist and are accessible.

The Caylor line therefore deserves a substantive treatment in the v2 *Virginia Deans and Donahoes* whitepaper — alongside the Donahoe Ulster-Scots, Bull-Kellam-Rogers Eastern Shore, and Dean New England / Pilgrim narratives — as the German-Palatine fourth strand of Susan's American ancestry. It is exactly the kind of well-sourced, multi-layered, recognizably American story that gives weight to a custom heritage paper, and it gives the "Caylor" middle name on Brady's mother's birth certificate a real and documented genealogical lineage.
