## 1. Onancock / Onley / Pungoteague / Pennyville / Hollybrook / Melfa / Craddockville / Cashville / Hacks Neck / Chesconessex — The Bull/Kellam/Rogers/Boggs/Floyd/Garrison Ground in Accomack

The southern half of Accomack County, where Brady's Bull, Kellam, Rogers, Boggs, Floyd, and Garrison ancestors lived from the 1660s through the early twentieth century, is a strip of low, sandy, peninsular country lying between the Chesapeake Bay and the Atlantic. Whitelaw treats every "ancient house" tract on that ground in his exhaustive 1,511-page survey, *Virginia's Eastern Shore: A History of Northampton and Accomack Counties* (Virginia Historical Society, 1951; Peter Smith reprint 1968) — a parcel-by-parcel reconstruction of land patents, conveyances, and standing structures that took him more than fifteen years to compile (Whitelaw 1968, vol. I "For a Better Understanding," vol. II "Accomack County continued"; Worldcat 431201).

**Onancock** was founded by act of the General Assembly in 1680 as Accomack's first county seat, originally called "Onancock Towne" or briefly "Port Scarburgh" after Col. Edmund Scarborough — the latter name fell out of use within a generation (Wikipedia, *Onancock, Virginia*; Onancock Historic District nomination, 1992, Virginia DHR 273-0001). The Algonquian root *Onancock* is generally rendered "foggy place." The town sat at the head of a deep tidal creek opening onto the Chesapeake; it served as Accomack's commercial port for tobacco and (later) trucked produce well into the twentieth century (Whitelaw 1968, II:1141–1158).

**Pungoteague** — the name is from the Eastern Algonquian for "sand-fly river" — held Accomack's county court in two taverns from 1663 through 1708 before the seat moved to Drummondtown (later Accomac Town) (virginiaplaces.org, *Accomack County*; *St. George's Church (Pungoteague)*, Wikipedia). Pungoteague mattered for two reasons relevant to the Donahoe whitepaper: it was the seat of justice during the years Richard Kellam II was patenting and conveying land (1660s–1690s), and it became the seat of St. George's Parish (see §3 below) — the Anglican parish whose vestry territory contained much of the Bull/Kellam/Rogers/Boggs/Floyd/Garrison continuum.

**Onley**, **Melfa**, **Craddockville**, **Pennyville**, **Hollybrook**, **Cashville**, **Hacks Neck**, and **Chesconessex** are smaller communities scattered through the same parish district. Onley is best known as the home of antebellum governor Henry A. Wise (1856–1860) and is documented by virginiaplaces.org and Whitelaw 1968, II:1228–1234. Melfa (named in 1885 when the New York, Philadelphia & Norfolk Railroad pushed through) sits beside Burton's Chapel / Oak Grove Methodist (Wikipedia, *Melfa, Virginia*; Historical Marker WY-22, hmdb.org #7615); see §9 below. Craddockville is documented in the Eastern Shore Public Library's vertical file and as the cemetery context for Hollybrook Farm Burials (Find-a-Grave 2512594). Hollybrook itself is one of dozens of named farm seats in the Pungoteague-Craddockville district; Whitelaw lists its tract sequence in vol. II.

**Hacks Neck**, the long marsh-bordered peninsula projecting into Pocomoke Sound, takes its name from the Bohemian-born physician George Hack, who patented there in 1658; Whitelaw 1968, II:1252–1260, traces the descent of those tracts; see also W. C. Mariner, *Hacks Neck and Its People, Past and Present* (n.d., Indiana State Library cat. VWeyYOTeWpQ). The Department of Historic Resources blog post "Alice Boucher of Colonial Virginia's Eastern Shore" (DHR, 2019) documents that William Boucher leased 100 acres from George Hack at Hacks Neck in October 1658 — a useful early datapoint for the leasing-out pattern that defined the area's tobacco economy.

**Chesconessex** is named for the Chesconessex band of Algonquian-speakers who lived along Chesconessex Creek before English settlement (Salisbury University, *Lower Eastern Shore Native American History* libguide). The Chesconessex tribute-band is documented by Rountree & Davidson 1997 as one of the small client-bands tributary to Powhatan's paramount chiefdom on the Eastern Shore "fringe."

**Cashville** is a small unincorporated locality not separately treated in Whitelaw or in the standard published gazetteers; the name appears on USGS topo maps and in twentieth-century Eastern Shore directories but does not surface in the seventeenth- or eighteenth-century record. Treat as a post-railroad-era name pinned to a much older settlement matrix (Eastern Shore Public Library Heritage Center vertical files would be the next stop; no published source for the place-name etymology was located).

**Land patterns 1665–1900.** Whitelaw documents that the Eastern Shore's seventeenth-century land grants were small to mid-size (200–1,500 acres) plantation tracts oriented to creek mouths, not the great river-front estates of the Tidewater proper (Whitelaw 1968, I, "General History"). The Accomack district was, as Lorena Walsh and others have shown, "one of the least productive regions for tobacco cultivation in Virginia and habitually received the lowest prices in the colony for its Oronoco tobacco" (cf. Hardin, "Tobacco Types and Their Regional Variation in Colonial Virginia," *Historical Geography* 9 [2010]; Walsh, *Motives of Honor, Pleasure, and Profit*, 2010). Eastern Shore planters compensated by diversifying earlier than the rest of Virginia — corn, livestock, fishing, salt-making — which is why the area's smallholders survived the eighteenth-century tobacco depression that broke many Tidewater families. The 290-year Bull-Kellam-Rogers-Boggs-Floyd-Garrison continuum on this ground is, demographically, exactly the kind of small-yeoman-farmer continuity Whitelaw's tract histories make visible.

## 2. Northampton vs. Accomack — The 1663 Split

The Eastern Shore was first organized in 1634 as **Accomac Shire**, one of the eight original shires of Virginia (encyclopediavirginia.org, *Counties*; co.accomack.va.us, *About the County*). In 1642–43 the General Assembly renamed it **Northampton County**, reportedly to honor the home county in England of Obedience Robins, the leading Eastern Shore burgess of that generation (Wikipedia, *Northampton County, Virginia*; virginiaplaces.org, *Northampton County*).

By the early 1660s the population had spread northward into what is now Accomack County, and the round-trip to the southern court was untenable. **In 1663, Northampton was split into two counties.** The northern half resumed the original name — Accomack — while the southern half retained Northampton (Wikipedia, *Accomack County*). The split also gave Col. Edmund Scarborough, Robins's aggressive northern rival, control of his own court (virginiaplaces.org, *Accomack County*).

The two counties were briefly recombined in 1670 by Royal Governor William Berkeley, re-created in 1671, and split definitively in 1673 (after Scarborough's death) with Accomack receiving the larger share of land (Wikipedia, *Accomack County*; Wise 1911, ch. 8–10).

**Implication for the whitepaper:** Brady's deep ancestors — Richard Kellam II (Accomack), John Floyd I "of Hog Island" (Northampton), John Parker Sr "of Mattapony" (Accomack), and the Coulbourne in-laws across the Maryland line in Somerset — lived in *both* counties before and after the split, and several owned tracts that straddled the line. When citing a colonial document, always check which county was extant at the date in question; a Kellam patent of 1660 is a Northampton record, the same family's 1665 tithe entry is an Accomack record (Nottingham, *Accomack Tithables 1663–1695*; LVA Library Guides, *Colonial Tithables*).

## 3. Colonial Anglican Parishes — St. Peter's New Kent, St. James Northam Goochland, Hungars Northampton, St. George's Accomack, Lynnhaven Princess Anne

The Church of England was the established church in colonial Virginia. The parish was a unit of both ecclesiastical and civil administration: the vestry registered births, marriages, and deaths; collected the parish levy; oversaw care of the poor; and processioned property lines every four years.

### St. Peter's Parish, New Kent (Mary "Molly" Kimburrow's 1703 christening)

St. Peter's Parish was established by the General Court of Virginia on **29 April 1679** (Diocese of Virginia; *St. Peter's Church, Talleysville*, Wikipedia). The current brick church was begun by William Hughes (carpenter) and Cornelius Hall (bricklayer) in 1701 and was in use by July 1703 — the third-oldest standing colonial church in the Commonwealth, and a National Historic Landmark since 2 March 2012 (Virginia DHR 063-0027). The parish retains its **Vestry Book (1684–1758) and Register (1685–1786)**; both have been published and are widely available (Boatwright Genealogy, "St. Peter's Episcopal Church"; stpetersnewkent.org, *Parish Records*). Mary Kimburrow's 1703 christening, if it appears in the Register, would be in the very first decade of recorded entries — and would confirm her birth in the established settled portion of New Kent rather than the upriver Monacan country implied by the later "Mowhemencho" Bible entry. **Action item: pull the 1703 New Kent register entry verbatim from the published Register.**

### St. James Northam Parish, Goochland (Mary Kimburrow's 1761 marriage to John Woodrum)

When Goochland County was carved from Henrico in 1727, the parish was set off as St. James Northam (north of the James) and St. James Southam (south). St. James Northam was led from 1750 to 1777 by Rev. William Douglas, whose meticulous record-keeping produced **the Douglas Register**, "an indispensable compilation of births, marriages, and deaths covering the period from about 1750 to 1797, with some miscellaneous records going back to 1705" (Genealogical.com, *The Douglas Register*; FamilySearch, *St. James Northam Parish*). Douglas's compilation also includes the Manakin-Town Huguenot lists and an index of Goochland wills 1728–1840. **Mary Kimburrow's 1761 marriage to John Woodrum is recorded in the Douglas Register** (per the Bull-Kellam family corpus; the underlying entry should be cited verbatim with page number once pulled). Douglas's authority on this entry is as good as it gets for eighteenth-century rural Virginia.

### Hungars Parish, Northampton

Hungars is the Anglican parish that covered most of southern Northampton — the ground around the former Accomac chiefdom and the dissolving Gingaskin reservation. The parish has had three church buildings on or near the present site. The first, built ca. 1646 on land bequeathed by Richard Vaughan in 1645 "toward the building of a house for God's service," stood about 150 yards north of Pear Plain on the west bank of Hungars Creek and was originally called Nuswattocks Church (hungarschurch.net; Virginia DHR 065-0012; *Hungars Church*, Wikipedia). The second was contracted to Symon Thomas in 1679 for 10,000 pounds of tobacco. The current brick building was begun in 1742, completed by 1751, originally over 90 feet long ("the longest Anglican parish church in the colony"), and physically shortened to about 74 feet in an 1851 renovation. It remains active in the Episcopal Diocese of Southern Virginia.

### St. George's Parish, Accomack (Pungoteague)

Accomack Parish was created in 1663 at the county split. **In 1762 the parish was divided in two by a line running bay-to-sea**, with the upper half retaining the Accomack name and the lower half becoming St. George's (genealogytrails.com, *Colonial Churches of Accomack County*; *St. George's Church (Pungoteague)*, Wikipedia; Virginia DHR 001-0040). A frame church stood at Pungoteague by 1678; the present brick cruciform building — flemish-bond walls 22 inches thick, gambrel roof, square-tile floor — was completed about 1738 and was known as the "Ace of Clubs" church for its unusual plan. Renamed St. George's in 1800 to match the parish, **it is the only colonial church remaining in Accomack County.** The original vestry books (1763–1787) survive at the Library of Virginia. The communion silver given by Queen Anne in the early eighteenth century is still in use (Historic St. George's Episcopal Church, "About"). For Brady's purposes: this was the Anglican parish in which the Kellam, Bull, Rogers, Boggs, Floyd, and Garrison families would have been registered baptisms / marriages / burials throughout the colonial period and into the early federal era — until the Methodist revival pulled most of them off the parish rolls (see §9).

### Lynnhaven Parish, Princess Anne (paternal Smallwood/Clark side — context only)

Lynnhaven Parish was set off from Elizabeth River Parish in 1643 as a parish of Lower Norfolk County; Princess Anne County (with Lynnhaven inside it) was carved from Lower Norfolk in 1691 (genealogytrails.com, *Lynnhaven Parish*; *Old Donation Episcopal Church*, Wikipedia; *Adam Thoroughgood*, Wikipedia). Lynnhaven River was reportedly renamed by Adam Thoroughgood (1604–1640) ca. 1635 after his birthplace at Lynn, England, when he patented ten square miles along its shores. The parish's first services were held in 1637 in Thoroughgood's house; the first church building rose on his land in 1639 at "Church Point." This is the parish that anchors Brady's *paternal* (Smallwood/Clark/Custis/Thoroughgood) ground per file 16 — included here only for cross-branch context, not for the Donahoe whitepaper synthesis.

## 4. The Eastern Shore Native American Story — Accomac, Accohannock, Gingaskin Reservation, Indian Town

The canonical scholarly source on this is **Helen C. Rountree and Thomas E. Davidson, *Eastern Shore Indians of Virginia and Maryland*, University Press of Virginia, 1997** (paperback Charlottesville, 1997; not UNC Press, despite the prior file 16 attribution). Rountree (Old Dominion) and Davidson (Jamestown-Yorktown Foundation) reconstruct the culture and history of the Eastern Shore Algonquian-speaking peoples from approximately A.D. 800 through the disbanding of the last reservation tribes in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Rountree is also author of *Pocahontas's People: The Powhatan Indians of Virginia Through Four Centuries* (University of Oklahoma Press, 1990) and is the recognized scholarly authority on Tidewater and Eastern Shore Algonquian peoples.

### The Accomac (Accawmacke) chiefdom

The Accomac were the southernmost of the Eastern Shore tribes, occupying what is now Northampton County. Their primary settlement, also called Accomac, sat near present-day Cheriton on Cherrystone Inlet (*Accomac people*, Wikipedia; Rountree & Davidson 1997, ch. 2). They were Eastern Algonquian (Powhatan) speakers and were loosely affiliated with Powhatan's paramount chiefdom — but as Rountree emphasizes, "His control over the Accomac was not as complete as his control over tribes within the core of Tsenacommacah; the Eastern Shore was on the 'ethnic fringe'" (Rountree & Davidson 1997; encyclopediavirginia.org, *Tsenacomoco*). This "fringe" status is part of why the Accomac were friendlier to the English than the upriver Powhatan — they had less reason to be loyal to Powhatan and a great deal of incentive to trade.

### The Accohannock (Occohannock)

North of the Accomac, occupying what became Accomack County, sat the Accohannock — Eastern Algonquian speakers with villages on the Annemessex River, Accohannock Creek, and the Chesapeake islands (*Accohannock Indian Tribe*, Wikipedia; Rountree & Davidson 1997). Rountree disputes the modern claim that the Accohannock were also known as the "Annamessex," writing in court testimony quoted by the Indianz.com 2018 report and the Baltimore Sun's 2018 coverage that "the Accohannock and the Annamessex in the 17th century were two entirely different sets of people."

### Gingaskin Reservation (1641–1813) — the first detribalization in U.S. history

In 1641 the Accomac accepted a 1,500-acre reservation patent from the colonial government for the remnant of their ancestral lands; **upon accepting the patent they began to be called the Gingaskin** (virginiaplaces.org, *Gingaskin Reservation*; *The Gingaskins of Virginia*, LVA UncommonWealth blog 2011; Historical Marker WY9; Rountree & Davidson 1997, ch. 6–7). Various legal and boundary disputes with English neighbors reduced the reservation to 650 acres by 1680. From 1792 onward the General Assembly mandated that Northampton County's court appoint trustees over the reservation; those trustees were largely indifferent and ultimately pressured the Gingaskin into agreeing to a partition.

**In 1813 the General Assembly enacted legislation dissolving the reservation, partitioning the land into 27 lots of 25.5 acres each, and granting individual fee-simple ownership to recognized Gingaskin members** (LVA EAD finding aid for Northampton County (Va.) Land Records Relating to Gingaskin Indian Lands, 1795–1815, vi04050.xml). This was **the first instance of the termination and legal allotment of an Indian reservation, and the detribalization of its members, in United States history** — half a century before the Dawes Act (Rountree & Davidson 1997; LVA UncommonWealth 2011).

Three-quarters of Gingaskin allottees retained their lands until 1831, when most were forced out in the wake of the Nat Turner insurrection and its accompanying anti-free-Black legislation in Virginia. The community persisted under the place-name **Indian Town / Indiantown**; today the area in the Cape Charles–Cheriton corridor of Northampton County contains farmed fields, woodlands, and Indiantown Recreation Park (Virginia DHR; co.northampton.va.us, *Historical Markers*). A 2021 *Eastern Shore Post* article documented an Accawmacke descendant suing to reclaim ancestral lands — evidence the community survives genealogically and continues to assert claim.

**Implication for the whitepaper:** The Bull-Kellam-Rogers-Boggs-Floyd-Garrison ground in southern Accomack and the Gingaskin/Indian Town ground in southern Northampton sit within fifteen miles of each other, in the same parish neighborhood, and their respective populations were neighbors for two centuries. The Gingaskin / Eastern Shore Algonquian story is *adjacent* context for the whitepaper, not direct ancestry; Brady's Native ancestor (Mary "Molly" Kimburrow) traces to the upper James River Monacan, not to the Accomac/Gingaskin.

## 5. Mowhemencho — Mary Kimburrow's Bible-Recorded Birthplace, on John Smith's 1612 Map

Mary "Molly" Kimburrow's family-Bible birthplace is given as **Mowhemencho**. This is one of five Monacan villages located by Captain John Smith in the Piedmont upstream of the Fall Line of the James River and labeled on Smith's 1612 *Map of Virginia* (engraved by William Hole; first issued separately in 1612, reprinted in *The Generall Historie of Virginia, New-England, and the Summer Isles*, 1624). The five villages were Monahassanugh, Rassawek, **Mowhemencho**, Monassukapanough, and Massinacack (encyclopediavirginia.org, *The Smith Map*; nps.gov, *Mapping the Chesapeake*; Library of Virginia, *John Smith's Map of Virginia and Its Derivatives* libguide; virginiaplaces.org, *The Monacan in Virginia*).

**Mowhemencho was located on the south bank of the James River, "probably covering some of the level area bordering the stream in the extreme eastern part of the present Powhatan County, between Bernards Creek on the east and Jones Creek on the west"** (virginiaplaces.org, *The Monacan in Virginia*, citing Bushnell). It is the same site that, by 1700, was being called **Manakin Town** by the French Huguenot refugees who settled there in 1700–1701 (huguenotmanakin.org; *Manakin Towne*, Griffith Preservation; Historical Marker, hmdb.org #21141). The Manakin-Town Huguenot settlement was directed by Governor Francis Nicholson and granted ten thousand acres "which had belonged to the Manakin [Monacan] Indians" (huguenotmanakin.org, *Journey to Manakin Town in 1700*). The settlement was constituted as **King William Parish** by act of the House of Burgesses in December 1700.

**The Monacan were not a Powhatan-affiliated people.** They were Eastern Siouan (Catawba/Tutelo–related), spoke a Siouan rather than Algonquian language, and were both trading partners and rivals of Powhatan when the English arrived in 1607 (huguenotmanakin.org, *Monacan Indians and Manakintown*; encyclopediavirginia.org, *Monacan Indian Nation*; virginiaplaces.org, *The Monacan in Virginia*). This is the same Monacan nation centered on Bear Mountain in Amherst County that Brady's paternal Clark line traces into (per the prior Clark/Monacan whitepaper — a separate branch from this one).

**Post-1660 displacement.** The Monacan began deserting their James-River villages near the end of the seventeenth century under sustained English encroachment (huguenotmanakin.org, *Monacan Indians and Manakintown*). By 1700 — when the Huguenots arrived — Mowhemencho was effectively a deserted Monacan town. **However, the Monacans continued to visit the site regularly: a 1702 report records Monacans returning to Manakin Town to hunt and trade** (virginiaplaces.org, *The Monacan in Virginia*). This continuing presence after 1700 is exactly the timeframe Mary Kimburrow's putative birth (1703) would have been in — an early-eighteenth-century Native presence on a notionally Huguenot-occupied site.

**Archaeology.** Specific archaeological excavation reports for the Mowhemencho site itself were not located in this research pass. The Smithsonian-sponsored excavations at Monassukapanough (1911 and 1931) and the 2001 University of Virginia documentation of long Monacan occupation at that site (1300–1670) provide methodological precedent but not direct evidence for Mowhemencho's footprint or material culture (virginiaplaces.org, *The Monacan in Virginia*). **This is a gap.** Whether a focused archaeological survey of the Bernards Creek / Jones Creek ground in eastern Powhatan County has ever been published is an open question requiring follow-up — Library of Virginia and Virginia DHR site-files are the appropriate next stop.

**Implication for the whitepaper:** The Mowhemencho identification is the strongest available evidence for a Monacan connection on Mary Kimburrow's birth side. It is *family-Bible* evidence (per file 16), not currently corroborated by a named primary source for the birth itself. The whitepaper should describe Mowhemencho carefully — what was documented about the village in 1612, what was happening there 1660–1700, what the 1700 Huguenot settlement displaced — without overclaiming the family-tradition birth into "documented Monacan ancestry." (See file 16 for the documentary status: the 1761 vs. 1727 marriage-date inconsistency is the key data-integrity question still unresolved.)

## 6. Pomfret Plantation — Col. William Coulbourne's 1679 Patent, Somerset County, Maryland

Pomfret Plantation is the historic-house tract Brady's Coulbourne ancestors held for nine continuous generations from 1663 / 1679 through 1921. The site is on the south side of the Great Annemessex River in present-day Marion Station, Somerset County, Maryland, between Jones' Creek (west) and Coulbourne's Creek (east) (Maryland Historical Trust property file S-82; *Pomfret Plantation*, Wikipedia; National Register Properties in Maryland NRID 822).

**The land was first patented to William Coulbourne in 1663 by Cecilius Calvert, 2nd Lord Baltimore.** Coulbourne re-patented an enlarged 1,400-acre tract under the name "Pomfret" in **June 1679** (S-82 architectural survey file; Wikipedia, *Pomfret Plantation*). Pomfret was **listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984.** The standing house at Pomfret today is a two-story, four-room-plan, gable-roofed frame house built between 1810 and 1830, with a two-story hyphen joining an early-19th-century kitchen wing to the main block — its primary historical significance is the well-preserved Federal-period interior woodwork (S-82; National Register Inventory Nomination Form, NPS Gallery 571eea0f-7960-4c4d-ba32-1e270f467e7b). The Coulbourne Family Cemetery is located on the property (Find-a-Grave 2554860).

**Coulbourne family ownership ran 1663–1921 — nine consecutive generations.** This is the longest single-family land tenure documented in Brady's deep tree to date.

**Quaker context.** William Coulbourne was a magistrate (a "captain who is a justice") of Somerset County, and his Quaker sympathies are well documented though his formal membership in the Society of Friends is not established. **George Fox visited Coulbourne's house in early 1672** during Fox's American journey. Fox's *Journal* records: "On the 17th day we passed eight miles to a captain's house who is a justice, whose name is William Colebourne, where we had service. And on the 19th we passed about nine miles among Friends and on the 20th day of the 12th month [Feb.], we had a precious and a glorious meeting at the same justice's house before mentioned. And there were many people of account, the judge of that country and the captain and the high sheriff of last year and the head secretary at whose house the priest preached" (quoted in WikiTree, *William Coulbourne*; *Secrets of the Eastern Shore*, "A weird, wild mystic"; Carroll, "300 Years of Quakerism" thirdhaven.org; Project MUSE, "Some Thoughts on George Fox's Visit to America in 1672," *Quaker History*; *History of Methodism* and Baltimore Yearly Meeting sources).

**No record evidence proves William Coulbourne ever became a Quaker;** but the Fox visit documents that he housed and protected Friends at a moment when both Maryland and Virginia were actively persecuting them — a substantive religious-tolerance datapoint for the deep ancestral picture. The 1672 Fox meeting at Coulbourne's house is also commemorated in modern Somerset County Quaker historical literature (vacouncilofchurches.org, *Baltimore Yearly Meeting*; thirdhaven.org).

**1687 Nanticoke treaty.** File 16 records that Col. William Coulbourne participated in the 1687 Nanticoke treaty. The Nanticoke were the Eastern Algonquian-speaking nation centered on the Nanticoke and Choptank Rivers in what is now Maryland's Eastern Shore. Coulbourne's role as a Somerset County magistrate would have placed him at the colonial side of any treaty negotiations involving the Maryland-Virginia Eastern Shore frontier. (The specific treaty document and Coulbourne's verbatim signature are an outstanding archive pull, likely held at the Maryland State Archives.)

## 7. Hog Island / Shooting Beach — John Floyd I's 1681 Patent, Northampton

Hog Island is a barrier island off the seaside (Atlantic) coast of Northampton County. It takes its name from the colonial practice of releasing hogs on the island to feed and reproduce — by the end of 1608 the population of hogs on the south side of the James (the original "Hog Island" near the colonial settlement) had grown from three to sixty, and the practice was repeated on multiple Eastern Shore islands (dwr.virginia.gov, *Hog Island: 1610*; *History of Hog Island*; *Hog Island (Machipongo, Virginia)*, Wikipedia). The barrier-island Hog Island was **first patented to a group of 22 English colonists in 1672** (a letter patent naming Henry Patrick, Thomas Hewes, William Mainey, and others). Subsequent patents consolidated the island into smaller numbers of hands.

**File 16 documents that John Floyd I "of Hog Island" (LRNC-CKK, ~1640–1687) patented 100 acres in 1668 and 2,200 acres in 1681 ("Hogg Island alias Shooting Beach").** The 1681 patent is the canonical Floyd anchor on the seaside Northampton ground. Whitelaw 1968 vol. II treats the seaside-island tracts in detail (cross-reference Whitelaw, "Hog Island Patents," and the Nugent *Cavaliers and Pioneers* abstracts of Virginia land patents and grants 1623–1800, vol. II, available open-access on archive.org). A relevant Hog Island deed records Floyd's surname as "Lloyd" — consistent with his Welsh origin per file 16.

**Why Hog Island mattered.** The island was a livestock-and-fishing operation more than a tobacco plantation; it was also a strategic perch overlooking the Atlantic-side approach to the Chesapeake. The patentees, including Floyd, were appointed to colonial offices (Floyd was constable of Northampton County in 1669) precisely because their seaside presence let them serve as eyes for the colony against pirates, French privateers, and unauthorized European traffic. The island's standing structures (none colonial-era survive) and its barrier-island ecology are documented in HABS VA-73 (tile.loc.gov pnp/habshaer/va/va2200/va2288) and the Virginia Coast Reserve Study, March 31, 1976 (govinfo.gov).

**Late 19th–20th-century history.** Settlers later concentrated at the village of **Broadwater** on Hog Island; in the 1930s a sequence of violent storms forced the village to be abandoned, and the remaining sheep returned to a feral state. The island is now part of the Virginia Coast Reserve, owned by The Nature Conservancy.

**Implication for the whitepaper:** John Floyd I represents the seaside-Northampton patentee class — Welsh-origin, mid-grant scale, local-office-holding. His descent through Nathaniel Floyd ↔ Berry Floyd ↔ Matthew Floyd Sr is the line that ties Brady to the *Bona Nova* arrival of 1624/5 (per file 16); Hog Island is the geographic anchor that grounds the surname in southern Northampton through the late seventeenth century.

## 8. Mattapany / Mattapony — John Parker Sr's 1660 600-Acre Patent, Accomack

**John Parker Sr "of Mattapany" (LY2W-JFR, 1634–1695) received a patent for 600 acres in Accomack County in 1660** and named the plantation "Mattapany." The land was north of the present Broadway Road and extended from the bay to a short distance east of the neck road (esva.net/ghotes, *Descendants of George Parker*; cross-reference Whitelaw 1968 vol. II for the parcel-by-parcel descent). In 1672 Parker received an additional 400-acre patent in Accomack, and in 1687 a 200-acre island off Matchatank Creek called "Parker's Island." His will is dated **9 January 1692** and was probated **19 September 1695**.

The "Mattapany" name is Eastern Algonquian — there is also a Mattapany in southern Maryland (Calvert County) that became the country seat of the Calverts and a fortified outpost during King William's War (Wikipedia, *Fort Mattapony*; colonialencounters.org, *Mattapany Summary*); the two should not be conflated. Brady's ancestor's tract is the Accomack Mattapany.

**Implication for the whitepaper:** John Parker Sr is documented as a 1655 immigrant to Virginia (transported by his brother George Parker), birthplace Southampton, Hampshire, England (per file 16). His 1660 Mattapany patent puts him in the founding generation of post-1654 Eastern Shore land-holders — early enough to receive a 600-acre original patent rather than a derivative grant from existing tracts. The Parker line then runs forward to Capt. William H. Parker (the Revolutionary-War DAR Patriot anchor identified in file 16). Mattapany is the geographic spine of that descent.

## 9. Religious Arc — Anglican Establishment to Methodist Stronghold

The Eastern Shore's religious history runs in three documented arcs: the established Anglican parishes (Hungars and St. George's; see §3 above), a Quaker presence from the 1660s (William Coulbourne's hosting of George Fox in 1672 is the canonical anchor; see §6), and a powerful Methodist revival starting in the 1770s–1780s that effectively reshaped Eastern Shore Christianity by 1820.

**Methodist revival on the Eastern Shore.** The standard published source is **Kirk Mariner, *Revival's Children: A Religious History of Virginia's Eastern Shore* (Salisbury, MD: Peninsula Press, 1979; reissued by Secant Publishing)** — Mariner is the historian who first emphasized the importance of the late-eighteenth-century Methodist revivals to Eastern Shore cultural development. The earliest documented Methodist convert on the Eastern Shore was **William Elliott (1754–1836)**, who was converted to Methodism by tradition in 1772 (LVA, *Dictionary of Virginia Biography*, "Elliott, William"; encyclopediavirginia.org, *Methodists in Early Virginia*).

**Harry Hosier and Thomas Coke preached on the Eastern Shore in 1784**, traveling Accomack County and preaching at Oak Hall, Chancetown, Locustville, Mappsburg, and the Drummondtown courthouse. Hosier was the first Black preacher to preach on Virginia's Eastern Shore (esvatourism.org, *Glimpse into the Eastern Shore of Virginia's Black History*; *History of Methodism in the United States*, Wikipedia). The Methodist Episcopal Church was formally constituted in 1784 at the Christmas Conference in Baltimore — and the second major Virginia revival ran 1785–1788, hitting the Eastern Shore hard.

**William Elliott's Sunday School (1785).** Elliott began a Sunday School in his home in 1785 — possibly the nation's oldest continuous Sunday School. The Sunday School moved to **Burton's Chapel** (Chancetown community, 1801) in 1816 / 1818, and to **Oak Grove Methodist Church (Melfa)** in 1870. Elliott was buried in the Burton family cemetery near Quinby in 1836 (LVA, *Dictionary of Virginia Biography*; hmdb.org #7615; Historical Marker WY-22; mearsmanagement.com, *Burton's Chapel*; Find-a-Grave 137272525, *Elizabeth Garrison Burton Elliott (1816–1898)*).

**The Bull family burial at Oak Grove Methodist Church / Burton's Chapel, 1898** (per the Donahoe family corpus) sits squarely in this Elliott / Burton's Chapel / Oak Grove lineage — it places the Bull line inside the Methodist mainstream of southern Accomack by the late nineteenth century. **The Burton-Elliott congregation is the religious anchor of the Bull/Kellam/Rogers/Boggs/Floyd/Garrison ground from the 1780s forward.** Cross-reference Find-a-Grave for specific Bull burials at Oak Grove and Burton's Chapel; pull the Mariner *Revival's Children* index for explicit Bull-family entries.

**St. Peter's Episcopal Parish (New Kent) remains active** in the Diocese of Virginia, as does Hungars in the Diocese of Southern Virginia and Old Donation in the Diocese of Southern Virginia (Lynnhaven Parish). Eastern Shore Anglican / Episcopal continuity is real but minority; the *demographic* religious history of the area from 1780 forward is overwhelmingly Methodist.

## 10. The 1665 Tithe List — Richard Kellam II "6 Tithes"

A "tithable" in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Virginia was a person on whom the colonial poll tax (capitation tax) was levied. The General Assembly defined tithables to include free white males age sixteen and over, all enslaved Africans age sixteen and over, and all Native American servants (male and female) age sixteen and over (LVA Library Guides, *Colonial Tithables*; FamilySearch wiki, *Virginia Taxation*; J. B. Robb, "Interpreting the Annual Virginia Tax Lists"). Tithables lists were prepared annually by parish-appointed enumerators ("listers"), who walked the parish writing down the heads of household and the number of tithables in each household.

**The 1665 Accomack tithe list survives** (one of the earliest surviving Eastern Shore tax records) and is published as **Stratton Nottingham, *Accomack Tithables, 1663–1695* (originally Onancock, 1931; reprint Heritage Books, ISBN 978-1-58549-262-6)**. The Nottingham compilation is the canonical published source for this corpus.

**Per file 16's prior research, Richard Kellam II appears in the 1665 list as "Richard Kellum 6 tithes."** A six-tithable household in 1665 Accomack is large — it implies five additional tithables in the household beyond Kellam himself. Five tithables in a 1665 Eastern Shore Accomack household most plausibly comprised some combination of: adult male servants (most likely indentured English or Welsh), one or two adult enslaved Africans (the Eastern Shore had begun importing enslaved Africans in significant numbers starting in the 1660s — between 1675 and 1695 "3,000 black slaves were brought in to the region," per the *Town of Accomac* historical overview), and possibly one or two adult sons of the household. Six tithables is at the upper end of the Accomack distribution for that year; it documents Kellam as a consequential household head, not a marginal patentee. **The exact 1665 verbatim entry should be pulled from Nottingham's compilation** — page citation pending — and reproduced in the whitepaper to anchor the Kellam continuity claim.

**Why tithables matter for the whitepaper.** The 1665 entry establishes Richard Kellam II's documented household standing in Accomack thirty years after the Eastern Shore's first English settlement, eight years before the definitive 1673 county split, and two years after the parish system's reorganization at the 1663 split. It is the earliest hard-edge documentary anchor on the Bull-Kellam-Rogers-Boggs-Floyd-Garrison ground for this branch.
