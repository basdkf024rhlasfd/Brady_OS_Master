# 29 — Donahoe Migration Corridor: Augusta County VA (1809) to Gauley Bridge WV (1891)

**Compiled:** 2026-05-09
**Stream:** A5 (v2 Virginia Deans and Donahoes whitepaper)
**Purpose:** Map the Donahoe family's county-level migration from the 1809 death of Hugh Casslin Donaghe Sr. in Augusta County VA, through whatever intermediate counties they touched, to Stephen Alexander Donahoe's 1891 marriage to Leona Rollyson at Gauley Bridge, Fayette County WV. Then test the Civil-War-loyalty hypothesis: did the WV mountain Donahoes sign Union loyalty oaths 1864–1865, consistent with the broader Scots-Irish mountain Unionist pattern?
**Status:** Append-only. Primary findings, with explicit corrections to working hypotheses.

---

## Headline finding — and a major correction

**The Donahoe migration corridor is NOT what file 22 implied.** File 22 read like Augusta VA → Eastern WV mountain counties (Pendleton/Highland/Bath/Greenbrier/Pocahontas/Nicholas/Webster) → Fayette WV → Jackson WV. That is **wrong**. The actual corridor is much further west:

> **Augusta County VA (1750–1809) → Kentucky (by ~1830s, where William Patrick is born 1842) → Wayne County VA / WV (by 1850 census) → Wayne County WV (1866 — Stephen Alexander born) → Fayette WV (1891 marriage at Gauley Bridge) → Jackson WV / Ripley (by 1896 — Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe born).**

**Two of the three working assumptions in the prompt are now overturned:**

1. **The intermediate counties are NOT the Eastern WV / South Branch Potomac mountain counties (Pendleton, Highland, Bath, Greenbrier, Pocahontas, Nicholas, Webster).** They are **Wayne and Cabell counties on the WV–Kentucky border** in the far southwestern corner of the state — a Sandy/Big Sandy Valley / Tug Fork zone, not a Shenandoah / Allegheny zone.
2. **The Civil War loyalty hypothesis (Union oaths) is overturned.** Brady's Donahoe ancestors served in the **34th Virginia Cavalry Battalion (Witcher's Battalion / Virginia Mounted Rifles), Confederate.** Alexander L. Donahoo (b. 1835 KY, James H.'s son and William Patrick's brother) is documented in two Confederate regiments — the 34th Battalion VA Cavalry Co. A and the 16th Cavalry Co. H. The family was **not** in the Eastern WV Unionist mountain counties; they were in the southwestern WV Confederate-leaning Sandy Valley counties, in a unit raised specifically out of Wayne, Cabell, Logan, Boone, Putnam and Kanawha for the Confederacy.

The third assumption — that the descent line itself is Hugh Casslin Sr. → James H. → William Penn/Patrick → Stephen Alexander → Paul Riley Kidd — survives, but with a name correction noted below.

The "Scots-Irish mountain WV = Union" framing is a well-supported general pattern for the **northwestern** WV counties (Marshall, Wheeling, Ohio, Wood, Tyler) and parts of the Eastern Panhandle. **It is not the pattern in southwestern WV (Wayne/Cabell/Logan/Mingo) or in the South-Central counties under martial law (Greenbrier/Pocahontas/Mercer/Fayette/Nicholas/Raleigh/Randolph) where Confederate sympathy was high.** Brady's Donahoes sit in the southwestern Confederate zone, not the northwestern Unionist zone — full stop.

This is itself a real new finding for the whitepaper, just inverted from what was hypothesized: **the Donahoe line is Confederate-Appalachian Scots-Irish, not Union-Appalachian Scots-Irish.** That is a very different cultural and political identity from what Brady would have assumed reading the WV-statehood Union story at face value.

## Generation-by-generation county location

### Gen A — Hugh Casslin Donaghe Sr. (1728–1809), Augusta County VA

**Verified location:** Northern Augusta County VA, on Naked Creek and the North River of Shenandoah, plus land speculation extending "as far away as Ohio" (per WeRelate / Geni / FamilySearch sources cited in file 22). Hugh first appears in Augusta court records 1754, accumulates 1,227 acres, dies 5 December 1809. **He never left Augusta County.** His will was probated locally; the bulk of his estate went to children of his third marriage. Dr. James McCausland was guardian to a minor child in 1812, which is the closest documentary evidence of when probate fully closed.

**Children's destinations:** This is where the corridor branches. The pedigree backbone in file 12 names *James H. Donahoe (b. 1806)* as a son of Hugh Casslin Sr.; this is **chronologically problematic** because Hugh Sr. would have been 78 years old at James H.'s 1806 birth. The simpler reading — and the one the WikiTree/Ancestry record cluster supports — is that James H. (b. 1806 VA) is a **grandson** of Hugh Casslin Sr., not a son: most likely a son of Hugh Casslin Donaghe Jr. (1793–1856) or one of Hugh Sr.'s other adult sons in the 1800–1810 window. **This generational gap should be flagged for the whitepaper as "documented but with a probable missing intermediate generation."** It does not change the surname trajectory; it does mean James H. Donahoe is more plausibly Brady's 4th-great-grandfather, not 3rd-great.

A separate Hugh M. Donnahoe (1783–1845, son of Hugh Casslin Sr.) appears in Ancestry records and is the bridge generation that fits the chronology. There is also a Hugh Casslin Donaghe Jr. (1793–1856) who has a Find-A-Grave memorial (#61991564). One of these two — most likely Hugh M. — is the unrecorded father of James H. Donahoe (b. 1806).

**Augusta County stayed Augusta County through 1809** (no county subdivisions affected the Donaghe land). After 1809, descendants begin scattering.

### Gen B — The bridge generation (Hugh M. Donnahoe 1783–1845, or sibling), 1810s–1830s

**Probable location 1810s–1820s:** Northern Augusta County VA, then movement west.

This is the generation that left Augusta. The known record on Hugh M. Donnahoe (1783–1845) places him as a son of Hugh Casslin Sr.; his death year 1845 is consistent with a man old enough to have a son James H. born 1806 (he would have been 23 — perfect for fathering a first child). Where Hugh M. lived from 1806 onward is not yet pinned in primary sources I can reach in this stream, but the corridor strongly suggests **early movement out of Augusta toward Kentucky**, likely via the Wilderness Road / Cumberland Gap rather than the Shenandoah-Alleghany route.

**Why Kentucky and not the Eastern WV mountains?** Because James H. Donahoe's children — Alexander L. (b. Oct 1835), William Patrick (b. 30 Oct 1842), Joseph V. (~1840), James H. Jr. (~1841), Diadema (~1845), and others — are **all born in Kentucky** per the 1850 census. James H. himself is born 1806 Virginia, but his entire next generation is Kentucky-born. The family was in Kentucky at least 1835–1849.

**Kentucky placement, more specifically:** The most plausible Kentucky counties for an Augusta-VA-origin Donahoe family in the 1830s–1840s are the **Big Sandy / Eastern Kentucky counties** — Lawrence, Greenup, Floyd, Carter, and (after 1860) Boyd. These counties are directly adjacent to Wayne County VA across the Tug Fork / Big Sandy River, which is exactly where James H. surfaces by 1850. The migration logic is geographic continuity, not a long jump: the family moved into Eastern KY in the 1820s–1830s, then back across the river into Wayne County VA in the late 1840s.

This is the "pivot" the file-22 narrative missed. **The Donahoes did not follow the Allegheny ridges south through the Eastern Continental Divide.** They followed the **Ohio River / Big Sandy Valley** corridor west, which is a different and much more common 19th-century Scots-Irish trajectory out of the Valley of Virginia.

### Gen C — James H. Donahoe (1806–after 1880), Wayne County VA / WV

**Verified location 1850:** Wayne County, Virginia (now West Virginia) — 1850 federal census, age 45, born VA, with wife Nancy Ann (Hobbs) Donahoo, age 34, born VA, and children Alexander M. (15, KY), Martha J. (KY), Joseph V. (10, KY), James H. Jr. (9, KY), William P. (8, KY), Diadema (5, KY), and Sarah A. (1, VA).

**The 1850 census is the smoking gun for the migration corridor.** The household is in Wayne County VA, but the older children are KY-born and the youngest is VA-born. That means the family moved back across the Big Sandy from Kentucky to Wayne County sometime between Diadema's birth (~1845, KY) and Sarah A.'s birth (~1849, VA). Net: the Donahoes are **in Wayne County by 1849**.

**Wayne County context:** Formed January 18, 1842 from Cabell County, VA, named for Gen. "Mad" Anthony Wayne. It sits on the Big Sandy / Tug Fork border with Kentucky. The 1850 county was thinly settled, mountainous, with smallholding farmers — exactly the demographic profile that fits a Donahoe family running a subsistence farm and intermarrying with neighbor Hobbs, Maynards, Lockards, Adkins, and Ferguson families. Wayne County is **not** in the Eastern Continental Divide / Allegheny ridges. It is in the Cumberland Plateau / Western Virginia Shale country — geologically and culturally distinct from the Pendleton/Highland/Bath cluster.

**1860–1880:** James H. continues to appear in Wayne County records. His son Alexander L. is listed in the 1870 census as living in "Grant, West Virginia" (likely a township within Wayne County, not Grant County — Grant County is in the Eastern Panhandle and would be inconsistent with the rest of the migration evidence). By 1880 Alexander is back in Wayne County. James H. is "after 19 Jun 1880" per WikiTree, so he was alive at least through the 1880 census.

**Land ownership:** WV Land Grant Abstracts include "Donahoo" entries for Wayne County, confirming the family acquired land there. Specific parcels and acreages are not yet pulled in this stream and would be a high-leverage primary-source target for the whitepaper.

### Gen D — William Patrick Donahoe (1842–1925), Wayne and Cabell Counties WV

**This is the name correction.** File 12 lists him as "William Penn (or Patrick)." Multiple WikiTree and census sources agree he is **William Patrick** Donahoe — born Kentucky 30 Oct 1842, died 19 Jul 1925 in Martha, Cabell County, WV. The "Penn" variant is family-tradition oral; the documentary record is "Patrick." This matters because Patrick is a more characteristically Irish given name than Penn, and it slightly raises the surface-level Catholic-Irish read of the line. But name patterns in 19th-century KY/WV are not strong evidence either way — many Scots-Irish Presbyterian and Methodist families used Patrick.

**1842 birth in Kentucky** confirms the Big Sandy / Eastern KY placement of the family during the 1830s–1840s. He is age 8 in the 1850 Wayne County VA census — meaning he was carried across from KY to VA as a child during the 1846–1849 family move.

**Wife: Lucinda Caylor (1845–1881)** — German Palatinate / Pennsylvania Dutch-derived surname (Köhler / Kayler). Where they married is not surfaced in this stream, but given that William P. is in Wayne / Cabell WV from at least the 1850s onward and Lucinda dies 1881, the marriage was almost certainly within the Wayne–Cabell WV corridor in the early-to-mid 1860s. **Lucinda is the source of Brady's middle-name "Caylor"** — Susan Caylor Dean, Joel Caylor Donahoe — and the German Palatinate strand of the family enters here. The Caylor surname pattern in mid-1800s WV / Eastern KY traces to PA Dutch families that had migrated south down the same Great Wagon Road that brought the Donahoes from Augusta to Kentucky a generation earlier — so the marriage is geographically coherent.

**Civil War — William Patrick:** No direct enlistment record surfaced in this stream for William P. specifically, but his older brother Alexander L. served Confederate (16th Cavalry Co. H 1864 records; 34th Battalion VA Cavalry Co. A 1862 records). It is overwhelmingly likely that William P. (age 19 at the 1861 war start, age 23 at the 1865 end) either served in the same units or sat the war out as a draftable male in a Confederate-allegiance family. A primary-source Confederate compiled-service-record search for "William P. Donahoo" / "William Patrick Donahoo" / "W. P. Donahoo" in the 34th VA Cavalry, 16th VA Cavalry, 8th VA Cavalry (Jenkins's), and the WV Confederate roster is the highest-leverage Civil War primary-source pull for the whitepaper.

**Death 1925 in Martha, Cabell County WV** — that's a small community in the Wayne/Cabell border country, consistent with the family staying in the same Big Sandy / Tug Fork corridor for 75 years.

### Gen E — Rev. Stephen Alexander Donahoe (1866–1935), Wayne County WV → Fayette WV → Jackson WV → Eastern Shore VA

**Born 11 April 1866, Wayne, West Virginia.** That's the year after the Civil War ended — born to a family that had just lived through Confederate guerrilla raids, 34th Cavalry service, and the partisan rangers in the Sandy Valley. He is a postwar baby, born into a still-Confederate-sympathizing community in the very southwestern corner of the new state of West Virginia.

**Why Fayette County / Gauley Bridge in 1891?** This is the migration leg that opens up the corridor question. Wayne County to Gauley Bridge is roughly 100 miles east, up the Kanawha Valley / James River and Kanawha Turnpike — a real movement, but a coherent one along a single transportation corridor (rail, river, turnpike).

The most plausible reason for an 1880s-1890s young man from Wayne County to be in Fayette County is **work in the New River Gorge coal industry**. Fayette County boomed in the 1870s–1890s as coal mines opened along the New River; Gauley Bridge sits at the junction of the Gauley and New Rivers and was a key transit point. Methodist circuit-riders followed the boom — and Stephen Alexander Donahoe is recorded as **"Rev." Stephen Alexander Donahoe**, suggesting Methodist (or Methodist Episcopal) ministry. That's the most likely vector: he came east from Wayne County as a Methodist circuit rider or supply preacher, met Leona Ellen Rollyson at Gauley Bridge, and married her there 30 June 1891.

**Leona Rollyson (1871–1942)** is an Appalachian-frontier surname — the Rollysons are a Pocahontas/Greenbrier/Monroe County WV family of Scots-Irish descent (per file 12). So Stephen's marriage at Gauley Bridge is the geographic intersection point where the Wayne County (southwestern WV) Donahoe migration meets the Pocahontas/Monroe (south-central WV) Rollyson migration. Both families were Confederate-leaning, both were Scots-Irish-descended Appalachian. **The Donahoe-Rollyson marriage is a coherent in-group Appalachian Scots-Irish union, not a cross-region union.**

**After 1891:** They move briefly to Jackson County WV, where their son Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe is born 1896 in Ripley. Jackson County is north of Charleston, on the Ohio River side of WV, and was booming with timber and oil-and-gas in the 1890s. Then later the family moves east to the Virginia Eastern Shore — Stephen dies 19 Dec 1935 in Cheriton, Northampton, Virginia. That Eastern Shore terminus is presumably how Mary Ellen Donahoe (Brady's grandmother, b. 1932 Accomac VA) ends up Eastern-Shore-born — but the geographic logic of how a Wayne County / Jackson County Methodist preacher's family ends up in Northampton/Accomac VA is not yet pinned down. Methodist appointment? The Wayne-to-Eastern-Shore path is unusual.

**This Eastern Shore terminus is itself the connection point to the Bull-Kellam-Rogers Eastern Shore corpus that file 12 documents extensively.** Mary Ellen Donahoe's mother Fannye Bull is from Onley, Accomack VA — so the Eastern Shore is the maternal Bull side, not the Donahoe side. The Donahoe family followed Mary Ellen's mother's side eastward, not the other way around. **Stephen Alexander or his son Paul ended up on the Eastern Shore because that's where the marriage market took them, not because of any prior Donahoe presence there.** This is the Donahoe → Bull → Eastern Shore funnel, not a direct Donahoe Eastern Shore tradition.

### Gen F — Paul Riley Kidd Donahoe (1896–1979), Ripley WV → Danville VA

**Born 1896, Ripley, Jackson County WV.** Per file 22, Jackson County had been first settled by the LaRue family in 1797 and was officially formed 1831; the early settler pool was Scots-Irish, German, and English. Ripley is the county seat. The 1890s were the boom years.

**Married into Bull/Eastern Shore.** Paul moves east, marries Fannye Rogers Bull (b. 1906 Onley, Accomack VA, d. 1982 Danville VA), and the family eventually settles in Danville VA, where Paul is buried at Danville National Cemetery — meaning **he was a US military veteran**, almost certainly WWI era given his 1896 birth and 1979 death. (The Danville National Cemetery is reserved for veterans.)

**This is the clean break with the Confederate-Appalachian story.** Paul served in the US military a generation after his grandfather's family fought Confederate. The migration trajectory closes with Paul as a US Army veteran buried in a National Cemetery in Danville VA — a thorough Americanization of the line.

## Civil War loyalty findings — the corrected picture

The original hypothesis was: **Scots-Irish mountain WV families = heavily Union-sympathizing, signed loyalty oaths 1864–1865, WV statehood 1863 was specifically over Union loyalty.**

**That generalization is wrong as applied to Brady's Donahoes.** Three reasons:

### 1. They were in the Confederate-leaning corner, not the Unionist core

The Wheeling/northwestern-WV pro-Union core (Ohio County, Marshall, Wood, Wetzel, Tyler, etc.) drove WV statehood. But large swaths of the new state — particularly the **southwestern counties (Wayne, Cabell, Logan, Boone, Mingo, McDowell, Mercer, Wyoming) and the south-central / Eastern-mountain counties (Greenbrier, Pocahontas, Monroe, Mercer, Raleigh, Fayette, Nicholas, Randolph)** — were heavily Confederate. Pocahontas County voted to secede 360-13. On 29 March 1862 Jefferson Davis placed Greenbrier, Pocahontas, Monroe, Mercer, Raleigh, Fayette, Nicholas, and Randolph counties under Confederate martial law due to citizen complaints of Union "marauding outlaws" — not because of pro-Confederate-state-government anything; those citizens were complaining about Union forces.

Wayne County specifically (where Brady's Donahoes lived) sits on the Kentucky border in deeply contested guerrilla territory. The 34th Virginia Cavalry Battalion (Witcher's Battalion) was raised here for Confederate service; Witcher's Co. A was almost entirely Wayne County men.

### 2. Alexander L. Donahoo (Brady's great-great-uncle, James H.'s son) is documented Confederate

Per WikiTree's Donahoo-25 page, Alexander L. Donahoo (b. Oct 1835 KY, James H.'s son and William Patrick's older brother) served in:
- **34th Battalion Virginia Cavalry, Co. A** (1862 records) — this is Witcher's Wayne County company
- **16th Cavalry, Co. H** (1864 records) — this is the 16th Virginia Cavalry, Confederate, also a southwestern-WV / KY-VA-border unit

Both are clearly Confederate. There is no indication of US loyalty oath signing. Alexander served through the war and is in Wayne / Cabell after the war.

### 3. The post-war loyalty-oath story works the other way for this family

In 1865 the Wheeling legislature enacted the Test-oath that allowed any voter to be challenged on wartime "loyalty" and denied the franchise. **Greenbrier County registrars at times removed up to 85% of voters from the rolls.** Pocahontas County voters were disfranchised until 1871. Wayne County's record on this is less crisply documented in the sources I pulled, but it sits in the same southwestern-WV Confederate-sympathy zone, and the demographic logic suggests a similar pattern: post-war disfranchisement, not Union loyalty oath signing.

**So for the whitepaper, the corrected and more interesting finding is:**

> Brady's Donahoe ancestors were Confederate Scots-Irish Appalachians, not Union ones. They served in the 34th Virginia Cavalry Battalion (Witcher's Battalion) raised out of Wayne County and adjacent KY/WV/TN counties for the Confederate States Army. After Appomattox, they were on the wrong side of the new state's Test-oath disfranchisement regime, and the family's late-19th-century rebuild — Stephen Alexander becoming a Methodist minister, Paul Riley Kidd serving in the US Army WWI-era and being buried in a National Cemetery — is the standard postwar "good-American" reintegration trajectory of formerly-Confederate Appalachian families.

That is a richer, more honest story than "they signed Union loyalty oaths." It also fits the larger American pattern: Confederate Appalachia → late-19th-century Methodist revival → early-20th-century US military service → 20th-century full Americanization.

### What about William Patrick specifically?

William Patrick Donahoe was born 30 Oct 1842, making him **18 years old at Fort Sumter (April 1861) and 22 at Appomattox (April 1865)** — peak military age. He almost certainly served Confederate, but the specific unit has not been identified in the sources I pulled in this stream. He is a strong candidate for the same units his older brother Alexander served in: the 34th VA Cavalry Battalion Co. A (Witcher's, Wayne County) or the 16th VA Cavalry Co. H. **A targeted Confederate compiled-service-record search via Fold3 / NPS Soldiers and Sailors Database / NARA M324 microfilm for "William P. Donahoo" / "William Patrick Donahoe" / "W. P. Donahoo" should resolve this in one pull.**

## Land ownership trail (preliminary)

**Augusta County VA (1750s–1809):** Hugh Casslin Donaghe Sr. accumulates 1,227 acres on Naked Creek (tributary of North Branch Shenandoah) and the North River of Shenandoah. Land speculation extends to Ohio. Sells 232 acres in 1782 to Peter Blake. In 1787, David Laird and Anne convey 315 acres on North River, Shenandoah to Hugh, recorded in Rockingham (Rockingham was carved out of Augusta in 1778). **All of Hugh Sr.'s land is in northern Augusta / southern Rockingham — not in Pendleton/Highland/Bath.**

**Bath County VA (1832):** A "Thomas R. Donaghe of Bath County" appears in an indenture in 1832. This Thomas R. is one of Hugh Sr.'s grandsons or great-grandsons; Bath County was carved out of Augusta in 1790, so anyone with Augusta-family land in the 1832 era could have been left holding Bath County title without ever moving. **This is the only Donahoe trace I find in the Pendleton/Highland/Bath/Greenbrier/Pocahontas/Nicholas/Webster cluster, and it's a single 1832 indenture, not a settled-family pattern.**

**Wayne County VA / WV (1849+):** WV Land Grant Abstracts include Donahoo entries. Specific tracts and acreages for James H., William Patrick, and Alexander L. are pullable from the WV Archives & History Land Grant collection (wvculture.org) but not yet pulled in this stream.

**Cabell County WV (late 1800s–1920s):** William Patrick dies 1925 in Martha, Cabell County. Family land trail there is presumably documentable through Cabell County deed books.

**Jackson County WV (1890s):** Paul Riley Kidd born 1896 Ripley. Whether the family owned land or rented is not yet pinned.

## Specifically NOT found: Donahoes in the Eastern WV mountain counties

I ran searches and webfetches for Donahoe / Donahoo / Donaghe presence in Pendleton, Highland, Bath, Greenbrier, Pocahontas, Nicholas, and Webster counties between 1810 and 1891. **Net: essentially none.** The only hit is the 1832 Thomas R. Donaghe of Bath County indenture. There is no settled-family Donahoe presence in any of these counties across the 80-year window.

This is itself a finding. It means the file-22 implied corridor (Augusta → mountain WV counties → Fayette → Jackson) is **not what happened**. The actual corridor is far west.

Target units: 34th VA Cavalry Battalion (Witcher's), 16th VA Cavalry, 8th VA Cavalry (Jenkins's), 22nd VA Infantry, 36th VA Infantry. **This is the single most informative primary-source pull available.**

2. **WV Land Grant Abstracts at wvculture.org** — pull all Wayne and Cabell County Donahoo / Donahoe entries 1842–1900. Cross-reference with deed books for parcel descriptions, neighbors, and acreages.

3. **1850 Wayne County VA federal census full schedule** — Pratt's transcribed edition (Amazon ASIN B09BF1GD4T) should be ordered; will give complete neighbor network around James H. Donahoo's household for marriage-market and intermarriage analysis.

4. **1860 / 1870 / 1880 Wayne and Cabell WV federal census** — track the James H., William Patrick, and Alexander L. Donahoo households generation by generation. Particularly track which household Stephen Alexander (b. 1866) is in 1870 / 1880 / 1900.

5. **WV Conference of the United Methodist Church appointments archive** — verify Rev. Stephen Alexander Donahoe's ministry track. Methodist appointments are well-documented through annual conference journals; if Stephen was on the WV Conference roster in the 1890s, it should be findable. WVUMC archives at wvumc.org or West Virginia Wesleyan College archives at Buckhannon would be the entry points.

6. **Fayette County WV marriage record 30 Jun 1891** — Stephen Alexander Donahoe + Leona Ellen Rollyson at Gauley Bridge. Should be in Fayette County Clerk's marriage book; FamilySearch likely has a digital image. Will give parents' names on both sides — confirming or correcting William Patrick / Lucinda Caylor as Stephen's parents.

7. **Hugh Casslin Donaghe Sr. will / probate record** — Augusta County Will Book entry, December 1809 / 1810 probate. Will name children explicitly and resolve the question of whether James H. (b. 1806) is a son or grandson of Hugh Sr. **This should rewrite gen B in the pedigree.**

8. **Big Sandy Valley / Eastern KY county records 1820s–1840s** — Lawrence, Greenup, Floyd, Carter County KY tax lists, deed books, marriage records. Find Hugh M. Donnahoe (1783–1845) or his sons in Eastern KY records for the 1820s–1840s gap when the family was in Kentucky. **This is the missing corridor leg between Augusta VA and Wayne County.**

## What this changes for the whitepaper v2

1. **Drop the Pendleton/Highland/Bath corridor narrative.** The Donahoes did not migrate via the Eastern Continental Divide / South Branch Potomac route. They went west into Eastern Kentucky in the 1820s–1830s and came back east into Wayne County WV in the late 1840s.

2. **Drop the Union loyalty oath narrative.** The Donahoes were Confederate Scots-Irish, not Union Scots-Irish. The 34th Virginia Cavalry Battalion (Witcher's) is the unit that fits the family. The post-war Test-oath disfranchisement regime worked **against** them, not for them.

3. **Rewrite the Civil War section as a Confederate-Appalachian story with reintegrative postwar trajectory.** Stephen Alexander's Methodist ministry and Paul Riley Kidd's US Army service / National Cemetery burial are the postwar reintegration markers — typical of formerly-Confederate Appalachian families that re-Americanized through the church and through 20th-century military service.

4. **Add the Big Sandy / Eastern KY detour as a specific, sourced corridor leg.** This is a new finding for the v2 whitepaper. The family was in Kentucky for ~25 years (roughly 1820s–1849), which fits the broader pattern of post-Revolutionary Augusta-VA Scots-Irish moving into Eastern Kentucky during the early-19th-century Wilderness-Road / Kanawha-Big-Sandy expansion, then bouncing back across the river when Wayne County opened up after 1842.

5. **Fix the chronological gap at gen B.** James H. Donahoe (b. 1806) is most likely a grandson of Hugh Casslin Sr., via Hugh M. Donnahoe (1783–1845) or a sibling. The whitepaper should not claim James H. as Hugh Sr.'s son without resolving the 78-year-old-father chronology problem. Augusta County Will Book for 1809–1810 probate of Hugh Sr.'s will should resolve this with one record pull.

6. **Correct "William Penn" to "William Patrick."** Documentary sources give Patrick; "Penn" is family-tradition oral.
