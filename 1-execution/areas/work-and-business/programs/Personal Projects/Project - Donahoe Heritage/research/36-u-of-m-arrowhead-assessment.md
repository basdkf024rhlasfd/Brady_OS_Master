**Credibility — overall:** **2** (long shot). ## 1. The story we are trying to verify

Roy Franklin Dean (b. 1886, d. 1964) — Brady's paternal great-grandfather — collected arrowheads "over the years" from the Read farm on Rushton Road, Livingston County, Michigan. The family farm belonged to Editha Read Dean's brother Albert Jr. ("Allie") Read; Roy collected on visits across decades. At an unspecified date, the collection was taken to **the University of Michigan** for assessment. The assessment reportedly identified **one arrowhead as "very valuable,"** but no one in the family today remembers which one. The collection now sits with the Smallwood children (Brady's generation).

**What this means for archival search.** A "University of Michigan assessment" of a private arrowhead collection in the mid-20th century could plausibly mean any of four things:

1. **Formal accession** — Roy donated or loaned some/all of the collection to UMMAA, generating a deposit slip, donor file, and catalog entry.
2. **Informal expert opinion** — Roy mailed a few pieces or carried them to Ann Arbor; a faculty member (most likely James B. Griffin, UMMAA director 1946–1975) wrote back with an identification but never accessioned the artifacts.
3. **Visit-and-show** — Roy or a relative drove the collection to the museum on a single day, got an oral assessment, took the collection home. No written record survives.
4. **Misremembered institution** — the assessment may have been at a different Michigan institution (Cranbrook Institute of Science, the State Archaeologist's office in Lansing, or Michigan State College's anthropology program) and the family later collapsed it to "U of M" because that is the famous one.

Of these, **#2 and #3 are the most likely** based on how mid-century private-collection assessments typically worked. Outcome #2 leaves a paper trail at Bentley Historical Library; outcome #3 leaves nothing. Outcome #1 would leave records both at UMMAA (object-level) and Bentley (correspondence). Outcome #4 redirects the search.

## 2. The negative result, stated plainly

Online searches across the following terms returned **no direct hit** on Roy Dean's collection or assessment:

- "Roy Dean" + "Livingston County" + Michigan + arrowhead
- "Roy F. Dean" + Howell / Brighton / Hamburg + arrowheads (1940s–1950s)
- "Read farm" + Rushton Road + Livingston County + arrowhead OR archaeology
- Michigan Archaeological Society journal back-issue indexes
- UMMAA Digital Image Database / Online Collections public-facing pages

This is not surprising. UMMAA's online collections (~29,000 images per public materials) cover a small fraction of the museum's three-million-artifact total, and accession-level donor files for mid-20th-century private collections are almost never digitized — they live in physical folders at the **Bentley Historical Library** (which received over 200 linear feet of Griffin's papers in 2003 plus the broader Department of Anthropology records, 1927–1983).

The family's memory ("U of M arrowhead assessment") is the kind of provenance fact that archives **can** confirm or refute, but only via in-person or mail request to specific physical folders. The web does not surface it.

Griffin was UMMAA's director from 1946 through 1975 — the entire window in which Roy Dean (d. 1964) could plausibly have brought a collection to the museum as a living person. 13. **Topical Files** ← secondary target
14. **Archaeological Sites and Projects** ← tertiary target

The Correspondence 1930–1975 series is arranged alphabetically by surname of correspondent (judging by the Bentley's individual folder URLs: e.g., "Bell, Robert E., 1947–1975," "Ferdon, Edwin N., Jr., 1951–1967," "Lechler, George, 1937–1950," "Wheat, Joe Ben, 1948–1972"). **A "Dean, Roy" or "Dean, Roy F." folder, if it exists, would be in the D bin.** Pulling that folder is a single archivist request.

The **Topical Files** series and the **Archaeological Sites and Projects** series are likely organized by site or by Michigan county; a "Livingston County" folder, if one exists, would be searched there for any reference to the Read farm.

**Cost-of-search estimate.** A reference archivist at Bentley could check the Correspondence 1930–1975 D-folder for "Dean, Roy" in approximately 15 minutes of stacks time. That is the minimum-cost first move on this entire research thread.

### 3.2 Department of Anthropology (University of Michigan) records, 1927–1983 — Bentley

**Finding aid:** `umich-bhl-03100`. Department-level administrative records covering the same window. Less likely to contain individual private-collection correspondence than the Griffin papers (department files tend to be administrative rather than research-correspondence), but worth a parallel pull if the Griffin search comes up empty. Could contain: assessment-request log books, departmental correspondence routed through the chair, or staff reports referencing private collections.

### 3.3 Michigan Archaeological Society records, 1924–2019 (bulk 1956–2010) — Bentley

**Finding aid:** `umich-bhl-851849`. The MAS journal *The Michigan Archaeologist* (started 1953 as *Michigan Archaeological Society News*, renamed 1957) routinely published private-collection writeups and site reports authored by both professional and avocational archaeologists. A journal index search for "Dean," "Read farm," or any Livingston County collection report from the 1953–1965 window could surface a published mention. The MAS has chapter structure; if a Livingston County or southeast-Michigan chapter existed, its records may be inside this collection.

The journal's **back-issue index** is publicly listed at micharch.org and Penn's onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/serial?id=micharchaeol — Brady can scan that index without a Bentley visit.

### 3.4 UMMAA itself — accession records, donor files, catalog cards

**If there was a formal accession** (outcome #1 above), it would generate object-level records at UMMAA's Research Museums Center (3600 Varsity Drive, Room 1646, Ann Arbor, MI 48108; 734-764-8991). UMMAA's public site notes that **all collections-access requests must be submitted via a request form**, and as of late 2024 the museum was experiencing unusually high request volume with NAGPRA requests prioritized — meaning a non-NAGPRA private-collection provenance inquiry will be slower to process than a Bentley reference request.

**Practical implication:** start the search at Bentley (faster, cheaper, broader), and only escalate to UMMAA registrar if Bentley turns up a reference indicating an object-level deposit.

## 4. The specific arrowhead: what "very valuable" probably means

This is informed speculation, included to help any future archivist or descendant (Smallwood-children generation) recognize the piece if they pull it out of the family's collection box.

Livingston County, Michigan sits in the **southern Lower Peninsula**, an area worked by Indigenous peoples continuously from the **Paleoindian period (~12,000–10,000 BCE)** through the **Late Woodland and historic Anishinaabe / Three Fires Confederacy** periods (~1600 CE and forward). A working dairy farm with a generations-long collecting habit could have produced any of:

1. **A fluted Paleoindian point** (Clovis, Gainey, Holcombe types) — the rarest Michigan find and the single most likely candidate for the "very valuable" identification, because complete fluted points from secure southern Michigan farmstead contexts are uncommon enough that Griffin specifically would have flagged one.
2. **A Hopewell-tradition exotic-stone point** (Middle Woodland, ~200 BCE–500 CE) — Obsidian or non-local cherts from Indiana, Ohio, or further out indicate trade-network connection. Griffin's career was substantially built on Hopewell research; a Hopewell point would have been the artifact most likely to make him stop and write back.
3. **An Archaic-period side-notched or bifurcate-base point** in unusual material (Bayport chert, Onondaga, Flint Ridge) — common form, uncommon stone source.

The "Pop kept them because no one else cared" detail in file 24 suggests the collection is mostly typical Late Archaic / Woodland small-point material with one outlier. **A fluted Paleoindian point or a Hopewell exotic-stone biface is the most plausible "valuable one."** A Smallwood-child examining the box today should look first for: a thin, finely-worked point with a basal flute (a vertical channel-flake scar); or a point made from a stone color that does not match the rest of the collection (translucent pinkish-grey, deep blue-grey, or banded fortification chert vs. local Bayport grey).

This is observation, not identification. Any actual identification should be done by a present-day UMMAA curator, an MAS-affiliated lithic specialist, or a state archaeologist with the actual artifact in hand.

## 6. Honest confidence assessment

- Probability that Bentley's Griffin papers contain a "Dean, Roy" folder: **~10–15%**. Griffin received hundreds of identification requests; he kept some, didn't keep others, and surviving correspondence skews toward professional peers, not avocational private collectors. But the cost of asking is so low that the expected value is positive even at low probability.
- Probability that *Michigan Archaeologist* indexed a Livingston County collection report referencing Roy Dean or the Read farm: **~5–10%**. Higher if Roy was MAS-affiliated (oral history doesn't say); much lower if he was a private collector who never joined.
- Probability that UMMAA holds an actual Dean accession: **~3–5%**. Most assessment-only encounters never accessioned.
- Probability that the assessment was at a different Michigan institution and the family collapsed it to "U of M": **~20–25%**. Worth keeping the Cranbrook / MSU / State Archaeologist tracks alive as backstops.
- Combined probability that *some* surviving documentary trace can be located via Moves 1–4: **~30–40%**. Long shot, but not hopeless. The phrase "very valuable" the family remembers is precise enough — it sounds like a reported expert opinion, not a generic "wow nice collection" reaction — that *something* probably triggered the description.

## 7. What this stream cannot answer (intentionally)

This research file makes **no attempt** to:

- Identify the actual valuable arrowhead in the family's box. That requires a hands-on examination by a qualified lithic specialist with the artifact in front of them.
- Speculate on financial value. Mid-century museum assessments of "valuable" usually meant scientific significance (rare type, exotic material, intact flaking) rather than dollar value, and the modern market for legally-collected private Indigenous lithics is its own complicated subject including ethical questions about repatriation that the Smallwood-children generation should think about before any sale.
- Determine the legal collecting status of the original gathering. Pre-1979 surface collection on private farmland by a property-adjacent family member is generally lawful under Michigan and federal law of the period; post-NAGPRA (1990) considerations apply only to formal museum holdings, not to family possession. If any descendant ever wishes to **donate** the collection to a museum or to repatriate it under NAGPRA-aligned protocol, UMMAA's NAGPRA office (nagpra.umich.edu) is the entry point.
