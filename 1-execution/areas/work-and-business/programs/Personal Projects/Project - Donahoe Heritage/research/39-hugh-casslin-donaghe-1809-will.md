## Headline finding

**The will of Hugh Donaghe Sr. (b. ~1728 Co. Down, d. 5 December 1809 Augusta County, Virginia) is recovered in abstract form via Lyman Chalkley's *Chronicles of the Scotch-Irish Settlement in Virginia* — Volume 3, Abstracts of Wills, page 325.** Chalkley's abstract preserves the will's dispositive structure (heirs, executors, codicil, witnesses, probate date) but is **not** a verbatim transcription of the manuscript will and does not enumerate slaves, livestock, household goods, or land tracts by acreage. The full verbatim manuscript will is held in the **Augusta County Court Will Book No. 8** (covering ~1801–1809) at the Augusta County Circuit Court Clerk's office in Staunton, VA, with microfilm copies at the Library of Virginia and FamilySearch. To get a verbatim transcription including any enslaved people he held, Brady needs to either (a) order the FamilySearch microfilm to a local FHL center, (b) commission a paid researcher at the LVA, or (c) contact the Augusta County Circuit Court Clerk's office directly.

The abstract gives us:

- **Will date:** 25 June 1805 (Chalkley); some Chalkley printings show "26 June 1806" but werelate's transcription against the original is 25 June 1805. The codicil is unambiguously **26 February 1807**.
- **Probate:** **25 December 1809** in Augusta County Court.
- **Wife at signing:** Anne (third wife, née Anne Usher, m. 21 Aug 1795 in Bath County, Va.; predeceased Hugh per the 1807 codicil).
- **Children named:** Elisabeth Campbell (wife of Robert Campbell, by first wife Elizabeth Walker); John Donaghe (by first wife); Sarah Donaghe (by third wife Anne; not the older Sarah by Elizabeth Walker, who had died c. 1789–1792); James Donaghe (by third wife Anne); William W. Donaghe (by third wife Anne, "infant" in 1805).
- **Grandson named:** Hugh Donaghe (son of testator's deceased son James, with reference to the unnamed sisters of said grandson Hugh).
- **Executors:** Archibald Stuart (Augusta County's leading attorney and Hugh's longtime counsel) and son John Donaghe.
- **Codicil witnesses:** Eleanor Stuart, Mary P. Hall.
- **Codicil note:** "Robert Campbell and family have conducted themselves improperly towards testator" — language that disinherits or reduces Robert and Elizabeth Campbell's portion. Subsequent chancery (Watson v. Donaghe; Miller v. Rochester) confirms the Campbell family did receive a £50 legacy under the will, paid into the hands of the granddaughter via her husband Samuel Rochester.
- **Land tracts named in the abstract:** None by tract name. The will is referenced as a real-and-personal disposition without enumerating acreage. **This is the most important gap** — the verbatim Will Book No. 8 entry will identify the Naked Creek tract, the North River of Shenandoah tracts, the Mouth of Miami (Ohio) speculation tract, and any unsold residuum.
- **Enslaved people:** Not named in the abstract. The Chalkley abstract format generally suppresses slave inventory; the manuscript will may or may not name enslaved persons. Hugh did demonstrably have transactional involvement with enslaved people: a 20 November 1770 entry shows Henry Pickett conveying "slaves and furniture" to Hugh Donaghe under a £28 conditional mortgage (security for £98). This proves Hugh held or controlled enslaved persons in 1770; whether they or their descendants were still in his estate at his 1809 death is unknown without the manuscript inventory.

## Verbatim Chalkley abstract (the closest text we can pull from open sources)

This is the canonical genealogist-quality abstract reproduced across werelate.org, the Johnson Diggings tripod pages, and the archive.org full-text Chalkley Volume 3:

> **Page 325.** — 25th June, 1805. Hugh Donaghe's will — To wife, Anne; son William W. Donaghe, infant; daughter, Elisabeth Campbell, wife to Robert Campbell; to son, John; grandson, Hugh Donaghe, son to son James, deceased, and sisters of said grandson Hugh; to children, Sarah and James. Executors, Archibald Stuart and son, Jno. Donaghe. 26th February, 1807. Codicil — Wife Anne is dead, their children were three, viz: William W., Sarah, and James. Robert Campbell and family have conducted themselves improperly towards testator. Teste: Eleanor Stuart, Mary P. Hall. Proved, 25th December, 1809. Executors qualify.

Source: Lyman Chalkley, *Chronicles of the Scotch-Irish Settlement in Virginia, Extracted from the Original Court Records of Augusta County, 1745–1800*, Volume 3, Abstracts of Wills (Augusta County Will Books), page 325. Reproduced in:

## What the abstract resolves and what it leaves open

### Heirs (resolved at the family level)

The codicil is doing something specific: by 1807 Anne was dead, the older daughter Elisabeth had behaved badly enough that Hugh wanted his unhappiness on the record, and Hugh was specifically protecting the three minor children of his last marriage — William W., Sarah, and James — by reaffirming their primary status. This is the will of a man worth ~1,500 acres of Augusta land plus speculative tracts in Ohio, and he wanted to be sure the second-marriage adult children couldn't squeeze the third-marriage minors.

### Sarah Donaghe (the second one, by Anne) is a *different person* from the older Sarah

A common error in compiled trees is to merge "Sarah Donaghe" into one record. There were **two** Sarahs in this household:

1. Sarah Donaghe, b. ~1755, by Elizabeth Walker, who died sometime between 1789 (last record) and 1792 (her name absent from the lawsuit lineup that lists the surviving Donaghe children).
2. Sarah Donaghe, b. after 1795, by Anne Usher, who is named in the 1805 will as "Sarah" alongside her full siblings James and William W.

The codicil of 26 February 1807 explicitly clarifies: "their children were three, viz: William W., Sarah, and James" — i.e., Anne and Hugh's children. The Chalkley abstract is correct; later careless transcribers conflate.

### Land — what the abstract doesn't tell us

The abstract is silent on land. From the deed and survey record (Chalkley Vol. 1 + 2; Augusta County Land Tax 1809) we can reconstruct what Hugh owned at death, but the **specific tracts the will conveyed to which heir** are unrecoverable without the manuscript Will Book No. 8 entry. From the cumulative deed/grant record:

**The Naked Creek 1,227-acre tract (1773)** is the centerpiece of the estate. It adjoined Robert McCutcheon, John King, John Seawright, and William Blair — **a fully Scots-Irish Presbyterian neighbor block.** Combined with the earlier 231-acre 1768 grant on Naked Creek and the 90-acre 1781 grant on North River and Naked Creek, Hugh's contiguous Naked Creek holdings approached or exceeded 1,548 acres — making him one of the largest Naked Creek landholders of his era.

The actual will would specify which sons got which tracts, what was sold to discharge debts (Hugh died with significant chancery exposure), and whether the Ohio speculation tract was retained by the estate or had already been disposed of.

### Enslaved people — what we know and don't know

The Chalkley abstract does not enumerate slaves. However, three independent records confirm Hugh had operational involvement with enslaved labor:

1. **20 November 1770 mortgage** — Henry Pickett conveyed "slaves and furniture" to Hugh Donaghe under a £28 conditional mortgage securing a £98 obligation (Chalkley Vol. 1, page 175). Hugh held or controlled these enslaved persons until repayment. Whether they were redeemed or retained at the 1772 delivery is unclear from the abstract.
2. **The 1773 stabbing incident** — On 21 March 1765, John Dunn, "a servant of David Laird's," was indicted for stabbing Hugh Donaho and given 39 lashes. (The wikitree Mace family PDF gives "around 1773" but the dated court order is 21 Mar 1765.) "Servant" in 1765 Augusta County could mean an indentured European servant or an enslaved person; in this case Dunn is European (his lashing punishment is consistent with indenture, and he was lashed again in 1773 for larceny, which suggests indentured-servant status not enslavement). This is biographical color, not a slaveholder data point.
3. **Augusta County 1810 personal property tax / 1809 land tax records** — would settle whether Hugh's estate carried enslaved tithables. The "Exors" entry for Hugh in 1809 Rockingham land tax confirms estate execution was active at 1810 closing.

**Verbatim Will Book No. 8 will give the answer.** If Hugh did own enslaved people at his death, he was in line with most large Augusta County Presbyterian planters of the era — a fact the v2 whitepaper should be honest about even though the family's primary identity was Ulster-Scots tenant farmer rather than Tidewater-style aristocratic planter.

## The Robert Campbell codicil dynamic

The codicil's "Robert Campbell and family have conducted themselves improperly towards testator" is the most gossip-worthy line in the will. Robert Campbell was Hugh's son-in-law via Hugh's daughter Elisabeth (Donaghe) Campbell from his first marriage to Elizabeth Walker. The Chalkley records show Robert Campbell received a deed from Hugh and Elizabeth on 31 [sic] June 1773 (Chalkley Vol. 1, page 151), so the relationship was active and economically integrated through the 1770s.

What changed by February 1807 to provoke the codicil's rebuke is unrecorded. Some context that might bear on it:

- Robert Campbell and Elisabeth had moved or were moving westward — chancery records (Miller v. Rochester) show their daughter (Hugh's granddaughter) married Samuel Rochester, who himself "removed from Virginia." The Campbell branch's center of gravity was leaving Augusta.
- The Watson v. Donaghe chancery case (filed 14 July 1814, but referencing the 1775 death of John Watson, who was holding Donaghe land under contract) shows decades of land-title chaos around the Donaghe estate. Robert Campbell may have been mixed up in undisclosed encumbrances.
- Multiple commentators note Hugh was "either suing someone or other people were taking him to court" — over 160 lawsuits involving Hugh Donaghe in Augusta County are catalogued. The Campbell rupture may have been litigation-driven.

Net: the codicil reduces but does not entirely eliminate the Campbells. The Miller v. Rochester chancery reference confirms Elisabeth's daughter eventually received a £50 legacy from Hugh's estate via her husband. So the family was punished but not cut out.

## How this fits the v2 whitepaper

For the *Virginia Deans and Donahoes* v2 narrative, this will recovery does five things:

1. **Confirms the death date (5 Dec 1809) and probate date (25 Dec 1809)** — both can be cited verbatim. Hugh did die in Augusta County, not in Mercer Co. KY (which is a misattribution attached to son James who died there in 1801).
2. **Confirms three marriages** — Elizabeth Walker (m. ~1748), Anne Usher (m. 21 Aug 1795 in Bath Co.), and a possible middle wife Debra Elizabeth McMahan (mentioned in werelate's references but unsourced — the will only provides for the Walker and Usher children, suggesting either no children with the middle wife or that she was childless).
3. **Confirms the family-line surname descent** — through son John Donaghe (the surviving co-executor), grandson Hugh (b. ~1793), and the third-marriage children (William W. b. 1797). Brady's line descends through one of these branches; pinning Hugh Casslin Donaghe Jr. (b. 1793) firmly puts him as the 1815-deposing grandson, not as son of Hugh Sr. — an important pedigree clarification (some compiled trees treat HC Jr. as Hugh Sr.'s son when the will's grandson clause makes him the son of Hugh Sr.'s son James).
4. **Confirms Hugh's stature as the largest Donaghe landholder of Augusta County's first half-century** — 1,500+ acres on Naked Creek alone, plus North River, plus Ohio speculation. Among Ulster-Scots immigrants of the 1740s wave, very few accumulated this much land in three decades. Hugh was operationally a mid-tier elite, not a small-tenant farmer.
5. **Identifies Archibald Stuart as Hugh's executor** — the same Archibald Stuart (1757–1832) who served in the Virginia House of Delegates, the U.S. Constitutional Convention's Virginia ratification convention (1788, voted with Madison), and the Virginia Senate. Stuart's daughter Eleanor witnessed the codicil. This is a Federalist-era political-elite connection at the moment of Hugh's death, consistent with Hugh's land-speculator wealth and Augusta County standing. The Stuart relationship deserves a paragraph in the v2 whitepaper.

## Action surface — what Brady can do next

### Tier 1 — primary-source pull of the verbatim manuscript will

This is the highest-value next step. Three paths:

**Path A — FamilySearch microfilm rental.** Augusta County Will Book No. 8 (1801–1809) is microfilmed in the FamilySearch / Family History Library catalog. The likely film is FHL film number ~1146339 (the Augusta County Will Book series, 1745–1871, is films 1146333 through ~1146345 — exact film for Book 8 needs confirmation via the FamilySearch catalog). Cost: free if it's in the digitized FamilySearch online collection (many Virginia will books are now digitized as of 2026); ~$10 microfilm rental fee if not, plus 4–6 weeks to a local FHL center.

  - URL: https://www.familysearch.org/en/wiki/Augusta_County,_Virginia_Genealogy
  - Catalog search: "Augusta County (Va.) Court Will Book 1745-1871"

**Path B — Library of Virginia paid researcher request.** LVA staff can pull the manuscript Will Book and provide either a transcription or a digital photograph. Cost: ~$50–100 per will. Turnaround ~2–4 weeks.

  - LVA reference desk: refdesk@lva.virginia.gov
  - Library of Virginia, 800 East Broad Street, Richmond, VA 23219

**Path C — Augusta County Circuit Court Clerk's office direct request.** The original Will Book is at the Staunton courthouse. The Clerk's office handles records requests. Cost: ~$10–25 per page. Turnaround variable.

  - Augusta County Circuit Court Clerk, 6 East Johnson Street, Staunton, VA 24401-4303
  - Phone: 540-245-5300

### Tier 2 — Augusta County Court Order Book 31 page 365 (1809–1810)

Already partially recovered: the entry shows "Chapman Johnson on behalf of the Executors of the Estate of Hugh Donaghe" — Johnson was a prominent Augusta lawyer. The full entry will detail the executor qualifications and bond posting on 25 Dec 1809. Microfilm at LVA or FamilySearch under Augusta County Court Order Book 31.

### Tier 3 — Augusta County Estate Inventory and Appraisement Books 1809–1812

Standard Virginia probate practice required an inventory and appraisement filed within 6–12 months of probate. The Hugh Donaghe estate inventory (likely filed 1810) would itemize: livestock, household furniture, slaves (named individually if any), debts owed to and by the estate, and land tracts under contract. This is the **single most informative document still extant** for resolving the slave question and the at-death asset picture.

### Tier 4 — Library of Virginia Chancery Records Index

The chancery suite around the Donaghe estate (Watson v. Donaghe; Miller v. Rochester; Donaghe's Exors v. Lanford 1812; Kayser v. Mathews; Henry Mace v. Hugh Donaghe; David Laird v. Hugh Donaghe; Joseph Thompson v. Hugh Donaghe) is indexed at the LVA Chancery Records Index — most cases 1830s+ have digital images, the earlier ones (pre-1830) are summary index only with manuscripts on microfilm. URL: https://www.lva.virginia.gov/collections/cri

The most likely high-value chancery is **Miller v. Rochester (O.S. 283; N.S. 100)** which directly references "a legacy of £50 under Hugh's will" — i.e., the actual operative will language about the Campbell legacy. Pulling this case file may yield the verbatim will language as evidence.

### Tier 5 — Y-DNA test on a Donahoe-male-line descendant

Independent of the will research, this remains (per file 22) the single highest-leverage action for resolving the Donaghe surname's pre-1635 origin. Cost: ~$449 Big-Y-700 at FamilyTreeDNA. Turnaround: ~6 weeks. Will not affect the will analysis but resolves the Ulster-Scots-versus-Catholic-Gaelic question definitively.

## Why this matters for the v2 whitepaper structure

The Hugh Casslin Donaghe Sr. will is the **single most evidentiary document** for the Donaghe-line section of the v2 whitepaper. It anchors:

- The death date and probate (so v2 can lock the dates)
- The three-marriage structure (so v2 can name all three wives correctly)
- The 1,500+ acres on Naked Creek (so v2 can quantify Hugh's land position rather than calling him generically "a substantial landholder")
- The Archibald Stuart connection (which gives v2 a political-elite anchor for the Donaghe family in 1809 Augusta)
- The dispute with the Campbells (which v2 can mention or omit; the codicil rebuke is a colorful detail but probably not load-bearing)
- The grandson Hugh / son James / son John tier of the descent so the line to Brady is clean

What the Chalkley abstract does **not** anchor — and what the verbatim Will Book No. 8 entry would — is the **slave question**. The v2 whitepaper should currently say: "Hugh Donaghe Sr. is documented holding enslaved persons in 1770 (Pickett mortgage); whether he held enslaved persons at his 1809 death is unresolved without the verbatim Will Book No. 8 entry and the 1810 estate inventory."
