## Headline

The biggest hero in this line is **Mary Ellen Donahoe — "Mom" — Brady's maternal grandmother.** She is the tentpole. Every other ancestor in this file is a candidate; she is the certainty. She carried the Donahoe name forward into the modern era, married into the Dean family, and raised Susan and Paulette through whatever the Bull farm 1950s loss actually was. The fact that this project exists at all — that there IS a Donahoe maternal line for Brady to research — is because Mom held it together. That is the headline.

Everyone else on this list is a person who, by similar lights, refused to be erased. The Famine immigrant who made the crossing. The Eastern Shore farmer who lived under Union occupation and chose how to survive it. The Catholic family that kept the faith in a Methodist county. The widows who held land and children together when the men were dead, drafted, drowned, or gone. The hypothetical Patriot ancestor whose service Susan and Paulette could pin a DAR pin to.

## 1. Approach — Who Counts as a Hero

The Monacan-project model is the standard. Joseph Anderson Clark (b. 1869, Irish Creek) refused to be reclassified white-or-colored under Walter Plecker's regime. He converted LDS in 1911. He sat for Plecker's hit list and didn't flinch. That's heroism — survival under institutional violence, dignity preserved, name kept.

Apply that same lens here. A Donahoe hero or heroine is anyone in the line who:

- **Survived a documented mass-violence event.** Famine. Civil War occupation. Plecker-era racial laws (if Eastern Shore Algonquian ancestry surfaces). The 1918 flu. Agricultural collapse in the 1950s. Surviving these IS the qualifying act.
- **Performed military service in any war.** Revolutionary, War of 1812, Mexican-American, Civil War (either side, with context), Spanish-American, WWI, WWII, Korea, Vietnam. Service alone counts. Heroism in service counts double.
- **Held civic office or community leadership.** Church wardens, parish priests, midwives, schoolteachers, justices of the peace, deacons, postmasters, town councilmen, Order of the Eastern Star members, Knights of Columbus. Eastern Shore communities ran on these volunteer roles for 300 years.
- **Held a household together through a recognizable hardship.** Widowhood is the most common one — a woman whose husband died young or in war and who kept the farm, raised the kids, paid the taxes, signed the deeds. The 1850s, 1860s, 1870s, and 1930s wills and probate records of Accomack County are full of these women. Most of them are nameless to family memory; they're not nameless to the courthouse.
- **Maintained a minority identity in a hostile or indifferent county.** In Accomack County, that means Catholic in a Methodist-majority area. It also means, if it surfaces, Algonquian-descended in a county that legally erased "Indian" from its records under Plecker.
- **Crossed an ocean.** The Famine immigrant who made it from Ireland to Eastern Shore Virginia is, by definition, a hero. The Atlantic in 1847 killed roughly one in seven passengers on coffin ships. Whoever made that crossing and started over is not optional in this whitepaper.
- **Refused a documented act of erasure.** This is the Joseph Anderson Clark category. We don't yet know if there's a Donahoe-line analog. If Phase 2 Agent 2 (Native ancestry) surfaces an ancestor on Plecker's Eastern Shore list, that ancestor's choice — to stay, to register Catholic, to take a Methodist wife, to leave for Maryland or Pennsylvania — was a survival decision. Name them and honor it.

What does NOT count, by this rubric: being rich. Being on a county courthouse pew donor plaque. Being descended from a Pocahontas claim that doesn't pass the OFFV registry. Being Famous in a Vague Way. Folklore is a hypothesis. Documented hardship survived is a fact.

## 3. Likely Candidate Categories (Names TBD as Phase 2 Resolves)

These are the buckets where a hero or heroine almost certainly exists. Names get added by reference as other agents complete their files.

### 3a. The Famine Immigrant

The first Donahoe to America. By H4 in `00-master-sources.md`, the Donahoe surname most likely arrives in the United States during the 1845–1855 Famine wave or just after. Whoever this person is, they:

- Survived An Gorta Mór (the Great Hunger) in Ireland, 1845–1852, in which roughly one million died and another million emigrated.
- Crossed the Atlantic in steerage, on a passage that took four to ten weeks, during a period when ship-board mortality from typhus, cholera, dysentery, and starvation was substantial.
- Arrived in America with the surname spelled some way (Donahoe, Donahue, Donohoe, O'Donoghue, Donaghy) and chose, or had chosen for them, the spelling we now have.
- Got to the Eastern Shore — which was NOT a typical Famine immigrant destination. Most Irish Famine immigrants landed in New York, Boston, Philadelphia, or Baltimore and stayed in the cities. An Eastern Shore arrival means either: passage through Baltimore and downward migration, OR (rarer) direct arrival at Norfolk, OR a generation in another city before the family pushed south.

This person is a hero by the Monacan-project standard. They are not optional in the whitepaper. **Phase 2 Agent 3 (Irish origins) is the source on this name.** If Agent 3 surfaces them, this file gets updated. If Agent 3 cannot identify them yet, the whitepaper still names the category and lists "find the immigrant ancestor" as the highest-leverage open question.

### 3b. The Civil War Survivor (Eastern Shore Edition)

The Eastern Shore is the unusual case in the Civil War. Per `00-master-sources.md`: Accomack and Northampton counties voted with Virginia to secede in 1861, BUT Union forces under General Henry Lockwood occupied the entire Eastern Shore in November 1861 and held it for the duration. Practical effects:

- Most military-age men did NOT enlist in Confederate units. Some slipped across the Chesapeake Bay to the mainland and joined Virginia regiments. Others stayed home. A small minority enlisted on the Union side.
- Every adult male was eventually required to take an Oath of Allegiance to the United States to conduct legal business — sell land, marry, register a will. These oath records exist and are partially indexed at the Library of Virginia.
- The economy continued under federal control. Watermen kept working. Farmers kept farming. There was no Sherman-style burning, no Petersburg-style siege. Eastern Shore Civil War survival is a survival of compromise and occupation, not a survival of battlefield.

A Donahoe ancestor (or married-in surname — Dean, related Eastern Shore families) who lived through this had to make a series of small, public, documented choices about loyalty under pressure. Each oath of allegiance is a primary-source decision point. **Phase 2 Agent 5 (Civil War) is the source.** Specific service records — Confederate enlistment slips, Union muster rolls, oath of allegiance signatures — bring forward into this file.

The category itself, even before names: anyone who farmed the Eastern Shore from 1861 to 1865 and held the family land through five years of military occupation belongs in the heroes section. That kind of grinding-it-out survival is exactly the form of heroism the academic record consistently undercounts.

### 3c. The Revolutionary Patriot

Per `00-master-sources.md` H9, given how deep the maternal lines likely go in Eastern Shore VA, the prior probability that there is at least one documented Patriot ancestor in this line is high. "Patriot" under DAR/SAR rules is generously defined: military service, civil service, signing the Oath of Allegiance to Virginia in 1777–1779, paying a war tax, supplying provisions to Continental or state forces.

Eastern Shore Virginia in 1775–1783 was a contested coastal zone. Lord Dunmore's loyalist fleet operated in the Chesapeake. The Eastern Shore raised militia companies. There is a documented Eastern Shore Continental presence. There is also documented loyalist activity. A Donahoe-line ancestor — almost certainly NOT through the Donahoe surname itself, which arrives ~70 years too late, but through one of the deeper maternal lines — is plausible to the point of likely.

**Phase 2 Agent 6 (DAR) is the source.** Specific Patriots get named here. The whitepaper will eventually have a short paragraph naming the Patriot, citing the DAR record number, and noting that Susan and Paulette's eligibility is alive.

### 3d. The Watermen and the Farmers

Eastern Shore Virginia is famous, by every regional historian (Rountree, Mariner, Wennersten), for two trades: agriculture (corn, wheat, sweet potato, later tomato) and the water (oyster, crab, finfish, later soft-shell crab as a national specialty). For 250 years, every man on the Eastern Shore was working some combination of the two.

Even without specific names, this category belongs in the whitepaper because it is a cultural inheritance. Brady's ancestors on the Eastern Shore — Donahoe, Dean, the deeper maternal surnames — were almost certainly watermen, farmers, or both. The Chesapeake oyster boom of the 1880s–1910s, the tomato cannery economy of the 1900s–1940s, the post-WWII collapse — these are not abstractions. They are the working life of three or four generations of this line.

If Phase 2 Agent 4 (Bull farm) surfaces specific land records, occupation entries from the 1850–1940 censuses, or oyster license registrations from the Virginia Marine Resources Commission predecessor agencies, names go here. Until then, the cultural inheritance is the entry.

### 3e. The Catholic-Minority Resilience Story

If the Donahoe family arrived as Famine Irish Catholics and stayed Catholic on the Eastern Shore, that is a quiet 150-year act. Accomack County in 1850, 1900, 1950, 2000 was overwhelmingly Methodist (with significant Baptist and Presbyterian populations). A Catholic family in Onancock had to drive or ride to a small parish — Holy Trinity in Onancock, if that's where they ended up — and maintain a religious identity that was, in the 19th-century Eastern Shore, foreign and minoritized.

Maintaining a minority religious identity in a small, gossip-heavy, Methodist county for five generations is its own form of heroism. It's also the most likely explanation for why Brady's mother's side has the Donahoe-line stories preserved at all: Catholic communities historically kept better baptismal, marriage, and death records than the surrounding Methodist parishes, and Catholic-minority families in Protestant-majority counties tended to know exactly where they came from because the surrounding culture kept reminding them they were different.

**This category needs ground-truthing from a couple of sources:**
- Holy Trinity Catholic Church Onancock (founded 1879 per regional records — verify) — baptism, marriage, burial registers
- Phase 2 Agent 8 (primary-source extracts) for Donahoe/Dean Catholic sacramental record hits
- Direct ask to Susan and Paulette: was Mom Catholic? Was her family Catholic? When did Catholic practice end, if it did, in the family?

If the answer is "no, the Donahoes were Methodist," the category drops out. If the answer is "yes," this is one of the most underrated chapters of the whitepaper.

### 3f. The Women Who Held Households

The single most undercounted heroism category in any genealogy. Every line has women who held households together through widowhood, war, depression, farm loss, or all of the above. The Eastern Shore has unusually good records of these women because Accomack County's continuous court record series (1632–present) preserves probate, dower allotment, and widow's-thirds documentation that a lot of younger counties simply don't have.

Specific candidates emerge by census-after-husband-death pattern. A Donahoe or related-surname woman who appears in the 1880 census as head of household with minor children, who appears in deed records selling or holding land, who pays the 1900 census enumerator her own name as occupation "farmer" or "head of household" — that's a hero. Her name belongs in the file.

**Phase 2 Agents 4 (Bull farm) and 8 (primary-source extracts) are the sources.** Names get added as widow-as-head-of-household entries surface in census or probate.

One specific case to watch for: if the Bull farm 1950s loss happened during or after the death of a Donahoe-line man, the widow who absorbed that loss and raised the next generation through it is a heroine by any standard. She may BE Mary Ellen Donahoe. We don't yet know.

## 5. Family Identity Reconciliation — Folklore vs. Record

Per skill convention, folklore is a hypothesis. Here's the state of play on the heroism-relevant claims:

**Folklore: "Native American ancestry."** Status: unverified. Eastern Shore Algonquian ancestry is geographically plausible (Accomac and Accohannock chiefdoms, Gingaskin reservation 1641–1813). Phase 2 Agent 2 is resolving this. If the answer is yes, the relevant ancestor goes in the heroes file as an erasure-survivor. If no, this folklore claim retires honestly. **The Pocahontas-specific version is almost certainly false** — the OFFV registry is well-curated and most Pocahontas claims fail it. We say so without softening.

**Folklore: "Deep Virginia roots."** Status: partially verifiable. The Donahoe surname is Famine-era (so 1840s+). But Mary Ellen's MATERNAL line, and her grandmother's maternal line, plausibly run back to 1607–1650 colonial Virginia through the deeper Eastern Shore surnames. Phase 2 Agent 1 owns this. If the deep-roots ancestor surfaces, they go in the heroes file under FFV-immigrant or Jamestown-survivor categories.

**Folklore: "Family lost the Bull farm in the mid-1950s."** Status: high prior probability given specificity of the claim (named farm, named decade). Phase 2 Agent 4 is resolving. The reframe stays the same regardless of resolution: if the loss is real, the person who absorbed it is a hero, not a footnote.

**Folklore: "Donahoe is Irish; the O' was dropped."** Status: the surname IS Irish (Ó Donnchadha). The O' was almost universally dropped in 1700s Penal Era Ireland and not reclaimed by American emigrants. So this folklore is structurally true. Whether THE specific drop happened in Ireland or at a US port is what Agent 3 is resolving.

**Folklore: "John Smith / Pocahontas connection."** Status: almost certainly false on Smith (he had no children). Almost certainly false on Pocahontas (one son, Thomas Rolfe; descendants documented, registry well-curated). We say so directly. The geographic adjacency is real — Pocahontas's father's confederation included the Eastern Shore tribes — but biological descent is rare and not assumed.

The Monacan whitepaper voice on folklore: where partly true, say so; where wrong, say so without softening; where unverified, say so and put it on the action surface. Apply here.
