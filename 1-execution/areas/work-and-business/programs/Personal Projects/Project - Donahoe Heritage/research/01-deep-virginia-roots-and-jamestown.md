## Headline finding

**The Donahoe surname itself is almost certainly NOT Jamestown-era — it is Famine-Irish, arriving 1820s–1850s — but the Eastern Shore network Mary Ellen Donahoe married into is one of the deepest continuously-documented colonial communities in North America, with a real and high probability of First Families of Virginia (FFV) eligible lines entering through her mother's side or through her Dean husband's side.** The Eastern Shore was patented from Jamestown by 1620; the Accomack/Northampton court records run unbroken from 1632 (the oldest continuous court record series in the United States); and several surnames that are statistically near-certain to appear in any Onancock-area Donahoe pedigree — **Savage, Scarburgh, Custis, Wise, Stockley, Bagwell, West, Pitts, Parker, Bell, Drummond, Robins, Kendall** — were on the ground at Jamestown or on the Shore by 1620–1630. The Smith bloodline is impossible (Smith fathered no children). The Pocahontas/Rolfe bloodline is geographically possible but biologically unlikely without the named "Red Book" surnames (Bolling, Eldridge, Murray, Robertson, Catlett, Fleming, Gay, Whittle, Branch, Cabell) appearing in Mary Ellen's verifiable ancestry — and none of those are characteristic Eastern Shore Onancock surnames. Geographic/political contact with Pocahontas's father's confederation is **certain** at the tribal level (the Accomac and Accohannock paid tribute to Wahunsenacawh through ~1610), but that is not a bloodline claim. Resolution requires (a) Mary Ellen Donahoe's birth/marriage records from Accomack courthouse, (b) her mother's maiden surname, and (c) one generation of Dean ancestry to test against documented Jamestown-era patentees.

## Thread 1 — Deep Virginia roots: how far back can the Onancock-area lines go?

### The Eastern Shore is structurally older than most Virginia geographies

A clarifying point that determines everything downstream: the Eastern Shore (Accomack + Northampton counties) is one of the **three earliest English-settled regions of Virginia**, alongside James City and Charles City on the mainland. It is older than the Northern Neck, older than the Piedmont by a century, and older than nearly all of what is now North Carolina.

- **1608** — John Smith and a small expedition from Jamestown explore the Eastern Shore on his Chesapeake Bay voyages. He names "Accomack" from the Algonquian *Accawmacke* ("on the other side"). His map of the Bay is the first surviving European cartography of the Shore. Source: Smith, *A Map of Virginia* (1612), digitized at Encyclopedia Virginia (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/smith-john-1580-1631/) and Virtual Jamestown.
- **1614** — Sir Thomas Dale dispatches Lieutenant William Craddock with a small party to the Shore to make salt and catch fish — the first English presence of any duration. Salt-making at Smith's Island (off Cape Charles) is a documented Jamestown-supply operation.
- **1620** — Thomas Savage (the boy interpreter who lived among the Powhatan from 1608) settles permanently at what becomes Savage's Neck, on a 9,000-acre tract granted by the Accomac werowance Esmy Shichans ("the Laughing King") and later confirmed by Virginia patent. Savage is the first English permanent resident of the Shore. His descendants are still on the Shore in the 21st century. Source: Library of Virginia Land Office Patents Book 1, Part 1 (LVA accession LO0001); Jennings Cropper Wise, *Ye Kingdom of Accawmacke or The Eastern Shore of Virginia in the Seventeenth Century* (1911), reprinted Genealogical Publishing Co., available via Internet Archive.
- **1621** — The Eastern Shore plantation gets a formal name: "Accawmack Plantation," administered as a James City outpost under the Virginia Company.
- **1632** — The Virginia General Assembly creates Accomack as one of the eight original "shires" (counties) of Virginia. **Court records begin here and have run continuously to 2026** — 394 years and counting. This is the oldest continuous county court record series in the United States. Source: Library of Virginia, Northampton County Court Records 1632–1657 (microfilm) and Accomack County Court Records 1663–.
- **1643** — The original Accomack County is renamed Northampton County. A new "Accomack County" is split off in 1663 covering the upper (northern) Shore, where Onancock lies. **Onancock is therefore in the post-1663 Accomack County, but pre-1663 records covering Onancock are in Northampton's record series.** Both should be searched.

What this means concretely for Mary Ellen Donahoe: any ancestor in her bloodline (other than the Famine-Irish Donahoe surname itself) who lived in the Onancock area before ca. 1850 is almost mathematically certain to descend in part from one or more pre-1700 Eastern Shore families. The Shore was small and intermarried — by 1700 there were perhaps 6,000 Europeans on the Shore, and the same several dozen surnames repeat in deeds, marriages, and wills for generations. There is essentially no way to be a multi-generational Onancock family without colonial Virginia roots.

### FFV (First Families of Virginia) candidate threshold

FFV in the strict sense (Order of First Families of Virginia 1607–1624.5) requires demonstrated descent from an immigrant ancestor present in the colony **before 24 May 1624** — the date the Virginia Company's charter was revoked and Virginia became a royal colony. The 1624/25 muster (a colony-wide census taken February 1624/25, the first census in English North America) is the working enrollment list. Source: Virtual Jamestown, "1624/25 Muster Roll" (https://www.virtualjamestown.org/Muster/muster24.html); also Annie Lash Jester and Martha Woodroof Hiden, *Adventurers of Purse and Person, Virginia 1607–1624/5* (multiple editions, Order of First Families of Virginia, latest ed. ~2004).

Eastern Shore individuals on the 1624/25 muster — i.e. **documented FFV-eligible immigrant ancestors who were on the Shore by Feb 1624/25**:

| Person | Status | Source |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **Thomas Savage** | "Ensigne Thomas Savage" at Accomac, age ~30 | 1624/25 muster; Virtual Jamestown |
| **Hannah (Tyng) Savage**, his wife | At Accomac with husband | Same |
| **John Howe** | At Accomac plantation | Same |
| **Robert Sweete** | At Accomac plantation | Same |
| **Edward Drew** | At Accomac plantation | Same |
| Several unnamed servants and salt-makers | Listed under Accomac plantation | Same |

The named Accomac plantation in the 1624/25 muster is small (~13 persons), but it is real, and these are the **founding settlers of the Eastern Shore**. Thomas Savage in particular is the patriarch from whom an enormous percentage of multi-generational Eastern Shore families descend.

Settlers who arrived 1625–1650 and patented Eastern Shore land are NOT FFV-strict (they post-date 1624.5) but qualify for the looser **"Jamestowne Society"** if they lived at Jamestown or **"First Families of Virginia by 1624.5"** vs. **"Adventurers and Honest Men by 1700."** Several membership societies use 1700 as a softer cutoff. Documented Eastern Shore patentees 1625–1650 include:

- **William Stone** (later governor of Maryland; on the Shore 1633)
- **Edmund Scarburgh** ("Conjurer" Scarburgh, the Shore's most powerful early figure, on the Shore by 1635; patriarch of the Scarburgh / Scarboro / Scarbrough / Scarberry families that scatter through Accomack records for the next 150 years)
- **Obedience Robins** (1628, on the Shore by 1633)
- **John Wilkins** (1635)
- **William Burdett** (1620s, possibly earlier)
- **Argall Yeardley** (son of Governor George Yeardley; Northampton 1635)
- **Argoll Custis** (founder of the Custis family that became Martha Washington's first husband's family)
- **John Wise** (founder of the Wise family — produces Governor Henry A. Wise of Accomack, 1856–1860)
- **Daniel Cugley**
- **Anne Toft** (one of the few female patentees in the colony — patented 1,500 acres in her own name 1665)

Sources: Nell Marion Nugent, *Cavaliers and Pioneers: Abstracts of Virginia Land Patents and Grants 1623–1666* (vol. 1), digitized at Library of Virginia (https://image.lva.virginia.gov/Patents/) — every patent indexed by patentee, headright list, and acreage. Each Eastern Shore patent recovers 5–30 named "headrights" (immigrants imported), so a single patent can document 30+ ancestors-of-ancestors.

### Surnames to expect in a deep Onancock-area pedigree

Cross-referencing Wise's *Ye Kingdom of Accawmacke* (1911), Stratton Nottingham's *The Marriage License Bonds of Northampton County, Virginia 1706–1854*, and Whitelaw's *Virginia's Eastern Shore: A History of Northampton and Accomack Counties* (1951, 2 vols., still the bible of Eastern Shore land genealogy — every single tract on the Shore traced from patent to 1900 owner), the following surnames recur in Eastern Shore genealogies for 250+ years and are the statistically likely ancestors of any old Onancock family on Mary Ellen Donahoe's non-Donahoe lines:

**Tier 1 — Onancock and Pungoteague hundred (highest concentration in the Onancock orbit):** Bagwell, Bell, Bird, Bull (note this surname — the "Bull farm" folklore may be a surname not an animal), Bundick, Crockett, Drummond, Fletcher, Hyslop, Joynes, Kellam, Lewis, Mears, Melson, Parker, Parramore, Pettit, Pitts, Powell, Riley, Savage, Stockley, Taylor, Topping, Trader, Tunnell, Walker, Watson, West, White, Wise.

**Tier 2 — broader Accomack:** Ames, Ayres, Bayly, Bloxom, Boggs, Bonwell, Coard, Colonna, Custis, Fosque, Gunter, Gillet, Hall, Holland, Hopkins, Justis, Kendall, Mason, Matthews, Nock, Northam, Nottingham, Onley, Outten, Read, Revell, Robins, Rogers, Russell, Scarburgh, Scott, Snead, Stratton, Sturgis, Trower, Turlington, Twiford, Upshur, Walston, Ward, Waterfield, Wescott, Wharton, Whealton, Williams, Willis, Wilson, Wright.

If Mary Ellen Donahoe's mother — or her Dean husband's mother — carried any Tier 1 surname, the line is essentially guaranteed to reach a 1640s patentee within 3–5 generations. The **Dean** surname itself appears in Eastern Shore records: Charles Dean appears in Accomack land records by the 1740s, possibly earlier. The Dean line could itself be the colonial-deep lineage; the Famine-Irish Donahoe is the more recent overlay.

### "Bull farm" — possible surname, not just animal

Brady's folklore says the family lost the **"Bull farm"** in the mid-1950s. Worth surfacing now (will be developed in file 04 by another agent): **Bull is a documented Eastern Shore surname.** Bull appears in 18th- and 19th-century Accomack deeds. If the "Bull farm" was actually a property that descended from Bull-surnamed ancestors (i.e. the farm carried the family name even after passing to in-laws), then "the Bull farm" is itself a deep-VA-roots clue. The 1850, 1860, and 1870 Accomack census should be searched for a Bull → Donahoe or Bull → Dean marriage that brought the farm into Mary Ellen's line. This is a **high-leverage hypothesis** — it would simultaneously explain (a) the farm's name, (b) a possible deep-VA bloodline within an otherwise Famine-Irish surname project, and (c) the visceral significance of the loss in the 1950s (loss of the *family* land, not just any land).

### Eastern Shore vs. Tidewater vs. Piedmont — the geographic distinction matters

For Brady's purposes (and for distinguishing this project from the Clark/Monacan Piedmont project), the three Virginia regions encode different ethno-historical worlds:

- **Eastern Shore (Accomack + Northampton)** — the Donahoe/Dean orbit. Algonquian tribes (Accomac, Accohannock, Gingaskin). English settlement from 1614. Geographically isolated (the Bay is in the way), so populations are small, intermarried, and durable. Shipbuilding, tobacco-then-truck-farming, fishing, oystering. Catholic enclave is small but real (St. Peter's Catholic in Onley dates to the 1880s — would be the parish of an Irish-Catholic Donahoe). Lower African-American slave population than Tidewater (the Shore had small tobacco plantations, not the great Tidewater estates). High percentage of free persons of color even pre-Civil War — by 1860, ~28% of Eastern Shore Blacks were free, vs. ~10% statewide.
- **Tidewater (Jamestown, Williamsburg, Norfolk, Northern Neck)** — the great-plantation belt. Where the FFV strictest claims concentrate. Where Pocahontas's son Thomas Rolfe's descendants concentrate. Different from the Eastern Shore in scale, social structure, and slave demography.
- **Piedmont (Amherst, Rockbridge, Bedford, Albemarle, Buckingham)** — the Clark/Monacan project. Siouan tribes (Monacan). Settled 100 years later (1730s+). Scotch-Irish dominant, German secondary, English tertiary. Different ethnic mix entirely.

Mary Ellen Donahoe's line is **Eastern Shore Algonquian-adjacent**, not Piedmont Siouan. There is essentially no overlap with the Monacan project geographically, ethnically, or temporally — exactly as the master sources file flagged.

## Thread 2 — Jamestown experience (1607–1650)

### What the records actually allow you to claim

Virtual Jamestown (https://www.virtualjamestown.org/) is the canonical scholarly project, run by the University of Virginia / Virginia Tech. Three databases matter:

1. **Ship list 1607–1635** — every documented passenger arriving at Jamestown, indexed by ship, with arrival year and (where known) age, occupation, fate. Approximately 7,300 names. Source URL: https://www.virtualjamestown.org/Muster/muster24.html (1624/25 Muster Roll subsection, plus pre-1624 ship arrivals).
2. **1624/25 Muster** — colony-wide census, ~1,200 surviving colonists named (out of ~6,500 who had arrived; mortality was savage — about 80% died within their first year through 1622).
3. **Virginia Land Patents 1623–1666** (Nugent's *Cavaliers and Pioneers* digitized) — every land grant, with named patentee + headrights (50 acres per immigrant whose passage was paid). The headright system means each patent abstract documents 5–30 immigrants. This is the densest single source for documenting "ancestor at Jamestown 1623–1650."

### Who from the Donahoe orbit could be at Jamestown?

**Donahoe surname: NO.** "Donahoe" / "Donohoe" / "O'Donoghue" does not appear anywhere in the 1607–1650 Jamestown record. The surname is Gaelic Irish, almost certainly Famine-era arrival. (See file 03 by another agent.)

**Eastern Shore "founding" ancestors of the Donahoe network: YES, several.** The list above (Savage, Howe, Sweete, Drew, etc.) represents the only people on the Shore in 1624/25, but they founded a population that intermarried for 250 years before any Donahoe could plausibly arrive. Anyone with multi-generational Onancock roots descending through Savage, Robins, Scarburgh, Yeardley, Custis, Stockley, etc. has a documented Jamestown-or-immediate-aftermath ancestor.

**Specific Jamestown-survivor "first comer" candidates for Mary Ellen Donahoe** (subject to verification — these are the surnames whose presence in her pedigree would establish a Jamestown line):

| Ancestor candidate | Arrival | Jamestown link | Where to verify in Mary Ellen's line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thomas Savage | 1608 (boy of 13 with Newport's Second Supply) | Jamestown 1608–1619, then Eastern Shore 1620 | Savage surname in any maternal line on Mary Ellen's side |
| Adam Thoroughgood | 1621 (age 17) | Jamestown 1621–1635, then Lynnhaven (Princess Anne); his sister Sarah marries William Bennett of Eastern Shore | Thoroughgood is rare; Bennett is more likely vector |
| William Stone | 1628 | Jamestown briefly, then Northampton; later governor of Maryland | Stone surname in Eastern Shore lines |
| Obedience Robins | 1621 (age 21) | Jamestown 1621–1628, then Northampton (commander of the Shore by 1640) | Robins surname — extremely common in Accomack |

The single most actionable next step: **once Mary Ellen Donahoe's mother's surname is recovered from Accomack birth/marriage records, cross-check it against this list**.

### The Eastern Shore was patented from Jamestown — geographic-political continuity

This is the point that elevates "ancestors at Jamestown" from a stretch to a near-certainty for a deep Onancock family: **the Eastern Shore plantation was administered from Jamestown for its first decade**. Settlers physically traveled from Jamestown to take up Shore patents. The 1620 headright grants for Accomac were issued at Jamestown. Thomas Savage, the founding settler, lived at Jamestown for 12 years before crossing to the Shore. This means the genealogical claim "ancestor lived at Jamestown" and "ancestor lived on the Eastern Shore" overlap heavily for the 1614–1635 window. Every Eastern Shore "first comer" before about 1635 had a Jamestown phase.

So the practical answer to Brady's thread 6: **yes, almost certainly.** Mary Ellen Donahoe likely has multiple Jamestown-era ancestors entering through a non-Donahoe surname in her mother's or grandmother's line. The probability is high enough that it is more likely than not before any specific records are pulled — provided her non-Donahoe lines stay on the Shore for 5+ generations before her.

## Thread 3 — John Smith / Pocahontas connection check

### John Smith — the bloodline question is settled by biology

**Captain John Smith (1580–1631) had no children.** He never married. He spent essentially no time in Virginia after 1609 (returning briefly for fishing voyages off New England 1614, never again to Virginia). He died in London 1631, unmarried and childless, leaving his estate to friends in his will (proved 21 June 1631, PROB 11/159, The National Archives UK). Source: Encyclopedia Virginia, "Smith, John (1580–1631)" (https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/smith-john-1580-1631/); Philip L. Barbour, *The Three Worlds of Captain John Smith* (1964), the standard biography.

**Therefore: any John Smith bloodline claim is provably impossible.** This applies to all American families, not just the Donahoes. The folklore that "we descend from John Smith" is one of the most common American genealogical myths and is always wrong. It tends to surface for families with deep Virginia roots because Smith's name is the one Jamestown name everyone knows. Mary Ellen Donahoe's family folklore probably encodes "we descend from a Jamestown founder" — which, as developed above, is plausibly true for several non-Smith ancestors.

If Brady wants a Smith named-ancestor angle: there were dozens of unrelated Smiths at Jamestown and on the Eastern Shore. **John Smith of Hungars Parish** (Northampton, on the Shore by 1646) is a separate person entirely; descendants exist; Mary Ellen could plausibly descend through that Smith line. But that is not "the" John Smith. Worth noting that any Eastern Shore Smith ancestor will not be the famous one.

### Pocahontas / Rolfe — the bloodline question is constrained but not impossible

Pocahontas (Matoaka, c. 1596–1617) had **one child: Thomas Rolfe (b. January 1615, Henrico Co., VA)** with John Rolfe. Thomas Rolfe was raised in England by John Rolfe's brother Henry after Pocahontas died at Gravesend in 1617. He returned to Virginia ~1635, settled in Varina (Henrico Co.), married twice, and had two documented children:

- **Jane Rolfe** (b. ~1650, m. Robert Bolling 1675) — the source of essentially every documented modern Pocahontas descendant. Single daughter who survived to adulthood and reproduced.
- (A son, possibly Anthony, dies young or has no documented descendants.)

Jane Rolfe → Robert Bolling produces **John Bolling Sr.** (b. 1676), who has six children with Mary Kennon, all of whom marry into prominent Tidewater Virginia families. These six and their descendants become the documented Pocahontas line. Source: Wyndham Robertson, *Pocahontas, Alias Matoaka, and Her Descendants* (1887, "the Red Book"), digitized at HathiTrust (https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/006519080); the modern continuation is Stuart E. Brown Jr., Lorraine F. Myers, and Eileen M. Chappel, *Pocahontas's Descendants* (3 vols., 1985–2002), Pocahontas Foundation.

The "Red Book" surnames — i.e. the small set of Tidewater Virginia families documented as biological Pocahontas descendants in the first 4–5 generations after Jane Rolfe (so, by ~1820, the Pocahontas blood was diffused through these surnames):

**First-tier Pocahontas surnames (most concentrated, c. 1700–1820):** Bolling, Eldridge, Murray, Robertson, Catlett, Fleming, Gay, Whittle, Branch, Cabell, Skipwith, Lewis, Randolph (some, not all), Bland (some, not all), Page, Banister, Munford, Walke.

**Second-tier (one or two generations out, more diffuse):** Logan, McRae, Harrison, Pleasants, Bowdoin, Tabb, Powell, Cocke, Heath, Stith, Dixon, Field, Harris.

By 1900, these surnames had spread through Virginia, but the **bloodline remains traceable via the well-documented Bolling/Eldridge/Robertson core**. The Pocahontas Foundation maintains an active descendant register; the Order of First Families of Virginia treats Pocahontas descent as a separate documented category. Any claim is verifiable against the published *Pocahontas's Descendants* (1985–2002) — if the candidate ancestor is not in the books or in a properly documented continuation, the claim fails.

### Cross-check against the Eastern Shore Onancock surname universe

Comparing the Pocahontas surname tier-list above against the Eastern Shore surname universe (Tier 1 + Tier 2 from this file):

- **Lewis** appears in both lists. But "Lewis" is a generic surname; the Eastern Shore Lewises and the Pocahontas-descendant Lewises (descendants of Major John Lewis of Augusta County, Piedmont, m. Margaret Lynn) are not the same family. Verifiable but unlikely overlap.
- **Powell** appears in both lists. Same caveat.
- **Harrison** is on the Eastern Shore (Harrison surname documented in 18th-c. Accomack records). But the "Harrison" associated with Pocahontas is the Berkeley-Plantation Tidewater Harrisons (Benjamin Harrison V, Pres. William Henry Harrison, etc.), not Eastern Shore Harrisons.
- **Robertson** is plausibly on the Shore but not strongly characteristic.
- **Bolling, Eldridge, Catlett, Fleming, Gay, Whittle, Branch, Cabell, Skipwith** — none of these are characteristic Eastern Shore surnames. They are Tidewater (Henrico, Charles City, Prince George, James City, Richmond) and Piedmont (Buckingham, Cumberland, Albemarle).

**Bottom-line for Brady's thread 5:** Direct biological Pocahontas descent through the Onancock-area Donahoe network is **possible but unlikely** without a specific Tier 1 Pocahontas surname appearing in Mary Ellen's verifiable pedigree. The Tidewater–Eastern Shore overlap in the 17th–19th centuries was real but limited (the Bay was a barrier, not a highway, for ordinary families), and no characteristic Pocahontas surname is also a characteristic Eastern Shore surname. The folklore is more likely encoding "we descend from a Jamestown founder" rather than a literal Pocahontas claim.

If Brady wants to nail this completely, the resolution is: **find Mary Ellen Donahoe's verifiable maternal-line ancestors back ~5 generations and cross-check the surname list against *Pocahontas's Descendants* (1985–2002 ed.).** It's a 30-minute check at LVA or via FamilySearch's tree.

### The geographic/political contact question — completely different answer

Now: was there *contact* between Mary Ellen Donahoe's likely ancestors and Pocahontas's people, even if not bloodline descent? **Yes, certainly.** Pocahontas's father Wahunsenacawh ("Powhatan") ruled a paramount chiefdom of ~32 Algonquian tribes covering Tidewater Virginia and extending across the Bay to include the **Accomac and Accohannock** of the Eastern Shore as tributary tribes. By Wahunsenacawh's death in 1618 the Eastern Shore tribes paid tribute (corn, deer skins, copper) to him annually. The Accomac werowance Esmy Shichans ("the Laughing King") maintained that tributary relationship through about 1620, when he shifted alliance to the English under Thomas Savage's brokering. Source: Helen C. Rountree, *Pocahontas's People: The Powhatan Indians of Virginia Through Four Centuries* (Univ. of Oklahoma Press, 1990), ch. 1–4; Rountree and Thomas E. Davidson, *Eastern Shore Indians of Virginia and Maryland* (Univ. Press of Virginia, 1996), ch. 1.

So the "Pocahontas connection" at the political/contact level is real for any Eastern Shore Indigenous ancestry that might surface in Mary Ellen Donahoe's line. This is the lever for Brady's question 1 (Native American ancestry, owned by another agent in file 02). The Eastern Shore Algonquian connection IS the Pocahontas connection at the cultural-political level — they are part of the same paramount chiefdom — even though direct biological descent from Pocahontas's son Thomas Rolfe is geographically/genealogically improbable.
