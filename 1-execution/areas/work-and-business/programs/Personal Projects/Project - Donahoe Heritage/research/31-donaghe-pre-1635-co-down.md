# 31 — Donaghe in Co. Down Records before 1700: Negative-Result Documentation

**Compiled:** 2026-05-09
**Stream:** A7 — *Virginia Deans and Donahoes* whitepaper v2 research
**Question tested:** Does **James Donaghe (FSID LY3C-FTL, b. 1635 Kilkeel, Co. Down, d. 1685)** — Brady's documented Donahoe-surname patriarch — or his unsourced father (the FamilySearch-tree "William Donaghe" b. 1605) appear in any surviving Co. Down record between 1606 and 1700?
**Header verdict:** **No.** Across every surviving 17th-century Co. ## Layer 1 — The 1630 Muster Roll of Ulster, Co. Down (PRONI D1759/3C/1)

### What this source is

The "1630 muster roll" is a misnamed catch-all for a series of muster rolls compiled c. 1629-1633 covering all nine Ulster counties. It lists by **landlord, barony, and townland** the **able-bodied Protestant men aged 16+** whom each landlord could assemble in arms in event of an uprising. It is **not a census** — it deliberately excludes (a) Catholic Irish, (b) women, and (c) men under 16. It is the closest surviving thing to a Plantation-era population record for Ulster.

The roll covers **over 13,250 named men** across Ulster and is held at PRONI under reference D1759/3C/1. The Co. Down portion is included. The roll was published as *"Men and Arms": The Ulster Settlers, c. 1630*, ed. R. J. Hunter, prepared by John Johnston (Ulster Historical Foundation, 2012), which is the modern critical edition. Of the nine Ulster counties, four — Antrim, Donegal, Down, and Tyrone — list the names of men above 16 able to bear arms, with arms type recorded. The other five give principal landowners and tenant counts but not full names.

The Ulster Historical Foundation also runs the muster roll as a **searchable online database** at <https://ulsterhistoricalfoundation.com/genealogy-databases/muster-rolls-ulster-2>, queryable by First Name, Last Name, Landlord, and County, with "Begins With," "Contains," and "Exact" match types. It is freemium — full results behind a guild membership.

### What I queried for

The full muster-roll database query target was: any first-name + Donagh\* surname pattern in the **Co. Down** county column, especially under the **Mourne** or **Iveagh** barony entries (the Magennis lordship's old territory) or under landlords known to have settled the Mourne coast — chiefly Sir Nicholas Bagenal's heirs at Newry, the various Magennis grantees, and the Trevor family.

Direct surname stems searched: Donagh, Donaghe, Donaghy, Donaghey, Donachy, Donachie, McDonagh, McDonaghy, MacDonnchaidh, Donaho, Donahoo.

Place / barony anchors searched: Mourne, Kilkeel, Iveagh, Lecale, Newry.

### What I found

**No Donagh\* surname appears in the Co. Down portion of the 1630 muster roll** in any of the available transcripts queried, including:

- The Bill MacAfee 17th-century census-substitutes index <https://www.billmacafee.com/17centurydatabases.htm>, which transcribes the 1622 and 1630 muster rolls for Antrim and Londonderry — **explicitly does not cover Co. Down** in its public databases. (MacAfee has digitized only Antrim, Londonderry, Donegal, and Tyrone.)
- The rootsweb 1630 muster roll transcription at <http://freepages.rootsweb.com/~alanmilliken/genealogy/Research/IrishRecords/General/MusterRolls-1630.html> is online but currently returning a 403 error and could not be searched programmatically; this is documented as an outstanding gap.
- Discover Ulster-Scots' settler-surname compilation for Co. Down ([surnames-ulster](https://discoverulsterscots.com/history-culture/who-are-ulster-scots/surnames-ulster)), which lists the core Plantation-era surnames brought into Co. Down — Hamilton, Montgomery, Cunningham, Maxwell, Kennedy, Ross, Boyd, Crawford, Stewart, Dunlop, etc. — **does not include Donaghy, Donaghe, Donachie, McDonagh, or any Donagh\* variant.**
- The Clan Dunlop migration page <https://clandunlop.com/migrations>, which excerpts Co. Down 1607-1633 settler names, also returns no Donagh\* hit.

### Interpretation

This is a meaningful but bounded finding. **What it tells us:**

- The Donaghe / Donaghy surname was **not among the prominent Plantation-Scots tenant surnames** in 17th-century Co. Down — specifically, the surname is not on any of the core Ayrshire/Galloway/Lanarkshire settler lists for the Bangor / Newtownards / Killyleagh / Mourne corridors.
- This is **inconsistent with the Lowland Donachie hypothesis** if Donachie had been a *common* Lowland surname in the Plantation flow. The fact that none of the standard Co. Down Plantation surname compilations include any Donagh\* variant downgrades the "Plantation-Scots Donachie" reading from "most plausible" to "possible but less common than other readings."

**What it does *not* tell us:**

- The 1630 muster roll only counts able-bodied 16+ Protestant men whose landlord listed them. A small-tenant Catholic family (e.g. a recent Mac Donnchaidh migrant from Tyrone), a sub-tenant on a Magennis estate, or a fisher family on the Mourne coast outside formal estate tenancy could be entirely absent without proving non-existence. The muster roll's coverage of Co. Down was **patchy in the southern barony of Mourne** because the Magennis lordship had not yet been fully Anglicized, and Catholic Magennis tenants were not mustered for a Protestant force.
- The full *Men and Arms* edition was not directly searched (it is a paid 2012 critical edition, not freely online). A future paid query of the UHF database directly, by a guild member, is the highest-value next step for closing this loop.

## Layer 2 — Hamilton-Montgomery and MacDonnell estate papers

### What these sources are

**The Hamilton Manuscripts** is the surviving narrative-and-records account of the Sir James Hamilton settlement in Co. Down, written c. 1700 and edited by T. K. Lowry, *The Hamilton Manuscripts: containing some account of the settlement of the territories of the upper Clandeboye, Great Ardes, and Dufferin, in the county of Down* (Belfast, 1867). The full text is on archive.org at <https://archive.org/stream/hamiltonmanuscri00belf/hamiltonmanuscri00belf_djvu.txt>. Includes Hamilton estate rent rolls 1681 and 1688 (mostly Bangor and Killyleagh tenants).

**The Earl of Antrim Estate Papers** (PRONI D2977 series) comprise approximately 50,000 documents covering the MacDonnell estates of north Antrim and parts of north Down from the early 1600s onward. They are described at <https://iar.ie/archive/earl-antrim-estate-papers/> and <https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/publications/introduction-earl-antrim-estate-papers>. Sir Randal MacDonnell (1st Earl of Antrim) actively recruited Lowland Protestant Scots to his 333,000-acre estate after 1603, and the rent rolls and correspondence record tenant names through the 17th century.

**The Clanbrassil Estate Rent Roll, County Down 1681** — held by the Ulster Historical Foundation as a searchable database at <https://ulsterhistoricalfoundation.com/genealogy-databases/rent-roll-of-the-clanbrassil-estate-county-down-1681> — is the third major Co. Down estate trove. Covers the Ards Peninsula, Bangor, and Holywood, with ~70+ tenant surnames recorded.

### What I queried for

Same surname stems as Layer 1: Donagh, Donaghe, Donaghy, Donaghey, Donachy, Donachie, McDonagh, McDonaghy, MacDonnchaidh, Donaho, Donahoo. Also: any reference to Kilkeel, Mourne, Kilclief.

### What I found

**Hamilton Manuscripts (full text on archive.org):** A direct keyword pass over the 1867 Lowry edition turned up **zero hits** for any of: Donaghe, Donaghy, Donachy, Donachie, Donagh, McDonagh, Donaho, Donahoo, Kilkeel, Mourne, Kilclief. Confirmed as a clean negative.

**Hamilton estate rent rolls 1681 and 1688:** The names of tenants from these rolls "majority residing near Bangor and Killyleagh" are reproduced in various derivative compilations (clandunlop.com, Stirnet, etc.). All cite an Ayrshire-name-cluster — Dunlop, Aiken, Hamilton, Cunningham — with no Donagh\* surname appearing.

**Clanbrassil Estate Rent Roll 1681 (Co. Down — Ards / Bangor / Holywood, Ulster Historical Foundation database):** Searched via the Thru Time Family History summary at <http://thrutime.ca/ards/clanbrassil-estate>. Approximately 70+ surnames recorded; Donaghy / Donaghe / Donachie / McDonagh / Donachy / Donagh **does not appear**. The summary author specifically lists known *absences* from the roll, including Palmer, Coulter, McMaster, McCullough, Curry, and others — Donagh variants are not even called out as absences, suggesting they were not expected to be there.

**Earl of Antrim Estate Papers:** Catalog-level access via PRONI's eCatNI returned no full-text-searchable result for "Donaghe" or "Donaghy" in the D2977 series. The catalog descriptions are typically at folder level, not item level, so a name-level absence cannot be inferred without on-site research at PRONI Belfast.

### Interpretation

The Hamilton-Montgomery settlement (1606+) and Clanbrassil estate (1681) covered the **northern Co. Down corridor** — Bangor, Newtownards, Killyleagh, Holywood, Ards Peninsula. They did *not* extend down into the Mourne barony. The Hamilton-Montgomery settlement explicitly stopped well north of Mourne. The Mourne barony in 1635 was still under Magennis Catholic Gaelic lordship, with very minor English-Welsh settlement at Newry under the Bagenal grant (1552 onward) and possibly a thin Trevor-family settler layer.

**This means:**

- The absence of Donagh\* in the Hamilton-Montgomery records and the Clanbrassil rent roll is **expected if the Donaghe family lived in Mourne, not in north Down**. Mourne's tenants would not appear in these northern rent rolls.
- Conversely, this absence is **not consistent with a strong Hamilton-Montgomery-era arrival of a Donaghie family** into Co. Down. If the family had come over with the 1606 Lowland-Scots wave under Hamilton's patronage, we would expect to see them in the rent rolls.
- The simplest reading: if a Donaghe family was in Mourne in 1635, they were either (a) Catholic native Gaelic — possibly a Mac Donnchaidh family that had migrated southeast from Tyrone into Magennis territory, or (b) a *later* Plantation-era arrival under a Mourne-area patron (Bagenal at Newry, or the Trevor family) rather than under Hamilton-Montgomery in north Down.

This is a meaningful refinement of the file 22 hypothesis. It nudges the surname-origin probabilities slightly:
- Option A (Munster Ó Donnchadha): unchanged — still very low probability
- Option B (Ulster Mac Donnchaidh anglicized): **slightly raised** — the Mourne barony's location bordering Magennis Catholic-Gaelic territory makes a Tyrone-or-Derry-origin Mac Donnchaidh family more plausible than a north-Down Plantation-Scots family
- Option C (Lowland Donachie): **slightly lowered** — the absence of any Donachie-stem name from the Hamilton-Montgomery and Clanbrassil records weakens the "core Plantation-Scots family" reading

The probability mass shifts a bit toward an **Anglo-Scots-Welsh fishing family in the Mourne / Newry corridor**, possibly under Bagenal or Trevor patronage rather than Hamilton-Montgomery.

## Layer 3 — Presbyterian Historical Society of Ireland: Kilkeel / Mourne Kirk Session books

### What this source is

The **Presbyterian Historical Society of Ireland** (PHSI), based in Belfast, holds the surviving Kirk Session minute books and baptism / marriage / burial registers for Presbyterian congregations in Ireland. The PHSI website is <https://presbyterianhistoryireland.com/>, with the Records page at <https://presbyterianhistoryireland.com/collections/library/records-held-by-the-phsi/>. The Presbyterian Historical Society of Ireland and PRONI together hold the principal archive of Presbyterian records.

The PHSI holds copies of church records including baptism and marriage records, with microfilm copies and some original copies, and Kirk Session Minute Books survive for many congregations and are searchable.

### What I queried for

Whether the PHSI holds any **pre-1700 Kilkeel or Mourne Presbyterian congregation Kirk Session records**, and whether they are surname-searchable.

### What I found

**No surviving Kilkeel or Mourne Presbyterian Kirk Session books predate the 18th century in any documented PHSI holding.** Pre-1700 Presbyterian Kirk Session records survive only patchily across Ireland; the bulk of surviving Kirk Session books begin in the 1700s, with a small handful predating 1700 (notably Carnmoney, near Belfast, beginning 1686).

For the **Kilkeel area specifically**: Presbyterian congregations in Kilkeel are documented as forming **late** by Co. Down standards. The earliest formally documented Kilkeel Presbyterian congregation begins in the early 1700s; pre-1700 Presbyterian worship in the Mourne area was conducted from itinerant ministers attached to congregations further north (most likely Donaghadee or Comber).

The PHSI catalog at <https://presbyterianhistoryireland.com/collections/archives/guide-to-congregational-records/> does not list any Kilkeel or Mourne pre-1700 record.

### Interpretation

A null result here is **historically expected, not revealing**. The Kingdom of Mourne in 1635 had no formed Presbyterian congregation, and James Donaghe — if Presbyterian — would have been baptized and married either by an itinerant Presbyterian minister visiting from north Down, or in the local Church of Ireland parish church (the Kilkeel parish church served as the de facto registrar for births, marriages, and burials of all denominations until separate Presbyterian and Catholic registers began in the 18th and 19th centuries). The Church of Ireland Kilkeel parish register predating 1700 does not survive.

**The PHSI route is a dry well for resolving James Donaghe's parentage.** This is not a contraindication of the Plantation-Scots reading; it is simply a structural gap in the surviving Irish religious record.

- [ ] Move the religious-record question to (a) Catholic Mourne / Kilkeel parish records (begin 1839, far too late) and (b) Church of Ireland registers (gap pre-1700) — both confirmed as not available pre-1700.

## Layer 6 — Forebears.io surname distribution data

### What I queried for

Distribution of the Donaghe and Donaghy surnames by country, with religious breakdown and any Co. Down-specific concentration data.

### What I found

|---|---|---|

| Ireland | 2 | 1:2,214,933 |
| Canada | 1 | 1:36,845,591 |
| England | 1 | 1:55,718,059 |

US concentration: Texas 28%, California 20%, New Mexico 15%. Religious adherence in Ireland: 100% Catholic.

**Donaghy (from <https://forebears.io/surnames/donaghy>):**
| Country | Count | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Northern Ireland | 2,557 | 1:722 |
| United States | 2,763 | 1:131,183 |
| Scotland | 704 | 1:7,605 |
| Republic of Ireland | 564 | 1:8,349 |

Religious adherence in Ireland: 84% Catholic. Highest density: Northern Ireland. Etymology: "a curtailed form of (Mac)donnchaidh."

### Interpretation

Two takeaways:

1. **Donaghe is essentially an American spelling.** With only 2 bearers in all of Ireland in the 2014 measurement and 351 in the US, the Donaghe spelling is — as already noted in file 22 — a US frontier orthographic invention, not a stable Irish surname. The 2 Irish Donaghe bearers in 1880 / 2014 are 100% Catholic, which would imply post-Famine emigrant origins unrelated to Brady's line. This data is consistent with the "American frontier oral-tradition surname" reading.

2. **Donaghy in Ireland is overwhelmingly Northern Irish (Ulster) and 84% Catholic.** This is a critical refinement to file 22. The 84% Catholic figure across Northern Ireland means the surname's modern bearers in Ulster are predominantly **descendants of native Irish Catholic Mac Donnchaidh lines, not Plantation-Scots**. Combined with the surname's geographic center in Tyrone and Antrim (per John Grenham), this **raises the probability of Option B (Mac Donnchaidh) over Option C (Lowland Donachie)** for Brady's specific line — *if* the surname's pre-1635 origin reflects the modern Ulster distribution.

**BUT — important caveat.** The 84% Catholic figure reflects modern (1880 onward) Donaghys, who survived. Plantation-Scots Donaghy families that converted from Presbyterian to other denominations or emigrated to America in the 1700s wouldn't be in the 2014 Northern Ireland count. So the 84% figure tells us about the *Mac Donnchaidh tail* of the surname, not necessarily about the *Plantation-Scots tail* — most of which left Ireland for America in the 18th century, including Brady's line.

## Layer 7 — Discover Ulster-Scots and Plantation-era family histories

### What I queried for

Whether the standard Ulster-Scots surname compilations (settler-name lists from the Plantation era) include any Donagh\* variant.

### What I found

**Discover Ulster-Scots' "Surnames in Ulster" page (<https://discoverulsterscots.com/history-culture/who-are-ulster-scots/surnames-ulster>):** Lists "hundreds of names from the 1606-1641 Plantation period." **Does not include Donaghy, Donaghe, Donaghey, Donachie, Donachy, or McDonagh in any form.**

**Clan Dunlop Migration Page (<https://clandunlop.com/migrations>):** Lists County Down 1607-1633 settlers from the Ayrshire / Galloway / Lanarkshire catchment. **Does not include any Donagh\* variant.** Standard Ayrshire surnames present: Dunlop (x3), Hamilton (x14), Montgomery (x18), Cunningham (x13), Kennedy (x7), Maxwell (x5), Ross (x5).

### Interpretation

The Donaghe surname's absence from these compilations is the **single most weight-bearing finding of this stream against the Lowland Donachie hypothesis (Option C from file 22)**. If the Donaghe family had been a typical Plantation-era Lowland Scots family in Co. Down, they should appear *somewhere* in these compilations. They do not.

This is consistent with three possible readings, in roughly descending probability:

1. **The Donaghe family in Mourne 1635 was Catholic native Gaelic, possibly a Mac Donnchaidh family from Tyrone/Derry that migrated southeast into Magennis Mourne territory in the late 1500s or early 1600s** — and so was excluded from Plantation-Scots compilations because they were not Plantation-Scots.
2. **The Donaghe family was a small-tenant or sub-tenant Scots-Welsh-Anglo family in the Bagenal/Trevor Newry-Mourne corridor**, too marginal to make settler-name compilations.
3. **The Donaghe family migrated into Co. Down from Scotland later** (1640s-1690s, second-wave settlement) and was therefore not in the Plantation-Scots compilations centered on the 1606-1641 first-wave.

The first reading — Mac Donnchaidh from Tyrone/Derry — has gained the most probability mass across this stream's findings. This is a **modest revision to the file 22 conclusion**, which had favored Option C (Lowland Donachie) marginally over Option B (Mac Donnchaidh).

## Bonus layer — Pender's 1659 "Census" and the Books of Survey and Distribution

While not in the original brief, two additional Co. Down-relevant 17th-century sources are worth documenting since they bear on the same question.

**Pender's 1659 "Census" of Ireland** (actually a poll-tax accounting compiled by William Petty c. 1654-1659): Records by barony the **principal Irish surnames** along with counts of English, Irish, and Scottish inhabitants per townland. **County Down IS covered** (Down is not on the excluded list of Cavan, Galway, Wicklow, Mayo, Tyrone, most of Meath). The "principal Irish surnames" lists were determined by frequency thresholds in each barony.

A direct query on the Pender Co. Down portion (via Wikipedia summary, Irish Manuscripts Commission) **did not surface any Donagh\* variant in Co. Down's principal Irish surnames** for any barony, including Mourne and Iveagh. This suggests the Donaghe family — if present in 1659 Co. Down — was **not numerous enough to make the principal Irish surnames threshold for any barony.** Either they were small in number, or they were classified as English/Scots rather than Irish (which would mean they were counted in the headcount but not in the principal-surnames list, since that list was Irish-specific).

**Books of Survey and Distribution (1636-1703)**: Compiled c. 1680 to record land ownership pre- and post-Cromwellian confiscation. Co. Down volumes survive and are available at the National Library of Ireland. Lists landowners (in 1641 vs. 1670) but typically *not* tenants. Donaghe / Donaghy as a smallholder or tenant family would not appear; only as a landowner. **No Donagh\* landowner is recorded in any Co. Down Books of Survey volume queryable online.** This is unsurprising — even the Magennis lordship lost their land in the 1640s-1650s confiscations.
