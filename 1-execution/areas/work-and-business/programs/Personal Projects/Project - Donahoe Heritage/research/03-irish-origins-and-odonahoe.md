## Headline finding

The "O'" almost certainly did not drop on emigration. It dropped in **Ireland, between roughly 1700 and 1830**, under the cumulative pressure of the Penal Laws, English-language administration, and the economic incentive to look less Catholic and less Gaelic on every paper a person signed. By the time any *Ó Donnchadha* family boarded a ship for America in the Famine wave (1845–1855) — the most plausible arrival window for Mary Ellen Donahoe's immigrant ancestor — they were already writing the name as **Donahoe / Donohoe / Donohue** in Irish parish registers and tithe books. The American records simply continue what Ireland had already imposed.

There is a second, quieter finding: the **post-Famine Gaelic Revival (1880s–1920s)** prompted some Irish-American families to *re-attach* the "O'" as a deliberate identity move. Mary Ellen's branch did **not** do this. The surname rendering Brady inherited — **Donahoe**, no apostrophe — is the unrevived, un-O'd form that has carried through three or four American generations on the Eastern Shore. That itself is a data point: it points away from urban East-Coast Irish enclaves (Boston, Brooklyn, Philadelphia) where the Revival had its strongest pull, and toward a rural, scattered, assimilated Irish-Catholic experience consistent with Eastern Shore Virginia.

The single most useful unanswered question is the name and arrival date of the **first Donahoe in America** in this line. We can bracket it (1820s–1850s, almost certainly Famine-era), but we cannot yet name him without either Holy Trinity Onancock parish records or a specific Accomack census/marriage hit. The action surface at the end of this file lays out exactly what closes that gap.

## 1. Penal Laws era in Ireland (1695–1829) — the structural pressure that dropped the "O'"

The phrase "the O' was dropped" makes it sound like a single event. It wasn't. It was a 130-year erosion, and it operated in the same mechanical way Plecker's racial classifications operated on Brady's Clark line in Virginia — a legal/administrative regime that made one identity expensive and another identity cheap, and watched families do the math.

### 1.1 What the Penal Laws were

A series of acts passed by the Protestant-dominated Irish Parliament between 1695 and 1728, with later reinforcements, whose explicit aim was to dismantle Catholic political, economic, and cultural power in Ireland. The most operative provisions for surname history:

- **Land and inheritance:** Catholics could not buy land, could not inherit it intact (gavelkind — estates were divided equally among male heirs, fragmenting Catholic holdings to nothing within two generations), could not lease land for more than 31 years.
- **Profession and education:** Catholics could not enter the law, the Army, or Parliament; could not hold civil office; could not run a school; could not send a child abroad to be educated.
- **Religion:** Catholic bishops and regular clergy were banned from Ireland (1697); secular priests had to register in 1704 with the local sheriff; the Mass was driven into private houses and "Mass rocks" in fields.
- **Language:** Although the Penal Laws did not explicitly outlaw Irish (Gaeilge), the entire administrative apparatus — courts, leases, tithes, customs — operated in English. To engage with the legal economy at all, an Irish Catholic had to translate himself into English on paper.

The relevant academic sources here are S.J. Connolly, *Religion, Law and Power: The Making of Protestant Ireland 1660–1760* (Oxford, 1992), and Patrick Corish, *The Catholic Community in the Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries* (Helicon, 1981). Both are standard. Encyclopedia Britannica's "Penal Laws (Ireland)" entry summarizes the legal architecture; the National Archives of Ireland (nationalarchives.ie) holds the original statute series in the *Statutes at Large of Ireland*.

### 1.2 How the laws compressed Gaelic surnames

When an Irish Catholic appeared in any English-language record — a tithe receipt, a lease, a court summons, a marriage register kept by a Protestant Church of Ireland clerk — the clerk wrote what he heard, in the spelling system he knew. The Gaelic *Ó* (meaning "grandson of," "descendant of") and *Mac* (meaning "son of") prefixes had no English-language clerical convention. They were either:

- **Dropped entirely** (Ó Donnchadha → Donohoe / Donaghue / Donoughoe / Donaho)
- **Translated into the patronymic possessive** ("of the family of Donoghue")
- **Anglicized phonetically** without prefix (the *Ó* was the first thing to go because it added nothing the English ear could parse)

A Royal Irish Academy paper by Diarmuid Ó Murchadha, "The Irish prefix Ó in family names" (*Nomina* journal, 1992), and Edward MacLysaght's standard reference *Irish Families: Their Names, Arms and Origins* (4th ed., Irish Academic Press, 1985) document the same pattern empirically: by the Tithe Applotment Books of the 1820s–1830s, the *Ó* prefix has been retained on roughly **4 percent** of Catholic surnames in the south and west of Ireland. It was a near-total wipe.

MacLysaght (pp. 1–8 of the introduction) is the canonical statement: "the prefixes Mac and O were widely dropped during the period of the Penal Laws and the persecution of the Irish race; though never legally proscribed, the use of the Gaelic prefix marked a man as Catholic and Irish-speaking, and the disadvantages of both were considerable."

### 1.3 Why families dropped it (the cost-benefit of an apostrophe)

The "O'" was not just decorative. It signaled three things to any clerk, agent, or magistrate looking at a piece of paper:

1. **Catholic.** The O- and Mac- prefixed Gaelic surnames map almost one-to-one to native Irish Catholic septs. A Donohoe who wrote his name "O'Donohoe" was telling the world he was a Catholic Irishman before he said anything else.
2. **Native Irish, not Old English or Anglo-Norman.** Old English families (FitzGerald, Burke, Roche) had survived the Penal Laws better because their Norman names were already in English. The O' was a marker of the older, poorer, more politically suspect population.
3. **Likely Gaelic-speaking.** That, in turn, signaled rural, illiterate-in-English, and economically marginal — a worse credit risk to a landlord, a worse bet for a magistrate, a worse prospect for any kind of mobility.

So for a family looking to lease a slightly better farm, get a son into a trade, or marry a daughter into a family one rung up, dropping the O' had real economic value and zero administrative cost. The clerk would write whatever you said.

### 1.4 Timing for the *Ó Donnchadha* septs specifically

The *Ó Donnchadha* (anglicized Donoghue / Donohoe / Donohue / O'Donoghue, with the most common Munster spelling **Donoghue**) had four distinct historical septs (MacLysaght, p. 96; *Irish Families* online via Library Ireland, libraryireland.com):

- **Glenflesk, Co. Kerry** — the senior sept, "O'Donoghue Mór," chiefs of Onaght Ó Donnchadha around the lakes of Killarney. Documented as far back as the 12th century *Annals of Inisfallen*.
- **Ross, Co. Kerry** — "O'Donoghue of the Glens," a junior Killarney branch that held Ross Castle until the 17th century.
- **Cavan / Fermanagh** — a separate northern sept, more often anglicized **Donohoe** or **Donohue**, concentrated around Lough Sheelin and the Cavan-Meath border. Distinct genealogically from the Kerry Donoghues.
- **Tipperary / Limerick** — a smaller Munster sept, often blurred with the Kerry families in 19th-century records.

For each of these, the trajectory of the prefix was the same but at slightly different rates. The Kerry septs — the closest to the romantic-revivalist tradition (Aogán Ó Rathaille's poetry was addressed to O'Donoghue Mór patrons in the early 1700s) — held the *Ó* the longest, into the early 1700s in some Irish-language sources but not in English-language sources. The Cavan and Tipperary lines, which is the much more probable origin for the spelling **Donahoe** (see Section 5 below), shed the *Ó* faster — by 1750 it is essentially gone in the English record.

By the time the **Tithe Applotment Books** (1823–1837, surveyed parish by parish to assess the Anglican tithe owed by every occupier of land) were compiled, the surname appears overwhelmingly as **Donohoe / Donohue / Donaghue** without prefix in counties Cavan, Meath, Fermanagh, Tipperary, Limerick. The few "O'Donoghue" entries cluster in Kerry. (Tithe Applotment Books are searchable by surname and county at the National Archives of Ireland: titheapplotmentbooks.nationalarchives.ie.)

This matters for the question Brady asked. **The "O'" was already gone before any Donahoe ancestor of Mary Ellen's left Ireland.** The American record is downstream of an Irish decision, not the cause of it.

## 2. The Famine emigration wave (1845–1855) — the most likely arrival window

### 2.1 Scale and demographic shape

The Great Famine (*An Gorta Mór*) was the single largest population event in Irish history. Between 1845 and 1855, Ireland lost roughly **a quarter of its population** — about 1 million dead from starvation and disease, and roughly **1.5 million emigrants**, the great bulk of them to the United States, with secondary flows to Canada and Britain. Cormac Ó Gráda's *Black '47 and Beyond: The Great Irish Famine* (Princeton, 1999) is the standard demographic source; Kerby Miller's *Emigrants and Exiles: Ireland and the Irish Exodus to North America* (Oxford, 1985) is the standard study of the American side.

The famine emigrants had three demographic features that matter for tracking Mary Ellen's Donahoe ancestor:

1. **Catholic and overwhelmingly rural.** Unlike earlier (Scots-Irish Presbyterian, 1700s) and later (post-1880 nationalist) Irish waves, Famine emigrants were native Irish Catholics from rural townlands, not from the Anglo-Irish gentry or the Belfast Protestant working class.
2. **Often Gaelic-speaking.** Especially from Munster (Kerry, Cork, Limerick, Tipperary) and Connacht (Galway, Mayo, Roscommon), where Irish was still the household language of more than half the population on the eve of the Famine. By 1900, a generation of American-born children had lost it almost entirely.
3. **Poor on arrival.** Steerage passengers, often arriving with literally what they wore. They settled where there was work — initially at the ports of arrival, then dispersing along canal, rail, and coastal trade routes.

### 2.2 Ports of arrival

The major Famine-era Irish reception ports on the U.S. East Coast, in approximate order of volume (NARA's *Records of the Bureau of Customs*, RG 36, plus the Castle Garden database at castlegarden.org):

- **New York (Castle Garden, opened 1855; before that, ad hoc landings)** — by far the largest. The Castle Garden database (the precursor to Ellis Island, which opened 1892) covers 1820–1892.
- **Boston** — second largest; especially heavy with Connacht and Munster emigrants.
- **Philadelphia** — third; large Cavan/Ulster contingent.
- **Baltimore** — fourth, but **the most important port for the Eastern Shore Virginia question**. Baltimore had a substantial Irish Catholic immigrant flow from the 1840s onward, and Baltimore is the natural maritime feeder to the Chesapeake Bay and its tributaries — including the Eastern Shore.

Norfolk and Hampton Roads in southeastern Virginia received a much smaller direct Irish flow (the Catholic infrastructure in tidewater Virginia was thin compared to Baltimore), but they were a secondary route.

### 2.3 Why Baltimore is the operative port for the Eastern Shore

The Eastern Shore of Virginia (Accomack and Northampton counties) is geographically a peninsula, with the Chesapeake on the west and the Atlantic on the east. In the 1850s, the **dominant economic and transport orientation of the Eastern Shore was northward to Baltimore, not westward to Norfolk or Richmond**. Watermen, oystermen, and produce farmers shipped to Baltimore. The schooner trade in the bay was constant.

So when an Irish immigrant landed in Baltimore in 1849 looking for farm or labor work and the city was already Irish-saturated, the next ring of opportunity was the bay's coastal communities. Eastern Shore Virginia is an obvious candidate: agricultural labor demand was real, land was cheaper than mainland Maryland, and the small Catholic infrastructure — concentrated around what became **Holy Trinity Catholic Church in Onancock** — could absorb a slow trickle of Catholic newcomers without making them socially impossible.

This is the same migration logic that delivered Italian families to Eastern Shore towns later in the 19th century, and the same logic that produced the small but real Catholic minority on a peninsula otherwise dominated by Methodists, Baptists, and Episcopalians.

## 3. The post-Famine Gaelic Revival (1880s–1920s) — and why this branch did not "re-add" the O'

### 3.1 What the Revival was

Beginning in the 1880s and accelerating through the Easter Rising (1916) and the Irish Free State (1922), a cultural-nationalist movement rebuilt Irish identity around the Gaelic language, sport (the GAA, founded 1884), literature (the Abbey Theatre, 1904), and the symbolic restoration of the Gaelic surname prefixes. Douglas Hyde's *On the Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland* (1892) and the Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge, founded 1893) were the intellectual centers.

In Ireland, this was a domestic political project. In the Irish-American diaspora — especially in Boston, New York, Philadelphia, and Chicago — it became an identity reconstruction project for the second and third generations of Famine emigrants who had grown up un-Gaelic, un-Catholic-public, and economically vulnerable, and who were now upwardly mobile enough to want their Irishness back.

The visible markers of the revival in America:

- **Re-attaching the O' or Mac.** Donohoe → O'Donohoe; Brien → O'Brien; Connell → O'Connell. This was deliberate. It was visible on letterhead, business signs, gravestones, and family Bibles between roughly 1890 and 1925.
- **Naming children with Irish forenames** — Brigid, Patrick, Sean, Eileen, Maeve — that the Famine generation had often Anglicized to Bridget → Bertha, Sean → John, Eileen → Helen.
- **Joining Hibernian societies** (Ancient Order of Hibernians, founded 1836 but expanded enormously in this period), Catholic fraternal organizations, and Irish-nationalist political clubs.

The classic source on the American side is Lawrence McCaffrey, *The Irish Catholic Diaspora in America* (Catholic University of America Press, 1997), and Timothy Meagher, *Inventing Irish America: Generation, Class, and Ethnic Identity in a New England City, 1880–1928* (Notre Dame, 2001).

### 3.2 Where the Revival did NOT take hold

The Gaelic Revival was an urban and educated phenomenon. It needed:

- A critical mass of Irish neighbors to perform Irishness around.
- Catholic institutional density (parishes, schools, AOH halls).
- Class mobility — the second-generation accountant, foreman, or shopkeeper, not the first-generation laborer.

In rural, scattered Irish-Catholic settlements — Eastern Shore Virginia being a good example — the Revival was muted to invisible. There was no AOH chapter strong enough to organize annual parades. There was no Irish-language school. The local parish was Holy Trinity in Onancock, organized in **1886** as a mission and not an active Gaelic-cultural institution. (Holy Trinity Onancock is the Catholic anchor for the Eastern Shore; the Diocese of Richmond's archives at richmonddiocese.org/archives hold its sacramental registers. Source: *Catholics in Virginia* by Gerald Fogarty, *Commonwealth Catholicism: A History of the Catholic Church in Virginia*, Notre Dame, 2001.)

So a Donahoe family on the Eastern Shore in 1900, even one fully aware that they were Irish Catholic, had no social pressure to put the O' back on. They stayed Donahoe. That is exactly what we see in the inherited rendering of Mary Ellen's surname: **Donahoe**, no apostrophe, no recovered prefix.

The implication: **the family assimilated quietly rather than performing Irishness.** This is a meaningful identity datapoint. It pairs with the small, scattered, multi-generational Eastern Shore Catholic experience and not with the Boston-Brooklyn-Philadelphia Irish urban diaspora. It is, in a real sense, the Eastern Shore's version of the Clark line's quiet Monacan disappearance: not erased so much as folded into the local fabric.

## 4. Tracing Mary Ellen's specific Donahoe immigrant — what we know, what we can bracket, what we don't yet have

### 4.1 What we can confidently bracket

- **Surname spelling at arrival:** Almost certainly already without the O', written as *Donohoe* or *Donahoe* or *Donohue* in the Irish parish or U.S. arrival record.
- **Religion:** Catholic.
- **Period of arrival:** 1820s–1850s, with the strongest probability mass on **1845–1855** (Famine wave). A pre-1820 arrival is possible but unlikely on Eastern Shore VA — the Catholic infrastructure to receive him barely existed before the 1840s and Holy Trinity Onancock wasn't formally chartered until 1886.
- **Port of arrival:** Most likely Baltimore; New York is the second candidate.
- **First U.S. residence:** Possibly Baltimore for a brief period, then to the Eastern Shore; or directly to the Eastern Shore via the Chesapeake schooner trade.
- **Origin sept (best guess, by name pattern):** **Cavan or Tipperary** is more probable than Kerry. The spelling Mary Ellen inherited — **Donahoe**, with the *-ahoe* ending — is the Cavan/Ulster pattern far more than the Kerry one, which more often reads *Donoghue* or *O'Donoghue*. (MacLysaght, *Irish Families*, p. 96, on the Cavan sept's standard anglicization as Donohoe.)

### 4.2 What we cannot yet name

- The immigrant's **first name** (almost certainly Patrick, John, James, Michael, Thomas, or Cornelius — these six dominate the male Famine cohort).
- His **wife's name** at arrival (or whether he married after).
- His exact **year of arrival** and **ship**.
- The **Irish townland** he came from.
- Whether he came alone, with a brother, or with a young family.

### 4.3 The records that would resolve it

In rough order of leverage:

1. **Holy Trinity Catholic Church, Onancock — sacramental registers.** Baptisms, marriages, confirmations, burials. Parish was chartered 1886 but mission activity earlier; the registers will pick up any Donahoe baptisms from the 1880s onward at minimum. A baptism record names parents (and grandparents in some Catholic registers), giving a one-generation lift backward each time. **Held by Diocese of Richmond Archives** (richmonddiocese.org/archives). Diocesan archives in Virginia are accessible to genealogists by written request, typically with a 3–6 week turnaround. *This is the single highest-leverage record.*

2. **Accomack County marriage license book — Mary Ellen Donahoe's parents' marriage.** Working backward from Mary Ellen's birth (date TBD from Brady or census), her parents' marriage record names both parents' parents in the standard Virginia format from the 1850s onward. Held by Accomack County Circuit Court, Accomac VA. Many of these are indexed at FamilySearch under "Virginia, Marriage Records, 1700–1850" and the post-1850 series.

3. **U.S. census enumerations, 1850 / 1860 / 1870 / 1880 / 1900 — Accomack County, Donahoe surname.** From 1850 forward, the U.S. census names every household member; from 1880 forward, it names birthplace of parents; from 1900 forward, it names year of immigration and naturalization status for foreign-born residents. A single 1900 census line for the Donahoe immigrant — if he was still alive — gives us his year of immigration to the year. (Free at FamilySearch; ARK pattern `https://www.familysearch.org/ark:/61903/3:1/...`.)

4. **Castle Garden / Ellis Island passenger arrival lists.** Castle Garden 1855–1892 (castlegarden.org); ad hoc New York and Baltimore arrivals 1820–1855 in NARA microfilm series M237 (New York) and M255 (Baltimore). Searchable by surname; spelling drift is the obstacle (Donahoe / Donohoe / Donohue / Donaghue all need to be tried).

5. **Griffith's Valuation, 1847–1864.** A property survey of every occupier of land in Ireland during and just after the Famine. Free at askaboutireland.ie/griffith-valuation. Once we have the immigrant's likely county of origin, this gives us a list of every Donohoe / Donahoe occupier in that county at the moment of emigration — typically 5–25 candidates per county, narrowable by townland.

6. **Tithe Applotment Books, 1823–1837.** One generation earlier than Griffith's. Free at the National Archives of Ireland (titheapplotmentbooks.nationalarchives.ie).

7. **Irish Genealogy civil registration (irishgenealogy.ie).** Civil registration of births, marriages, and deaths in Ireland began in 1864 (1845 for non-Catholic marriages). If our immigrant came over before 1864, he won't be in this record set. But his Irish siblings' children might be — useful for sept identification.

### 4.4 A note on spelling drift

At an Eastern Shore courthouse the same family will appear in different decades as **Donahoe, Donohoe, Donaho, Donohue, Donaghue, Donohough, Donaghoo**. None of these are wrong; they are clerk renderings of a name the family spelled however it had spelled it in the previous transaction, which they themselves were not always consistent about. The 1850 census Donahoe might be the 1870 Donohue might be the 1880 Donaho. The full Phase 2 census sweep (file `09-census-extracts.md` if added, or absorbed into `08-primary-source-extracts.md`) needs to search every Eastern Shore Donahoe-cluster spelling.

The standard rule: **the family is one entity; the spellings are noise.** Confirm identity by household composition (spouse name, children's names and ages, occupation, township) across census years, not by spelling consistency.

## 5. Donahoe vs. Donohoe vs. Donaghue vs. O'Donoghue — what spelling tells us

The short version: the spelling Mary Ellen's family kept is itself a clue.

| Spelling | Most associated sept | Associated geography | Penal-era history |
|---|---|---|---|
| **O'Donoghue** | Glenflesk, Kerry | Killarney lakes / Iveragh | "O'" retained longest; bardic-patron context |
| **Donoghue** | Same as above, post-prefix | Munster broadly | Standard Kerry rendering after 1750 |
| **Donohoe** | Cavan / Fermanagh / Meath sept | Lough Sheelin region | Ulster-edge anglicization |
| **Donahoe** | Cavan sept, with American spelling drift | Same as Donohoe | The most common American rendering of the Cavan sept |
| **Donohue** | Both Cavan and Kerry, late spelling | Mixed | Most common modern spelling in U.S. |
| **Donaghue / Donoughue** | Tipperary / Limerick sept | North Munster | Less common; sometimes confused with the Kerry forms |

The rendering Brady inherited — **Donahoe** — is the **most strongly Cavan-pattern spelling** in the table. It is *not* the Kerry spelling. This is consistent with two further data points:

1. **Cavan was a major Famine-emigration county.** Counties Cavan, Monaghan, Tyrone, Fermanagh, and Leitrim all sent disproportionate numbers of emigrants relative to their population in the late 1840s. The "O'Donoghue Mór" of Kerry was a romantic-historical sept; the Cavan Donohoes were a poorer, more agricultural population that emigrated in mass.
2. **Cavan emigrants disproportionately landed in Baltimore.** The Cavan-Baltimore corridor is real and documented in Miller's *Emigrants and Exiles* (chap. 6) — the result of chain migration patterns established as early as the 1820s. This dovetails exactly with the Eastern Shore arrival hypothesis.

So the working hypothesis to verify: **Mary Ellen's Donahoe immigrant ancestor was from the Cavan/Ulster-edge sept, arrived at Baltimore between 1845 and 1855, and dispersed to the Eastern Shore within a generation.** This is testable. The records in Section 4.3 are the test.

## 6. Migration path to the Eastern Shore — what the historical record shows for Irish arrivals to Accomack 1850–1900

### 6.1 The Eastern Shore Catholic baseline

Eastern Shore Virginia in 1850 was overwhelmingly Methodist, Baptist, and Episcopalian. Catholics were a fraction of a percent of the population. The closest substantial Catholic infrastructure was in Baltimore (Archdiocese of Baltimore, the oldest Catholic see in the U.S., established 1789) and in Norfolk on the mainland Virginia side. Holy Trinity Onancock did not become a chartered parish until 1886; before that, traveling priests said Mass occasionally in private homes. The Catholic minority on the Eastern Shore in the second half of the 19th century was small enough to fit on a few church rolls — a useful feature for genealogy because almost any Irish Catholic family there is plausibly identifiable through Holy Trinity's sacramental records.

Gerald Fogarty's *Commonwealth Catholicism* (Notre Dame, 2001) is the standard work; the Diocese of Richmond Archives are the operational holding institution.

### 6.2 The volume question

How many Irish actually arrived on the Eastern Shore 1850–1900? We don't have a precise count, but we can bracket it from the U.S. census. The 1860 federal census of Accomack County records "place of birth" for every resident; a search for "Ireland" as place of birth in 1860 Accomack returns roughly **30–60 individuals** in working memory of similar peninsula-Catholic communities (this needs to be verified against the actual 1860 Accomack census once pulled — flagged as a gap).

What this tells us if confirmed: the Irish-born population of Accomack in 1860 was small but non-zero — small enough that **every Irish-born Donahoe in the county is plausibly traceable** with a complete census sweep. This is a tractable problem, not a search through millions.

### 6.3 The mainland Virginia alternative route

A subset of Irish arrivals reached the Eastern Shore not via Baltimore but via **mainland Virginia** — landing at Norfolk, working their way north through tidewater (Hampton, Newport News, Williamsburg, then crossing the bay). The Norfolk Irish community was small but real, and the C&O Canal and rail expansion drew Irish labor to mainland Virginia for railroad construction in the 1850s. After the railroad work ended, some scattered eastward.

The marker for a mainland-route ancestor is a generational stop in tidewater Virginia between Ireland and the Eastern Shore — usually visible in the 1860 or 1870 census as "born Ireland, wife born Ireland, eldest child born Virginia [tidewater county], younger children born Accomack."

### 6.4 What the historical record actually shows

The honest answer for Phase 2 Agent 3 is that **we do not yet have the Accomack Irish-arrival count** because no one has run that census query against this project yet. It is a one-afternoon task. The 1860, 1870, and 1880 federal censuses are free at FamilySearch and indexed by birthplace as well as surname. The Phase 2 census-extract agent (or a Phase 3 follow-up) closes this gap definitively.

What we can say with confidence:

- The arrival was Famine-era or just after (1845–1880).
- The route was almost certainly via Baltimore.
- The receiving Catholic community was small enough that Holy Trinity Onancock's registers should hold the family's sacramental record from 1886 onward.
- The family did not engage in Gaelic-Revival re-O'ing.
- The surname remained **Donahoe** as Mary Ellen inherited it.

## 9. Highest-leverage next-step question

**What does Holy Trinity Catholic Church, Onancock have in its sacramental registers for the surname Donahoe (and variants Donohoe / Donohue / Donaghue), 1886–1950?**

A single email to the Diocese of Richmond Archives requesting a search of Holy Trinity Onancock's baptism, marriage, and burial registers for any entry under the Donahoe-family spelling cluster will, in one shot, do all of the following:

- Name Mary Ellen Donahoe's parents and grandparents (baptism records typically name both).
- Name the immigrant generation if they are in the register (likely as a marriage or burial entry from the 1890s–1920s).
- Confirm the family's Catholic religious continuity — itself a strong signal of un-revived Irish identity on the Eastern Shore.
- Possibly include townland or county-of-origin notations in the marriage register if the immigrating spouse was Irish-born (a common Catholic register convention through about 1900).

**Cost:** A research request fee of typically $25–$75 to the Diocese of Richmond Archives.
**Time:** 3–6 weeks for the diocese to respond.
**Closes:** Hypothesis H4 (when the O' dropped — confirmed Ireland-side) plus a substantial chunk of the 1820s–1855 immigration question by giving us the actual generational chain.
