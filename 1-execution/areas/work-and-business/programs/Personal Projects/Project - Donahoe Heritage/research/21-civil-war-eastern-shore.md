## 2. The Eastern Shore's unique war — the structural backdrop these ancestors lived through

### 2.1 Secession by referendum, occupation by force (1861)

Virginia's Ordinance of Secession passed the convention on April 17, 1861 and was ratified by statewide referendum on May 23, 1861. Accomack and Northampton voted with Virginia. Local Confederate organizing followed: the **39th Virginia Infantry Regiment** was raised in summer 1861 under Col. Charles Smith, with eleven companies — eight infantry, two cavalry, one light artillery — drawn primarily from Accomack and Northampton (Companies E, F, G, H, I, and L per FamilySearch's Accomack County genealogy guide). The unit drilled at Drummondtown (now Accomac Court House) and Eastville. Approximately 800 men enrolled.

The geography made the Confederate hold untenable. The Eastern Shore is a 70-mile peninsula bordered by Chesapeake Bay west, Atlantic east, connected to mainland Virginia only by water; the only land connection runs north into Union-loyal Maryland. Norfolk, Hampton Roads, and Fort Monroe (Union-held throughout) sat directly across the Chesapeake. From the Confederate war-fighting perspective, the Eastern Shore was an asset that could not be supplied or reinforced.

### 2.2 The Wishart Point engagement (October 5, 1861) — the only real combat

The single Civil War engagement on Eastern Shore Virginia soil involving any of these ancestors' neighbors was the **Wishart Point engagement on October 5, 1861.** USS *Louisiana*, commanded by Lt. Alexander Murray, learned of a schooner being outfitted for privateering near Wishart Point. Two boats with 23 sailors under Acting Masters Hartman K. Furniss and Edward Hooker landed and were opposed by Companies E and H of the 39th Virginia Infantry (~75 men). The Federal landing force burned the schooner and withdrew with four wounded; Confederate accounts report one casualty. **This is the only documented military action on Eastern Shore VA soil.** No engagement of any consequence followed, before or after. The war "happened to" the Eastern Shore as occupation, not combat.

### 2.3 Lockwood's Expedition (November 14–23, 1861)

On November 14, 1861, **Brigadier General Henry H. Lockwood** marched south from Snow Hill, Maryland with approximately 4,500 troops — primarily the **1st Eastern Shore Maryland Volunteer Infantry** (Col. James Wallace, recruited from Dorchester, Caroline, Talbot, Somerset MD), the **1st Delaware**, the **5th New York**, the **Purnell Legion**, and supporting Maryland units. Lockwood (a Delaware native, US Naval Academy professor) issued a proclamation guaranteeing the property and rights of any Eastern Shore resident who took an oath of allegiance and laid down arms. The proclamation explicitly preserved slavery as it existed under Virginia law — a political concession critical to Maryland and Delaware (slaveholding Union states).

The 39th Virginia Infantry under Col. Smith did not give battle. The unit dispersed; Smith eventually crossed to mainland Virginia. Lockwood's column reached Accomac Court House on November 17 and Eastville on November 18, occupying both county seats without combat. **By late November 1861 — within seven months of Sumter — the Union held the entire Eastern Shore peninsula.**

This is the moment Susan's ancestors' war effectively ended militarily and began civilly. From November 1861 forward, every ancestor in this line lived under federal occupation.

### 2.4 The 39th Virginia's afterlife — the Confederate channel that survived

The 39th VA was formally disbanded on **February 3, 1862** after failed reorganization attempts. The single most important post-dissolution unit for Eastern Shore Confederate genealogy is the **"Eastern Shore Refugees"** company, organized in Norfolk on **February 5, 1862** by **Captain John H. White** for the term of the war. The company numbered approximately 80 men, the great majority of whom had served in the 39th VA and made it across the Chesapeake. On June 25, 1862, the Refugees were assigned as 2nd Co. G of the **46th Virginia Volunteer Infantry**, and by September 1862 had been re-designated 4th and final Co. F of the same regiment.

**This is the canonical Eastern Shore Confederate enlistment channel.** Per Alton Brooks Parker Barnes's *Pungoteague to Petersburg* (3 vols., 1988–1994 — the standard regional treatment, *not* "Floyd Stallings" as `05-civil-war.md` originally suggested; that attribution was incorrect), a total of **452 men from the Eastern Shore of Virginia served in the Confederate Army** beyond those who took up "running the blockade." That 452 figure is the relevant denominator for any Bull / Kellam / Rogers / Boggs / Floyd / Garrison Confederate-service hypothesis.

The other potentially relevant Confederate unit named in `05-civil-war.md` was the 39th Virginia Cavalry Battalion (Richardson's Battalion of Scouts, Guides, and Couriers). FamilySearch's regimental history identifies this as Gen. R.E. Lee's personal cavalry command, recruited "many men from Frederick and surrounding counties" — i.e., **not** the Eastern Shore. The naming similarity to the 39th Virginia Infantry is coincidental. Eastern Shore Confederate ancestors should be searched in 39th VA Infantry, then 46th VA Infantry Co. F (Eastern Shore Refugees), then secondarily in 9th VA Infantry, 41st VA Infantry, and 6th VA Cavalry — not in the 39th VA Cavalry Battalion.

### 2.5 Daily life under occupation 1861–1865

Practical effects on the civilian population:

- **Oath of allegiance system.** To retain ordinary civil rights — sell crops, register a deed, transact at the courthouse, hold property, draw a salary, ship goods to Baltimore or Norfolk — Eastern Shore residents took an oath of allegiance to the United States, administered by Provost Marshals at Drummondtown (Accomac Court House) and Eastville from late 1861. Records survive in scattered form in **NARA RG 393** (Records of US Army Continental Commands, Department of Virginia/Eastern Shore) and the GHOTES-published "Eastern Shore of Virginia Register of Loyal Volunteers (Civil War Era)." A separate set, the **Amnesty Papers (NARA M1003)** filed under President Johnson's December 8, 1863 Amnesty Proclamation and 1865 implementing orders, covers former Confederates and a small set of pardoned residents 1865–1867.
- **Trade with the Confederacy was illegal.** Commerce across the Chesapeake to Confederate Virginia was contraband. Eastern Shore farms continued producing — sweet potatoes, oysters, corn, salt fish — but the legal market was Baltimore and Philadelphia, not Norfolk or Richmond. Smuggling was constant.
- **Naval blockade.** US Navy patrols (Potomac Flotilla, then North Atlantic Blockading Squadron) stopped vessels moving between Eastern Shore and the mainland.
- **Confiscation of "disloyal" property.** Property of men who slipped across to enlist CSA could be confiscated under the Confiscation Acts of 1861/1862. Records in Accomack and Northampton county order books, LVA.
- **Mary Graves / Locustville Academy episode.** The local landmark of civilian-impact stories: Locustville Academy (Accomack) was closed by Union troops after teacher Mary Graves refused the oath. This is the kind of incident that shaped daily life for these ancestors' children.

### 2.6 Slavery and emancipation — the Eastern Shore exception

This is where the Eastern Shore departed sharply from mainland Confederate Virginia.

**1860 enslaved population (corrected from `05-civil-war.md`):**

| County | Total population (1860) | Enslaved | Enslaved % | Free Black | Free Black % of Black population |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Accomack | 18,586 | 4,507 | **24.2%** | 4,380 | **43%** of African American total |
| Northampton | 7,832 | 3,872 | **49.4%** | 657 | ~14.5% of African American total |

Sources: virginiaplaces.org 1860 census table; African American Intellectual History Society 2019 article on Free Blacks in Antebellum Accomack. **Note: the Northampton enslaved figure of 3,872 corrects `05-civil-war.md`'s 3,798. Accomack's 4,507 is consistent.**

The single most distinctive Eastern Shore demographic fact is the **enormous free Black population in Accomack — 43% of the African American total, vs. ~12% statewide.** Accomack had the largest population of free African Americans in any Virginia county before Nat Turner's 1831 revolt. This means slaveholding on the Eastern Shore coexisted with a large free Black yeoman / artisan / waterman class, and the local enslaved-to-free ratio looked nothing like mainland Tidewater. **It does NOT mean the Eastern Shore was lightly slaveholding** — Northampton was nearly 50% enslaved, and Accomack's 24% is meaningful. The accurate frame: small holdings (1–5 enslaved per holder typical), embedded in everyday yeoman farming, alongside an unusually large free Black community.

**Lockwood's 1861 promise.** The November 1861 proclamation explicitly preserved slavery as it existed under Virginia law. In practice, enforcement collapsed quickly. Enslaved people on the Shore began self-emancipating from late 1861 onward.

**Emancipation Proclamation (January 1, 1863).** Did NOT apply to Eastern Shore Virginia — Lincoln explicitly exempted Accomack, Northampton, and Norfolk, the parts of Virginia under Union control as of that date. Slavery remained legally in force in Accomack and Northampton until **December 6, 1865** (13th Amendment ratification).

**De facto emancipation.** By late 1863 the institution had collapsed practically on most Eastern Shore farms, even while legally in force. Federal recruiters, Quaker activity, occupation reality made enforcement of slave law impossible.

**USCT enlistment from the Eastern Shore.** Eastern Shore VA Black men, both formerly enslaved and free, did enlist in USCT regiments. The most directly relevant unit was the **30th US Colored Infantry**, organized at Camp Stanton, Benedict, Maryland in February–March 1864, recruiting from "eastern shore and southern counties of Maryland" — but the Maryland Eastern Shore is geographically continuous with Virginia's, and Virginia Eastern Shore men routinely crossed the Pocomoke / state line to enlist. Other Virginia USCT regiments raised at Fort Monroe (2nd USCC, 2nd USCAH/Battery B, 10th, 23rd, 38th) drew Eastern Shore men. Total Black soldiers mustered in Virginia: at least 5,723. Eastern Shore VA's specific contribution is not disaggregated in the standard sources but is positively documented. **For Susan's slaveholding ancestors (if any are confirmed by Slave Schedule check), the question of whether their formerly-enslaved people enlisted in USCT and what their names are is part of the honest accounting.**

### 3.2 99th NY Infantry — `05-civil-war.md` correction

`05-civil-war.md` identified the **99th NY Infantry** (the "Union Coast Guard") as a Union regiment that "recruited locally" on the Eastern Shore of Virginia. **This is incorrect and should be corrected.** Per the New York State Military Museum and Veterans Research Center, the 99th NY was organized as a naval brigade on May 28, 1861, recruited by Col. William A. Bartlett in New York City; Companies G, H, I, K were formed principally of men from Massachusetts; Companies A, B, C from Red Banks NJ; Companies D and E also Massachusetts. **It was not recruited from Eastern Shore Virginia.** It was *stationed* on the Eastern Shore for blockade duty, but recruitment was in NY/NJ/MA. The actual Eastern Shore Union recruiting unit was the 1st Loyal East Virginia Volunteers (above).

## 4. The household experience for John K. Rodgers, William Samuel Kellam, and the Bull / Rogers children

### 4.1 What John K. Rodgers (b. 1823, age 38–42) actually did 1861–65

Without enlistment in either army or evidence to the contrary, the modal Eastern Shore farmer in his 30s–40s during the war:

- **November 1861**: Witnessed Lockwood's column passing through the county. Likely encountered Union pickets on roads.
- **Late 1861 – early 1862**: Took the oath of allegiance to the United States to retain property rights, sell crops, and transact at the courthouse. Oath records likely in NARA RG 393 or scattered Provost Marshal series.
- **1862–63**: Continued farming. Trade reoriented from Norfolk / Richmond to Baltimore / Philadelphia under Union sanction. Sweet potatoes, oysters, corn, salt fish remained the cash crops.
- **1863**: Watched the Emancipation Proclamation arrive — and pointedly *not* apply to Accomack and Northampton. The institution of slavery remained legal locally even as it collapsed practically.
- **1864**: Continued occupation. Some Eastern Shore men signed Amnesty Papers under the December 1863 Proclamation, looking ahead to post-war property protection.
- **1865**: Witnessed the legal end of slavery via the 13th Amendment, December 6, 1865.

If John K. Rodgers signed an oath of allegiance, the document is potentially recoverable from NARA RG 393 (Department of Virginia, Eastern Shore Provost Marshal records) or from county order books that recorded oath compliance in property transactions. **This is a high-value next-step research target.**

### 4.2 What William Samuel Kellam (b. 1817, age 44–48) actually did 1861–65

Same modal pattern, age-shifted senior. As a 44-year-old man with established farm holdings in 1861, he was at the upper edge of pre-1864 conscription age and well above typical voluntary enlistment age. The probability of CSA enlistment is low even setting aside occupation; the probability of Union enlistment is essentially zero. **He almost certainly lived the war as a civilian under federal occupation, signing the oath of allegiance to retain property.** If William Samuel Kellam owned enslaved people in 1860, the war ended that — practically by 1863, legally by December 1865. The 1860 Slave Schedule check on his name is the resolution.

### 4.3 What the Bull / Rogers children experienced 1861–65

**Joseph Saples Bull (b. 1850, age 11–15), William Edward Bull (b. 1854, age 7–11), William Frances Rogers (b. 1847, age 14–18)** lived the war as boys on Eastern Shore farms under federal occupation. The formative experiences were:

- **Federal soldiers visible.** Lockwood's column itself, then garrison troops at Drummondtown, Eastville, Cobbs Island, and along the coast.
- **Naval blockade visible.** Union gunboats stopping vessels in the Pocomoke and Chesapeake.
- **The collapse of slavery.** These children grew up watching enslaved people on neighboring farms (and possibly their own, pending Slave Schedule check) self-emancipate, leave, or enlist in USCT. The institution they were born into ended in their boyhoods.
- **Trade reorientation.** Family income shifted from a Norfolk/Richmond axis to Baltimore/Philadelphia.
- **The 1864 oath wave.** As the war wound down, oath-of-allegiance compliance and Amnesty applications became routine adult business in their households.

For William Frances Rogers turning 17 in 1864, CSA conscription was theoretically expanded to his age — but in occupied territory it was inapplicable. He was a 17-year-old farm boy in occupied Pennyville, not a conscription target.

The lasting effect on this generation: by the time Joseph Saples Bull (1850–1923) was an adult, he was farming an Eastern Shore that had been integrated into the Baltimore-Philadelphia economy for two decades and had dispensed with slavery entirely. The Bull farm, Pennyville Rogers homestead, and Craddockville Bull holdings of the post-war generation operated in a Northern-economic frame, not a Confederate-Tidewater one.

2. **The "99th NY Infantry recruited from the Eastern Shore" claim is incorrect.** The 99th NY was organized in May 1861 by Col. William A. Bartlett in New York City, with companies drawing from NY, NJ, and MA. It was *stationed* on the Eastern Shore for blockade duty but did not recruit there. The actual Eastern Shore Union recruiting unit was the **1st Regiment, Loyal Eastern Virginia Volunteers** (Co. A, mustered at Cobbs Island VA, June 30, 1864). Surname check on this roster: NEGATIVE for Bull, Kellam, Rogers, Boggs, Floyd, Garrison.
3. **Northampton 1860 enslaved population: 3,872, not 3,798.** Minor correction.
