## Headline finding

**The Eastern Shore of Virginia spent the Civil War as a Confederate-on-paper, Union-occupied island — and that single fact is the dominant family-history story for any Donahoe / Dean / adjacent ancestor in Accomack 1861–1865.** Within seven months of secession, Brigadier General Henry H. Lockwood marched a 4,500-man Union column down the peninsula in November 1861, took both counties without a battle, and held them for the duration. From that point forward the Eastern Shore lived under federal occupation, oath-of-allegiance administration, naval blockade against the Confederacy, and incremental emancipation. The genealogical consequence is concrete: a typical Eastern Shore man of military age in 1861 either (a) stayed home and signed a loyalty oath to keep his property, (b) slipped across the Chesapeake to enlist in a mainland Confederate unit at real personal risk, or (c) eventually enlisted in a Union or Maryland unit, including in some cases a US Colored Troops regiment if Black or mixed-race. **The default expectation that a deep-Virginia ancestor "served the Confederacy" simply does not hold here, and the absence of a CSA service record is not evidence of disloyalty or absence — it is the modal Eastern Shore experience.** For a Famine-era Irish immigrant Donahoe arriving 1845–1855 as a young adult, military age in 1861 is mathematically possible; the live questions are (1) which side, if either, and (2) whether he was even on the Shore yet. The slaveholding question is genuinely live for *non-Donahoe* surnames in Mary Ellen's maternal lines — older established Eastern Shore families absolutely did own enslaved people, and the 1860 Slave Schedule for Accomack will resolve it for any specific ancestor name we surface.

## 1. Eastern Shore Civil War context — the unique sub-history

### 1.1 Secession on paper, occupation in fact

Virginia's Ordinance of Secession passed the convention on April 17, 1861 and was ratified by statewide referendum on May 23, 1861. Accomack and Northampton counties voted with Virginia. On paper, the two Eastern Shore counties were Confederate territory from late May 1861 forward — but the geography made that paper status untenable. The Eastern Shore is a 70-mile peninsula bordered by the Chesapeake Bay on the west and the Atlantic on the east, connected to the rest of Virginia only by water. Norfolk, Hampton Roads, and Fort Monroe (still in Union hands throughout the war) sat directly across the Chesapeake. The peninsula's only land connection ran north into Union-loyal Maryland. The region was geographically a Union-controlled island from the moment Virginia seceded.

Local Confederate organizing did happen. The 39th Virginia Infantry was raised on the Shore in summer 1861, primarily in Accomack and Northampton, under Col. Charles Smith. Several independent companies drilled at Drummondtown (now Accomac Court House) and Eastville. But the unit was poorly equipped, lightly trained, and isolated from Confederate logistics. By October 1861, with Union forces concentrating at the head of the peninsula, the 39th VA effectively dissolved — many men returned home, some crossed to the mainland to enlist in other VA units, a smaller number stayed under arms.

### 1.2 The Lockwood expedition — November 1861

On November 14, 1861, Brigadier General Henry H. Lockwood (a Delaware native, professor at the US Naval Academy before the war) marched south from Snow Hill, Maryland with approximately 4,500 troops drawn primarily from the 1st Delaware Infantry, 5th New York, several Maryland regiments, and Purnell Legion (Maryland). He issued a proclamation on the march guaranteeing the property and rights of any Eastern Shore resident who took an oath of allegiance to the United States and laid down arms. The proclamation explicitly acknowledged slavery as it existed under state law and promised non-interference at the local level — a critical political concession given that Maryland and Delaware were slaveholding Union states.

The remnant 39th Virginia Infantry under Col. Smith did not give battle. The unit dispersed; Smith himself eventually crossed to mainland Virginia. Lockwood's column reached Accomac Court House on November 17 and Eastville on November 18, 1861, occupying both county seats without combat. By late November the Union held the entire peninsula.

**Source:** Official Records of the War of the Rebellion, Series I, Volume 5 (Lockwood's after-action report); Encyclopedia Virginia entry "Eastern Shore during the Civil War"; Kirk Mariner, *Off 13: The Eastern Shore of Virginia Guidebook* (1987) and *Once Upon an Island* (1996); Brooks Miles Barnes, "The Onancock Race Riot of 1907" *Virginia Magazine of History and Biography* (1984) — provides the continuous Eastern Shore political backdrop into the post-war and Jim Crow eras.

### 1.3 Daily life under Union occupation 1861–1865

Practical effects on the civilian population were sharp:

- **Oath of allegiance system.** To retain ordinary civil rights — sell crops, register a deed, transact at the courthouse, hold property, draw a salary, ship goods to Baltimore or Norfolk — Eastern Shore residents took an oath of allegiance to the United States. The oath records survive in scattered form; **the National Archives holds a series of "Amnesty Oaths" filed under the President's Amnesty Proclamation of December 8, 1863, indexed by state and county** (NARA RG 94, "Case Files of Applications from Former Confederates for Presidential Pardons" — known as the Amnesty Papers, M1003). For Accomack and Northampton, oaths were administered by Provost Marshals at Drummondtown and Eastville from late 1861 forward, distinct from the 1863+ amnesty papers.
- **Trade with the Confederacy was illegal.** Any commerce across the Chesapeake to Confederate Virginia was contraband. Eastern Shore farms continued producing — sweet potatoes, oysters, corn, salt fish — but the legal market was Baltimore and Philadelphia, not Norfolk or Richmond. Smuggling across the Bay was constant and prosecuted intermittently.
- **Naval blockade.** US Navy patrols (the Potomac Flotilla and later the North Atlantic Blockading Squadron) stopped vessels moving between the Eastern Shore and the mainland. Records of seized vessels and their owners are at NARA RG 21 (US District Court records, Eastern District of Virginia and District of Maryland prize cases).
- **Confiscation of "disloyal" property.** Property of men who slipped across to enlist in CSA units could be confiscated under the Confiscation Acts of 1861 and 1862. Records are in Accomack and Northampton county order books for the Civil War years and at LVA.
- **Provost marshal jurisdiction.** Civil disputes involving military matters, contraband, and oath violations went through Union Provost Marshal records (NARA RG 393, Records of US Army Continental Commands, Department of Virginia/Eastern Shore).

### 1.4 Slavery and emancipation on the Eastern Shore

This is where the Eastern Shore's experience departed sharply from mainland Confederate Virginia. Emancipation came earlier and via a different mechanism:

- **1860 enslaved population.** Per the 1860 US Census, Accomack County had 4,507 enslaved people (about 23% of total population of 18,586) and 1,196 "free colored" persons. Northampton had 3,798 enslaved (about 39% of 7,832) and 657 free colored. Both counties had unusually large free-Black populations relative to mainland Tidewater. **Accomack's enslaved share (~23%) was meaningfully lower than mainland Tidewater counties (~40–55%), but slavery was real, normal, and embedded in the Eastern Shore economy.** The myth that "the Eastern Shore wasn't really slaveholding" is false. The accurate statement is that Eastern Shore slaveholding was lower in concentration and skewed to smaller holdings — many slaveholders held 1–5 enslaved people rather than large plantations.
- **Lockwood's 1861 promise.** The November 1861 proclamation explicitly preserved slavery as it existed under Virginia law. This was a political necessity; in practice, enforcement collapsed quickly. Enslaved people on the Shore began self-emancipating from late 1861 onward, often by reaching Union lines or simply walking away. Some Eastern Shore enslaved men eventually enlisted in US Colored Troops regiments (most commonly the 9th, 10th, 19th, 22nd, 25th, 36th, 38th, and 39th USCT, raised in Maryland and Virginia 1863–1864).
- **Emancipation Proclamation (January 1, 1863).** Did NOT apply to Eastern Shore Virginia. Lincoln explicitly exempted the parts of Virginia under Union control as of that date — Accomack, Northampton, and the area around Norfolk. Slavery remained legal in Accomack and Northampton until December 6, 1865 (13th Amendment ratification).
- **De facto emancipation.** By late 1863 the institution had collapsed practically on most Eastern Shore farms even though it remained legal. Federal recruiters, Quaker activity from Pennsylvania, and the simple reality of Union occupation made enforcement of slave law impossible. Many enslaved people simply left. Some returned as wage laborers; many migrated to Baltimore, Philadelphia, or contraband camps near Fortress Monroe.

**Sources:** 1860 US Census, Slave Schedules, Accomack and Northampton VA, available free on FamilySearch (`https://www.familysearch.org/search/collection/1420440`); Encyclopedia Virginia "Slavery in Virginia"; Helen Rountree and Thomas Davidson, *Eastern Shore Indians of Virginia and Maryland* (UNC Press, 1996) — covers free-colored and Indian populations; Kirk Mariner, *Slave and Free on Virginia's Eastern Shore* (2014).

## 2. Donahoe + Dean + adjacent surname Civil War search

The canonical free database for Civil War service is the **National Park Service Civil War Soldiers and Sailors System (CWSS)** at `https://www.nps.gov/civilwar/soldiers-and-sailors-database.htm`. CWSS indexes the General Index Cards held by NARA — every Union and Confederate soldier whose service was officially recorded. It is comprehensive for Confederate enlistees and substantially complete for Union enlistees. **Note: as a research agent I cannot execute live web queries against CWSS in this run; the search protocol below is what Brady or Genie should run, with the structural expectations and likely matches drawn from the published literature and standard Eastern Shore unit rosters.**

### 2.1 Search protocol — Donahoe / Donohoe / O'Donohoe

Run all surname spelling variants on CWSS, both Union and Confederate, all states. Spelling was inconsistent in 19th-century muster rolls; the same man may appear as **Donahoe, Donahue, Donohoe, Donohue, O'Donohoe, O'Donahue, Donaho, Donaha, Donoho** on different documents.

Expected pattern based on CWSS structure:

- **Confederate Donahoes from VA** — likely small in number; the CSA had relatively few soldiers carrying any spelling of the surname from VA units, because the surname is heavily Famine-Irish-immigrant and most VA Famine immigrants concentrated in Norfolk, Richmond, and the Shenandoah corridor, not Accomack. Eastern Shore Confederate Donahoes would likely appear in 39th VA Infantry rosters before its dissolution, or in the 9th VA Infantry (Norfolk-Portsmouth area) or 41st VA Infantry (Tidewater) for men who slipped across.
- **Union Donahoes** — a much larger pool. The Famine generation was heavily Northern-resident, Catholic, and Democratic but Union-enlisted in large numbers, especially in NY, PA, MA, and Ohio. The Maryland 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th Infantry; the 1st and 2nd Delaware; and the various Pennsylvania regiments are the units most relevant to a Donahoe who lived on the Eastern Shore by 1861 and chose Union service via Maryland or Delaware mustering points.
- **Donahoe in US Colored Troops** — the surname does appear in USCT rosters, almost always when a freedman took an Irish surname from a former owner or employer or when an immigrant of color was misclassified. Worth checking but lower probability.

### 2.2 Search protocol — Dean

The Dean surname is an old English/Scots-Irish surname with much deeper Eastern Shore roots than Donahoe. Dean appears in Accomack records back to the 17th century. Dean men in 1861 fall into both Confederate and Union pools, and the surname is common enough that disambiguation requires county-and-age cross-checks.

Run on CWSS:

- **Confederate Dean from VA** — multiple matches expected. The 39th VA Infantry roster includes Dean entries (per the published roster in *39th Virginia Infantry* by Robert Driver, H.E. Howard Inc., 1991). Any Dean in 39th VA whose Accomack residence matches the 1860 Accomack census is a likely line candidate.
- **Confederate Dean from other VA units** — 9th VA Infantry, 41st VA Infantry, 6th VA Cavalry, 39th VA Cavalry Battalion (note: a separate unit from the 39th VA Infantry; this was a partisan ranger unit operating partly on the Eastern Shore later in the war, also called Richardson's Battalion).
- **Union Dean** — Maryland regiments (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th, 8th, 9th MD Infantry; 1st, 2nd MD Cavalry; Purnell Legion); 1st and 2nd Delaware; 99th NY Infantry (recruited partly on the Eastern Shore of Virginia after Lockwood's occupation, an unusual case of a "Virginia" unit that fought for the Union and recruited from occupied Accomack and Northampton).
- **The 99th NY Infantry deserves specific attention.** This regiment was originally raised as the "Union Coast Guard" and stationed on the Eastern Shore of Virginia from 1861 forward. It recruited locally — meaning Eastern Shore Virginia men, including some from Accomack and Northampton, served in a New York-numbered Union regiment from their home counties. Roster published by the New York State Adjutant General; available on Ancestry and free at the NY State Military Museum site (`dmna.ny.gov/historic/reghist/civil/`). Any Dean or related surname appearing in 99th NY rosters with Accomack/Northampton residence is a high-confidence Union service match.

### 2.3 Search protocol — adjacent surnames in Mary Ellen's likely line

Surnames to run on CWSS once census work in `09-census-extracts.md` surfaces them as ancestral. Common Eastern Shore surnames that intermarried widely with both Donahoe and Dean lines include: **Wessells, Mears, Bull, Bayly, Bayne, Custis, Drummond, Edmonds, Fletcher, Hopkins, Lewis, Mason, Mathews, Nelson, Parker, Powell, Read, Savage, Scarburgh, Smith, Stockley, Sturgis, Taylor, Walker, West, Wharton, White, Wise.**

Of particular interest given the "Bull farm" lore (thread 8 — owned by `04-onancock-and-bull-farm.md`): the **Bull surname** is well-attested in Accomack from the 17th century. Bull men in CSA service (likely in 39th VA Infantry or 6th VA Cavalry) and in 99th NY Union service would be ancestral candidates regardless of which side. The Bull farm's 1950s loss does not foreclose the prior generations being slaveholders, soldiers, or both.

### 2.4 The immigrant-Donahoe military age question

If the Famine-era Donahoe immigrant (working hypothesis: arrived 1845–1855) was 18–25 at arrival, he was 24–41 in 1861 — squarely military age. The questions are:

1. **Was he in Accomack by 1861, or still in a Northeastern port-of-entry?** A Donahoe still living in Boston, NY, or Philadelphia in 1861 likely enlisted in a Union regiment from that city. A Donahoe already on the Eastern Shore likely faced the Lockwood-era choice (oath, slip across, or enlist Union via 99th NY or Maryland units).
2. **Did he serve at all?** Famine immigrants enlisted in Union service at high rates because of bounties, wages, and Democratic Party patronage networks. CSA enlistment by Famine Irish was much rarer and concentrated in Louisiana, Norfolk, Richmond, and Charleston — not Eastern Shore VA.
3. **Did he take the oath?** If he stayed on the Shore and didn't enlist, his oath of allegiance is the relevant record. NARA M1003 (Amnesty Papers) covers former Confederates and a small set of pardoned residents 1865–1867; ordinary occupation-period oaths 1861–1865 are scattered in Provost Marshal records (NARA RG 393).

### 2.5 What CWSS will return — a structural prediction

Without live database access, a confident prediction based on the published literature: a CWSS search on Donahoe variants restricted to Virginia units will return very few matches (likely 0–3 across all spellings). A search on Dean restricted to Virginia units will return dozens of matches (the surname is very common); narrowing by 39th VA Infantry, 9th VA Infantry, 41st VA Infantry, and 6th VA Cavalry will yield a manageable shortlist that can be cross-referenced against the 1860 Accomack census to find the actual Mary-Ellen ancestral line. **The actionable next step is to run these CWSS searches live and capture the General Index Card image for any candidate match — the card gives unit, company, rank, dates of service, and notes (deserted, captured, killed, mustered out).**

## 3. Slaveholding question — honest treatment

### 3.1 The structural answer

Did any Donahoe / Dean / related ancestor own enslaved people in 1860? The answer breaks cleanly along surname-age lines:

- **Donahoe surname — almost certainly NO.** A Famine-era Irish immigrant arriving 1845–1855 had no capital. Famine emigration was distress emigration; immigrants arrived with passage money exhausted, often in debt to a sponsor, and worked for wages for years before accumulating any property. The probability of an 1845–1855 Donahoe immigrant having purchased enslaved people by 1860 is essentially zero. The 1860 Slave Schedule for Accomack will confirm this — a Donahoe slaveholder would be a thesis-overturning find, but the record will almost certainly show no such entry.
- **Dean surname — UNKNOWN, must check.** Dean is an old Eastern Shore surname dating to the 17th century. Some Dean families were yeoman farmers; some were larger landowners; some were tenants and laborers. The 1860 Slave Schedule for Accomack must be checked line by line for every Dean entry, especially heads of household identifiable as direct ancestors of Mary Ellen Donahoe's husband (Brady's maternal grandfather). **The honest expectation: at least one Dean ancestor in Mary Ellen's husband's line owned enslaved people in 1860, given the surname's depth on the Shore and the prevalence of small slaveholding among Eastern Shore yeoman families. This needs to be named in the whitepaper, not soft-pedaled.**
- **Other ancestral surnames — UNKNOWN, must check.** Bull, Custis, Drummond, Mason, Savage, Scarburgh, Wise, and similar deep-Shore surnames have a documented history of slaveholding in Accomack. If any of these appear in Mary Ellen's maternal line (her mother's family, grandmother's family, etc.) the probability of slaveholding rises sharply. The Custis family in particular (the same family Martha Washington came from) was a major Eastern Shore slaveholding lineage; any Custis branch in Mary Ellen's tree means slaveholding ancestors are a near-certainty.

### 3.2 The records that resolve it

- **1860 US Census Slave Schedule, Accomack County VA.** Free at FamilySearch (`https://www.familysearch.org/search/collection/1420440` — "United States Census (Slave Schedule), 1860"). Lists slaveholders by name with the count, age, sex, and color of each enslaved person held. Does not name the enslaved person. The 1850 schedule is the equivalent for the prior decade.
- **Accomack County Will Books and Order Books, 1700–1865.** Wills routinely bequeath enslaved people by name — these are the primary records that document slaveholding by the deeper colonial ancestors and document the enslaved people themselves, who deserve naming and tracing. Indexed at LVA and partly digitized at FamilySearch.
- **Accomack County Deed Books.** Bills of sale for enslaved people were recorded as deeds. Same indexing.
- **Federal Direct Tax of 1815, Accomack County.** Lists enslaved people held as taxable property.
- **The Civil War-era Accomack tax rolls.** Continued to assess enslaved people as taxable property until emancipation. LVA holds.

### 3.3 The honest framing for the whitepaper

Brady's family-circulation whitepaper should not soft-pedal this. The accurate statement, once the 1860 Slave Schedule is checked, will be one of three:

1. **"No ancestral slaveholders found in the surnames we've traced."** Possible but unlikely given Eastern Shore demographics and Dean surname depth.
2. **"Yes, [specific ancestor name] held [N] enslaved people in 1860, with the following bequests in his/her will naming individuals."** Most likely outcome for at least one ancestor.
3. **"Yes, slaveholding appears in the deep colonial line via [surname], specifically in [specific ancestor] who held [N] enslaved people in [year]."** Likely outcome for any 18th-century ancestor of significant property.

The whitepaper should name the enslaved people where the records preserve their names. This is part of the honest accounting and is the right thing to do for descendants of the enslaved who may be researching the same families. The Eastern Shore has active African American genealogical communities and projects (notably the Eastern Shore Public Library's African American genealogy collection and the work of historian Kellee Blake) who maintain records cross-referencing enslaved people to slaveholding families.

## 4. Pension applications

### 4.1 Union pensions (NARA)

Federal pensions for Union veterans and their widows are held by NARA in Washington, DC under RG 15 (Records of the Department of Veterans Affairs). The pension index (T288, 1861–1934) is digitized and free on FamilySearch (`https://www.familysearch.org/search/collection/1919699`). For any Donahoe or Dean appearing on CWSS as a Union enlistee, the pension index will show whether a pension claim was filed — by the veteran himself, by his widow, by his minor children, or by a dependent mother. The index gives application number, certificate number, state, and unit. The full pension file (often 50–200 pages) is at NARA and orderable; recently NARA has digitized many full files via Fold3 (subscription, but free at most public libraries and at the National Archives reading rooms).

**Pension files are the single richest Civil War genealogy record** — they typically include sworn affidavits about marriage, family composition, residence history, medical history, and the soldier's service in detail. A Union pension file for a Donahoe ancestor would resolve immigrant origin, arrival date, marriage records, and family structure simultaneously.

### 4.2 Confederate pensions (LVA)

Virginia did not pay Confederate pensions until 1888 (initial Act of Assembly), with substantially expanded coverage in 1900 and 1902. A Confederate veteran or widow had to be a Virginia resident, indigent, and physically eligible. **The Library of Virginia holds the complete set of Virginia Confederate Pension Applications, 1888–1934, fully indexed and partly digitized (`https://www.lva.virginia.gov/public/guides/rn30_confederatepensions.htm`).** The application files include service affidavits (often from comrades who served with the applicant), medical evidence, and family information for widow's pensions.

For an Eastern Shore Confederate ancestor — likely a man who slipped across to enlist in 39th VA, 9th VA, 41st VA, or 6th VA Cavalry — an 1888+ pension application is a high-probability find if he survived. The 1888 cutoff means he had to live to that year or his widow had to be alive to claim. Many Eastern Shore Confederate veterans died before 1888 of war injuries, postwar illness, or yellow fever epidemics; many widows did file.

### 4.3 What the pension search resolves

If a Donahoe pension file (Union, Confederate, or USCT) is found, it likely resolves: immigrant origin (Ireland — county and parish), arrival date and port, occupation history, marriage(s), children, residence sequence, and physical description. This is the single best-yielding record for any Civil War-era Donahoe ancestor.

If a Dean pension file is found, it likely resolves: deeper Eastern Shore residence history, family structure, kinship to other Eastern Shore Deans, and (for Confederate pensions) which mainland VA unit the man slipped across to.
