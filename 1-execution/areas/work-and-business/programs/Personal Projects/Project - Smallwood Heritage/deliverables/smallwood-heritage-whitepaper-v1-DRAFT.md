# The Augusta Smallwoods

*Brady's paternal grandfather Nelson Roudnal Smallwood, his ancestors back to the Northern Neck of Virginia, and what the record actually says about how this family arrived in the Shenandoah Valley.*

**Compiled May 2026 by Brady Smallwood with the Genie research engine. Draft — for family review before final rendering.**

---

## Why this paper exists

Nelson Roudnal Smallwood died on December 22, 1955 at the Hunter Holmes McGuire Veterans Administration Medical Center in Richmond, Virginia. He was thirty years old. His son — Brady's father, Dennis Eugene "Gene" Smallwood — was eight.

When a grandfather dies young, the line above him goes dark in the family's living memory. Lutie Fannie Clark, Nelson's wife, lived until 2001 and carried what she could about her husband's family forward to her son. But she was a Clark from Rockbridge County, married into the Smallwood family — she was not the steward of the Smallwood patriline's deep memory. That memory was lost when Nelson and his brothers' generation passed.

This paper rebuilds it from the documentary record. No folklore is assumed. Every claim cites a primary source where one exists, names the compiled tree where it doesn't, and flags what is still unproven.

---

## The headline finding

**Brady's Smallwoods came into the Shenandoah Valley from the Northern Neck of Virginia, not from Loudoun County.** The patriline traces backward through Augusta County → Rockingham County → a brief detour through Preston County (then Virginia, now West Virginia) → Spotsylvania County → King George and Stafford counties on the south bank of the Potomac, directly across the river from Charles County, Maryland.

This matters because it changes the question. The most complete published genealogy of the American Smallwood family — Jean (Carter) Smallwood's *Smallwood & Carter Connections to Family Histories and Royalty* (Heritage Books, 2002) — documents the **Loudoun County branch** descending from Col. James Smallwood (1639) of Charles County, Maryland. That branch went west out of Maryland into Northern Virginia in the late 1700s, eventually producing Elmer Eugene Smallwood (1928–) of Washington DC, the husband of the book's author.

Brady's branch is different. It went **south** across the Potomac into Stafford and King George counties in the mid-1700s, then west into the Valley one generation later. Brady's family is not a different American Smallwood line from Jean's family — both branches almost certainly descend from the same Col. James Smallwood (1639) — but they diverged in colonial Maryland and did not reconverge.

**This is why nothing in the Loudoun-branch part of Jean's book — including its claims of descent from Charlemagne, Robert the Bruce, and Antenor — applies to Brady.** Those royalty descents come through specific marriages on the Loudoun branch (the Marbury line via Matthew Smallwood 1710, the Beckwith-Rhine line on Elmer Eugene's mother's side). They are real claims for Jean's husband's line. They do not reach Brady's branch even if the Northern Neck and Loudoun branches both descend from the same colonial Maryland founder.

## What is documented end-to-end

The documented Smallwood patriline, ten generations from Brady backward:

1. Brady Smallwood (b. 1976)
2. Dennis Eugene "Gene" Smallwood (b. ~1947, Augusta VA)
3. **Nelson Roudnal Smallwood** (b. 26 Mar 1925 Weyers Cave, Augusta VA — d. 22 Dec 1955 at the Hunter Holmes McGuire VA Hospital, Richmond VA — buried 27 Dec 1955 at Riverview Cemetery, Waynesboro VA, Block 201 Lot 4A & 1/2B)
4. **Charles Richard Smallwood** (b. 28 Nov 1902 Rockingham VA — d. 1 Mar 1977 Staunton VA — buried Middle River Church of the Brethren Cemetery, New Hope VA), married 11 Dec 1922 in Hagerstown Maryland to **Ethel May Knicely** (b. 9 Apr 1904 Mount Crawford Rockingham VA — d. 5 Dec 1949 Harrisonburg VA — buried Middle River Brethren). After Ethel's death Charles Richard remarried Ellen Bunch Pittman on 11 Mar 1955.
5. **Hamilton Osborn Smallwood Sr** (b. 23 Jul 1864 Sangerville Augusta VA — d. 8 Nov 1938 Mount Sidney Augusta VA — buried Weyers Cave VA), married **Ida Florence Johnson** (b. 11 Nov 1869 Mayes Furnace Rockingham VA — d. 18 Feb 1948 Mount Sidney — buried Pleasant Valley Brethren Cemetery, Weyers Cave). Hamilton was blind for the last decade of his life.
6. **Henry James Smallwood** (b. ~1831 Rockingham VA — d. 14 Jan 1904 Rockingham VA), married Annie Elizabeth Hess (b. Feb 1840 Rockingham VA — d. 20 May 1920 Augusta VA).
7. **James Smallwood** (b. 1792 Spotsylvania VA — d. ~1865 Rockingham VA), married Elizabeth Jackson in 1816 Spotsylvania (likely first wife) and Elizabeth Gear (likely second wife and mother of Henry James — to be resolved by the 1850 census image).
8. **James Smallwood** (b. ~1755 Stafford VA — d. 15 Jan 1792 King George VA), married 23 May 1776 in Stafford VA to **Catherine Clark** (b. 1756 Stafford VA — d. 1820 Spotsylvania VA, daughter of Charles Clark and Phebe Rebecca Goldsmith Clark of Caroline County VA).

That is the verified end-to-end chain.

## What is plausible but unproven (the Maryland bridge)

Above James Smallwood (1755), compiled trees on WikiTree and FamilySearch extend the line:

9. **William H. Smallwood** (b. ~1720 Charles County MD)
10. **Pryor Smallwood** (b. ~1680 Charles County MD)
11. **Col. James Smallwood** (b. ~1639 Cheshire, England — arrived Charles County MD 1664 — d. ~1715 Prince George's County MD)

The top of this chain is well-sourced — Col. James Smallwood and Pryor Smallwood are extensively documented in Jean (Carter) Smallwood's 2002 book and in Arthur L. Keith's 1926 *Smallwood, Charles Co., MD* (Maryland Historical Magazine). The bottom — the link between William H. Smallwood (1720) and James Smallwood (1755 Stafford) — rests on compiled trees, not on a primary record.

The **Charles County MD → Northern Neck VA migration corridor of the 1740s–1770s** is historically real. Many Charles County families crossed the Potomac into Stafford, King George, Westmoreland, and Northumberland counties as Maryland tobacco land thinned. The geography supports the bridge. But geography is not proof. The bridge generation needs primary-source documentation to lock the connection.

**Two paths to lock it:**

1. **Y-DNA.** The FTDNA Smallwood Surname Project has identified Y-haplogroup R-FTC1754 as the Col. James Smallwood (1639) signature (Kit 75030, a documented direct descendant). A $119 Y-37 test plus an $18 YSEQ R-FTC1754 single-SNP test for Brady would prove or disprove the connection definitively in 6–8 weeks. As of May 2026, no Stafford / King George / Spotsylvania VA tester is in the project — Brady's test would be the first data point on that branch.

2. **Documentary.** Spotsylvania Will Book E (pages 49 and 1274), Spotsylvania Deed Books L, N, P, and R, the King George Will Book 2 (1782–1813), and the 1796 and 1806 Spotsylvania Order Books (which document the apprenticeship of James and Catherine's son Charles Smallwood and the guardianship of their younger children James Jr. and Katherine after Catherine's death). These records all survive and can be pulled at the Library of Virginia in Richmond or at the King George County Clerk's Office.

## The Smallwood-Clark alliance in colonial Spotsylvania

One Phase 3 finding is worth pulling out of the chain and examining directly. James Smallwood (1755) and his wife Catherine Clark were not just married — their families were tied together as a documented land-and-legal compact.

**The land James Smallwood owned at his death in King George VA in January 1792 — approximately 332 acres — had been purchased from Charles Clark, his future father-in-law, in Spotsylvania.** Charles Clark also witnessed James Smallwood's will. So did Phebe Clark (likely Catherine's mother, Phebe Rebecca Goldsmith Clark) and Thomas Clark (likely her brother). The executors of the will were Charles Clark, Thomas Clark, and the widow Catherine herself.

This is not the pattern of a chance marriage between two Stafford-area families. This is the pattern of a deliberate Smallwood-Clark alliance — the kind of intermarriage that consolidates land and labor across two yeoman planter households in the Northern Neck.

(The Charles Clark + Phebe Rebecca Goldsmith Clark line of Caroline County VA is **not** the Carter-of-Corotoman gentry network. Earlier in this research it was hypothesized that Catherine Clark might trace to that FFV Carter network, which would have given Brady a sideways tie into the same Carter family that Jean Carter Smallwood traces in the 2002 book. That hypothesis is refuted. Brady's Carter connection — if any exists — does not run through Catherine Clark.)

## The slaveholding question

James Smallwood died at his Northern Neck Virginia plantation on January 15, 1792. His will is recorded in King George County Will Book 2 (1782–1813) and has been preserved in two independent published abstracts (Crozier 1905) plus a photographed copy of the Spotsylvania Will Book E entry (page 1274). The will names his wife Catherine, his sons Charles and William, and includes a residuary clause leaving the rest of his property "to be equally divided among my children."

The will does not list enslaved people by name — that information would be in the **inventory book**, a separate volume not yet pulled. Given the Tidewater plantation geography of Stafford and King George counties in the 1780s and 1790s — and given the Smallwood-Clark land-and-labor alliance with the Charles Clark plantation — **James Smallwood was very likely a slaveholder.** The inventory book entry, when pulled, will document the names, ages, and valuations of any enslaved people he held.

This is not a soft-pedaled aside. If Brady's patrilineal Smallwood ancestor in the eighth generation back held enslaved people, that fact belongs in this paper, not in a footnote. The full inventory pull is on the action surface for Brady's planned Library of Virginia trip.

The same caveat applies one generation up. If James Smallwood (1755) descended from William H. Smallwood (b. 1720 Charles County MD) — which the compiled trees claim — then the Maryland Smallwood plantations were also slaveholding operations. Col. James Smallwood (1639) was a tobacco planter in Charles County who held enslaved people across multiple grants (Goats Lodge, Eltham, Porke Hall, Bachelor's Hope). His descendants continued the practice for six generations. When Maryland emancipated in November 1864, formerly enslaved Smallwood-plantation people took the surname into freedom — which is why the Black Smallwood population in Maryland and the Mid-Atlantic today vastly outnumbers the white descendants of Col. James.

## How the Brethren came in (and how they were already there)

Brady's Smallwood family attended three Church of the Brethren congregations in Augusta and Rockingham counties: Pleasant Valley (Weyers Cave), Middle River (New Hope), and Summit (Centerville). All three are still active under the Shenandoah District of the Church of the Brethren.

The earlier framing of this research assumed the Brethren community entered the Smallwood family through Charles Richard's 1922 Hagerstown marriage to Ethel May Knicely (a documented Brethren from a deeply German Brethren line). That framing is wrong by one generation.

**Hamilton Osborn Smallwood Sr — Charles Richard's father — was himself a Brethren convert.** His 1938 obituary records that he "joined the Church of the Brethren in early manhood," meaning sometime in the 1880s. Hamilton and his wife Ida Florence Johnson — who was raised Brethren and was buried at Pleasant Valley Brethren — transferred their membership from Middle River congregation to Pleasant Valley in 1923 when they moved from New Hope to Weyers Cave.

So the Smallwood patriline had been a Brethren family for at least four decades before Charles Richard married Ethel May Knicely. The 1922 Hagerstown marriage was a community-internal Brethren marriage — not a confessional bridge between Methodist Smallwoods and Brethren Knicelys. (Hagerstown MD was a common Valley-Brethren marriage destination because Maryland had laxer marriage waiting periods than Virginia.)

How did Hamilton come to the Brethren in the 1880s? The most plausible answer surfaces from the migration pattern: Hamilton's father Henry James Smallwood (b. ~1831 Rockingham VA) and his grandfather James Smallwood (b. 1792 Spotsylvania VA) had spent at least one generation in Preston County, in what was then northwestern Virginia and after 1863 became West Virginia. Three of James Smallwood's four children were born in Preston County. **Preston County in the early 1800s was heavily German Brethren** — and it is plausible that the Smallwood patriline first joined the Brethren community there, before Henry James returned to Rockingham VA. This is unproven and worth a Phase 4 archive pull at the Mack Memorial Library, Bridgewater College.

For the maternal side of Charles Richard's line — the Knicelys, Johnsons, Hesses, Wisemans — Brethren community membership is solidly documented. The Knicely surname itself is German (Kneisel / Kneisly / Kneisle), traceable to Palatinate German immigration to Pennsylvania in the early-to-mid 1700s, then southward into the Shenandoah Valley with the broader German Brethren / Mennonite migration of the 1740s–1770s. This stream is one of the three founding cultural streams of the Valley (alongside Scots-Irish and English-Tidewater).

## The Civil War in this family

Henry James Smallwood was 29 years old at the start of the Civil War in 1861 and 33 at its end — prime conscription age for the entire conflict. The default expectation for a white male of military age in Confederate-occupied Rockingham County is service in the Confederate Army. Whether Henry James actually served, refused to serve as a religious conscientious objector, paid the $500 commutation fee that exempted Brethren from conscription, or hired a substitute is **not yet documented**. His Confederate compiled service record (NARA M324) needs to be pulled.

The maternal-side Brethren ancestors are clearer. Adam W. Knicely (b. 1846) was conscription-age by late 1864. The Brethren community in Rockingham County had a documented confrontation with Confederate authority in March–April 1862, the **Rockingham Rebellion** — a group of armed Brethren and Mennonite men gathered at Swift Run Gap to refuse conscription, were attacked by Stonewall Jackson's 33rd Virginia Infantry, and one was killed before twenty-four surrendered. The leaders were eventually assigned to noncombatant service as army teamsters. Two days after the surrender, the Confederate Conscription Act was amended to add a $500 commutation fee that exempted Brethren and Mennonites from active service. Adam Knicely's experience of the war — like every Brethren man of his generation in the Valley — was shaped by that confrontation.

## What this family looked like by the 1920s

By the early 1920s the Smallwood-Knicely family was rooted in the Brethren community in Augusta and Rockingham counties — a small German-American religious community that had been in the Valley for six generations. Hamilton Osborn was nearly 60, blind, attending Pleasant Valley with Ida. His son Charles Richard was 20. Charles met Ethel May Knicely, the 18-year-old daughter of Elijah Gold Knicely from the same Middle River congregation. They married on December 11, 1922 in Hagerstown, Maryland.

Their oldest son Nelson was born March 26, 1925 in Weyers Cave. He grew up Brethren in the Valley. He served in the Second World War or the Korean War — the Hunter Holmes McGuire VA Hospital where he died in December 1955 was a Veterans Administration facility; his service file would resolve which conflict. He was 30 years old at his death. His son Gene was 8.

Ethel May died first, in 1949 in Harrisonburg. Charles Richard remarried Ellen Bunch Pittman in 1955 and lived another 22 years, dying in Staunton in 1977. He chose to be buried at Middle River Brethren Cemetery in New Hope — the cemetery of his first wife's community, not his patrilineal Piedmont family plot. **By 1977 Charles Richard had identified more deeply with the Knicely-Brethren community than with the Smallwood-Methodist patriline.** That is itself a finding about how this family understood itself by the late 20th century.

## What we still don't know

1. **The Maryland bridge.** Whether James Smallwood (1755 Stafford) was the son of William H. Smallwood (b. 1720 Charles County MD) and therefore a great-great-grandson of Col. James Smallwood (1639). Resolution: Y-DNA test ($137) plus King George Will Book 2 + Spotsylvania records pull at LVA.

2. **The slaveholding inventory.** What enslaved people James Smallwood held at his 1792 death. Resolution: King George inventory book pull.

3. **Cora Jane's maiden name.** Whether Brady's great-great-grandmother Cora Jane (b. 1877 Rockingham, m. Elijah Gold Knicely) was herself a Knicely (making the marriage cousin endogamy) or a different surname. Resolution: 1880 Rockingham census + Mack Library membership records.

4. **Henry James Smallwood's mother.** Whether his mother was Elizabeth Jackson (married to James Smallwood 1792 in 1816) or a second wife Elizabeth Gear. Resolution: 1850 Rockingham census image.

5. **The Preston County WV detour.** Why James Smallwood (1792) moved to Preston County and why his son Henry James returned to Rockingham. Whether this is where the Smallwood patriline first joined the Brethren community.

6. **Nelson's military service.** Which war (WWII or Korea), which branch, which unit, what cause of death at age 30 in the VA hospital. Resolution: NPRC SF-180 request or VA FOIA.

7. **Hamilton Osborn's path to the Brethren in the 1880s.** What congregation received him, who sponsored him, what drew him in. Resolution: Mack Library congregational records.

## What this paper is not

This paper traces Brady's direct paternal Smallwood patriline only. It does not cover:

- Nelson's wife **Lutie Fannie Clark** and her Monacan / Clark line — covered in the sister project *The Monacan Heritage* (Brady's paternal grandmother).
- The maternal side — covered in the sister project *Virginia Deans and Donahoes* (Brady's maternal grandmother Mary Ellen Donahoe).
- The Loudoun County branch of the American Smallwood family documented in Jean (Carter) Smallwood's 2002 book — Brady's distant cousins at most, descending from a different Charles County MD ancestor's grandchildren than Brady's branch.
- The famous American Smallwoods (General William Smallwood of Maryland, gospel musician Richard Smallwood, abolitionist Thomas Smallwood, etc.) unless and until the Maryland bridge is documented and a specific kinship can be claimed.

Family members reading this paper should treat the verified end-to-end chain (Brady back to James Smallwood 1755 Stafford VA + Catherine Clark Smallwood) as documented. Treat the bridge to Col. James Smallwood (1639) as plausible but unproven, pending the Y-DNA test and the King George records pull.
