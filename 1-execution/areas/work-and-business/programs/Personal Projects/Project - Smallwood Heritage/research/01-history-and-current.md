# 01 — Regional and Community History (Smallwood Branch)

Owner: Historian agent (Phase 2 parallel run, 2026-05-09).
Scope: regional / community history that frames Brady's Smallwood line. Not a genealogy of named ancestors — that is the work of the bridge specialist (`02`), identity reconciler (`05`), primary-source extractor (`08`), and census reader (`09`). This file gives those agents the historical scaffolding their record-level findings hang on.

The line in question moves across two distinct Virginia geographies and three distinct cultural worlds: the **Anglican / English-origin Northern Neck of Virginia** (Stafford, King George, Spotsylvania) in the colonial era, the **German Baptist Brethren and Scots-Irish Shenandoah Valley** (Rockingham, Augusta) from the early 19th century onward, and a **20th-century Maryland-Virginia industrial corridor** (Hagerstown, Baltimore, Richmond, Staunton) that pulled Brady's grandparents out of the Valley and back into it across a single 35-year arc.

## 1. The Northern Neck of Virginia, 1660–1800

The Northern Neck — the peninsula bounded by the Potomac, Rappahannock, and Chesapeake Bay — was the demographic and political core of colonial Virginia and the cradle of Brady's documented Smallwood ancestor James Smallwood (b. ABT 1755 Stafford VA — d. 15 JAN 1792 King George VA).

### 1.1 The Proprietary

The entire Northern Neck was granted by Charles II in 1649 (regranted 1660) to seven Cavalier supporters, eventually consolidated under the **Fairfax Proprietary**. Thomas, 6th Lord Fairfax, controlled this 5+ million acre patent through a Land Office in Lancaster County until the American Revolution dissolved it. Quitrents went to the Fairfax family, not the Crown. This is critical for the Phase 2 bridge specialist: **a James Smallwood holding land in Stafford or King George before 1776 would appear in Northern Neck Land Grant Books at the Library of Virginia**, not in Crown patents. (Source: Library of Virginia, "Northern Neck Land Grants and Surveys," guide at https://www.lva.virginia.gov/public/guides/opac/nngrntsabout.htm.)

### 1.2 Stafford and King George

Stafford County was formed in 1664 from Westmoreland; King George was carved out of Richmond County in 1721 and absorbed parts of Stafford in 1776. The two counties sit adjacent on the south bank of the Potomac, **directly across the river from Charles County, Maryland** — where Col. James Smallwood (1639) anchored the Maryland Smallwood line in 1664 (Keith, A.L., "Smallwood, Charles Co., MD," *Maryland Historical Magazine*, vol. 21, 1926). The Potomac at this stretch is two to three miles wide; the **Aquia, Potomac, and Hooe's Ferries** ran constant traffic across it from the 1690s onward.

Cross-river migration from Charles Co. MD into Stafford / King George VA was routine in the 1730s–1770s, driven by:
- Maryland's tightening Catholic restrictions after 1689 — though by the mid-18th century the Smallwoods of Charles Co. were Anglican / planter-class, the cultural pressure pushed many established families south
- Tobacco-soil exhaustion in southern Maryland and the search for fresh acreage in the Virginia Piedmont
- The Brent, Hooe, Mason, and Fitzhugh family networks that owned land on both sides of the river and routinely settled younger sons across it

This Charles Co. MD → Northern Neck VA corridor is documented qualitatively in Mitchell, Robert D., *Commercialism and Frontier: Perspectives on the Early Shenandoah Valley* (University of Virginia Press, 1977), ch. 1, and in Hofstra, Warren R., *The Planting of New Virginia: Settlement and Landscape in the Shenandoah Valley* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 2004), pp. 23–52, both of which describe the Tidewater Anglican planter migration as a parallel stream to the better-known Scots-Irish and German waves coming down the Great Wagon Road.

### 1.3 Stafford's burned records — the central archival problem

Brady's Phase 2 work hits a wall here that needs to be named up front. **Most of Stafford County's pre-1800 records were destroyed**: a portion in a 1750s courthouse fire and the bulk during Union occupation in the 1862–1863 Fredericksburg-area campaigns. What survives is a fragmentary set of deed and will book copies, plus chancery causes that ended up in surrounding county courts (King George, Spotsylvania, Prince William) or at the General Court in Richmond. (Source: Library of Virginia, "Stafford County Records," https://www.lva.virginia.gov/chancery/full_case_detail.asp?CFN=179-...; see also Eddins, Roger G., *Stafford County, Virginia, Will Books, 1699–1709, 1748–1763*, reconstructed from Land Office and Northern Neck records.) **King George County records, by contrast, are largely intact from 1721 onward** — and James Smallwood died there in 1792, which makes King George the primary attack surface, not Stafford.

### 1.4 Anglican parish geography

Stafford and King George were divided into a small set of Anglican parishes — **Overwharton Parish (Stafford)**, **Brunswick Parish (King George)**, and **St. Paul's Parish (which spanned both)**. Vestry books from these parishes survive in transcribed form in the Library of Virginia and at the Virginia Historical Society. A James Smallwood active 1775–1792 in this geography would, if conventionally Anglican, appear in vestry records, tithables lists, or burial registers at one of these parishes. The **absence** of a Smallwood in surviving Northern Neck Anglican records would itself be a clue — possibly pointing to Baptist, Methodist, or Quaker affiliation, all of which had small but documented footholds in the area by the 1770s.

## 2. The Shenandoah Valley, 1730–1860

By the time Henry James Smallwood (b. JAN 1832 VA) was a young adult, his world was a fully formed Valley society — but that society was less than a century old and built from three distinct migration streams that never fully merged.

### 2.1 The three settlement streams

The standard scholarship — Wayland, John W., *A History of Rockingham County, Virginia* (Ruebush-Elkins Co., 1912; Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/historyofrocking00wayl); Wayland, *The German Element of the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia* (1907; Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/germanelementofs00wayl); Waddell, Joseph A., *Annals of Augusta County, Virginia, 1726 to 1871* (2nd ed. 1902; Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/annalsofaugustac00wadd_0); and Mitchell, *Commercialism and Frontier* (1977) — converges on three streams:

1. **Scots-Irish (Ulster Presbyterian)**, entering the Valley primarily from Pennsylvania via the Great Wagon Road from the 1730s. Concentrated in **Augusta County's southern half** (Tinkling Spring, Stone Church, Bethel Presbyterian congregations) and pushing further south into Rockbridge.
2. **German Lutheran, Reformed, Mennonite, and Brethren ("Dunkers")**, also down the Great Wagon Road from Lancaster, Berks, and Lebanon counties PA, beginning in the 1730s. Concentrated in **Rockingham County and northern Augusta** (Massanutten settlement, Mole Hill, Linville Creek, Mount Crawford, Weyers Cave, the Middle and North Rivers of the Shenandoah). Hofstra (*Planting of New Virginia*, 2004) and Wust, Klaus, *The Virginia Germans* (University Press of Virginia, 1969) are the standard treatments.
3. **English-origin Tidewater Anglican planters**, moving west from the Northern Neck and Tidewater Virginia from the 1740s — slower-moving, smaller in number, but politically dominant because they brought the county-court justice-of-the-peace tradition, Anglican / later Episcopal parish structure, and slaveholding plantation agriculture. (Mitchell, ch. 2; Hofstra, ch. 3.)

The Smallwood line, if H1 holds (Northern Neck origin), is in stream 3 — and would have entered the Valley one or two generations later than the German and Scots-Irish founding settlers. That timing is consistent with the GEDCOM: James Smallwood died in King George VA in 1792; his son or grandson would have been the first Smallwood to appear in Augusta or Rockingham, plausibly between 1810 and 1830. Henry James Smallwood (b. 1832 VA) is likely the second Valley generation.

### 2.2 The English-stream geography in Augusta

The Tidewater-origin English-Anglican settlers who reached Augusta tended to cluster along the **wagon-road corridor between Staunton and Winchester** and along the **South River** valley — Sangerville (where Hamilton Osborn Smallwood was born in 1864), Mount Sidney, Verona, Fort Defiance, and Weyers Cave. This is precisely the geography of Brady's Smallwood ancestors from G+2 forward. (Source: Waddell, *Annals*, ch. 14; Couper, William, *History of the Shenandoah Valley* (1952), vol. 1.)

The English-origin and German communities in this corridor were not segregated socially — there was significant Anglican-German intermarriage by the 1830s — but they retained distinct religious affiliations. A Smallwood-Hess marriage (Henry James Smallwood + Annie Elizabeth Hess, c. 1855) is exactly the kind of cross-stream pairing that becomes routine by mid-century. (Wust, *The Virginia Germans*, ch. 7, treats the second-generation Anglicization of Valley Germans and the corresponding German-tinged broadening of Anglican / Methodist congregations.)

## 3. The Brethren community in the Valley

This is the religious and cultural overlay for the maternal half of Brady's Smallwood ancestry — the Knicely, Johnson, Hess, and Wiseman families, all of whom show Brethren cemetery affiliation in the GEDCOM.

### 3.1 Origin: Schwarzenau 1708 → Germantown 1719

The Brethren ("Dunkers" or German Baptist Brethren, later Church of the Brethren) originated as an Anabaptist movement in **Schwarzenau, Wittgenstein (Germany), in 1708** under Alexander Mack. They were one of the three German "peace churches" alongside Mennonites and Quakers — pacifist, plain-dress, baptizing only adults by triple immersion ("dunking"), and refusing to swear oaths. Persecution in the German states and the Netherlands drove the entire community to Pennsylvania between 1719 and 1735. The first Brethren congregation in America was at **Germantown, PA, 1719**. (Source: Sappington, Roger E., *The Brethren in Virginia: The History of the Church of the Brethren in Virginia* (Brethren Press / Committee for Brethren History in Virginia, 1973), ch. 1; Durnbaugh, Donald F., *The Brethren in Colonial America* (Brethren Press, 1967).)

### 3.2 Migration south: the Linville Creek congregation

By the 1740s Brethren families were moving south down the Great Wagon Road into the Shenandoah Valley. The first organized Brethren congregation in Virginia was **Linville Creek**, in present-day Rockingham County, formally established in 1755 — though Brethren families had been in the area since the late 1730s. (Sappington, ch. 2; Wayland, *German Element*, pp. 116–134.) From Linville Creek the community spread to Flat Rock, Beaver Creek, Greenmount, Mill Creek, Pleasant Valley, Middle River, and eventually Summit — the very cemeteries where Brady's Knicely / Johnson / Wiseman ancestors are buried.

### 3.3 The three congregations Brady's family attended

| Congregation | Location | Ancestor(s) buried | Founded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pleasant Valley Church of the Brethren | Weyers Cave, Augusta VA | Ida Florence Johnson (d. 1948) | Late 19th c., daughter congregation of Mill Creek / Cooks Creek |
| Middle River Church of the Brethren | New Hope, Augusta VA | Elijah Gold Knicely (d. 1955) and family | Mid-19th c., on the Middle River of the Shenandoah |
| Summit Church of the Brethren | Centerville (near Stuarts Draft), Augusta VA | George Robert Knicely (d. 1944), Sarah Amanda Wiseman (d. 1934) | Late 19th c. |

All three congregations are still active as of 2026 within the **Shenandoah District of the Church of the Brethren**, headquartered in Weyers Cave. The Shenandoah District maintains a digitized congregational history catalog at https://shenandoahcob.org. Membership lists, council minutes, baptism records, and cemetery transcriptions for all three are preserved at one of two places: the local congregation (most complete for 20th century) and the **Brethren Historical Library and Archives, Elgin IL** (denominational HQ; most complete for 19th century and for cross-congregational records). (Sources: Sappington, *Brethren in Virginia*, appendices; Brethren Historical Library and Archives finding aids, https://www.brethren.org/bhla/.)

### 3.4 Brethren cultural traits relevant to Phase 2

- **German-language services persisted into the 1880s in many Valley Brethren congregations.** A Knicely or Hess of the 1840 generation was likely bilingual; the 1880 generation was likely English-monoglot. This affects how their names appear in census records — "Kneisly" / "Kneisle" / "Kniesly" / "Knisely" can all be the same family across two census takers.
- **Endogamy was the norm**, not the exception. Marriages typically happened within the Brethren community across two or three counties. The Elijah Gold Knicely + Cora Jane Knicely[?] marriage, if both are descended from Adam Kniceley + Martha Loker as the GEDCOM suggests, is a textbook second-cousin pattern — and would be recorded with full disclosure of the relationship in the church's marriage minutes (consanguinity was tracked, not hidden, because the Brethren did not enforce a Catholic-style prohibition).
- **Plain dress and lifestyle** were enforced through 1900 and gradually relaxed through the 20th century. The Knicely / Wiseman generation buried at Summit were almost certainly plain-dress observers; the Charles Richard Smallwood generation (b. 1902) was the transitional cohort.
- **Brethren refused civil oaths and military service.** This is the connection to section 4 below.

## 4. Augusta and Rockingham in the Civil War

This is the political-history backdrop for Henry James Smallwood (b. JAN 1832 VA) — who was 29 in April 1861 and prime conscription age throughout the war — and for the Brethren / Mennonite community that includes most of Brady's maternal-side Valley ancestors of the same generation.

### 4.1 The Valley as a strategic theater

The Shenandoah Valley was the breadbasket of the Confederacy and the primary Confederate invasion corridor northward — Stonewall Jackson's 1862 Valley Campaign, the 1863 Gettysburg approach, Jubal Early's 1864 Washington raid all moved up the Valley. The corresponding Union counter — most catastrophically Philip Sheridan's "Burning" of September–October 1864 — laid waste to mills, barns, granaries, and livestock from Staunton north to Strasburg. (Sources: Heatwole, John L., *The Burning: Sheridan in the Shenandoah Valley* (Rockbridge Publishing, 1998); Mahon, Michael G., *The Shenandoah Valley, 1861–1865: The Destruction of the Granary of the Confederacy* (Stackpole, 1999).)

Augusta and Rockingham counties were Confederate-aligned by their county-court and slaveholding planter elite — but the German-speaking Brethren and Mennonite communities, concentrated in northern Augusta and Rockingham, were **the largest pacifist population in the Confederacy**.

### 4.2 The Brethren and Mennonite conscientious objection

The **Confederate Conscription Act of 16 April 1862** was the first in American history. It made all white males 18–35 (later 17–50) subject to military service. The Brethren, Mennonites, and Quakers immediately petitioned the Confederate Congress for exemption on religious grounds. (Source: Sappington, *The Brethren in Virginia*, ch. 6; Lehman, James O. and Nolt, Steven M., *Mennonites, Amish, and the American Civil War* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 2007), chs. 3–4.)

A first **substitute clause** allowed exemption by paying for a substitute or by providing $500 to the Confederate treasury — but the Confederate Congress passed a specific religious exemption on **17 October 1862** for "members of religious denominations who shall by oath or affirmation declare that they are conscientiously opposed to the bearing of arms," contingent on a $500 payment or providing a substitute. This was an unusually permissive provision compared to anything Britain had granted Quakers — but $500 in 1862 currency was a serious sum, and many Brethren families raised it through congregational mutual aid. (Sappington, ch. 6; Lehman & Nolt, ch. 5.)

What followed was nonetheless a period of acute crisis for the Valley peace churches:
- A group of **72 Brethren and Mennonite men** attempted to flee through the Allegheny Mountains to Union lines in March 1862 to avoid initial Confederate state militia call-ups. Most were captured; some were jailed in Richmond at Castle Thunder before being released or paroled. (Sappington, ch. 6, citing John Kline diaries.)
- **Elder John Kline** of Linville Creek, the most prominent Brethren minister in Virginia, traveled freely between Confederate and Union lines to minister to Brethren congregations on both sides until he was assassinated by Confederate irregulars near Broadway, Rockingham VA, on **15 June 1864**. His murder is the central trauma in 19th-century Virginia Brethren memory. (Funk, Benjamin, *Life and Labors of Elder John Kline, the Martyr Missionary* (Brethren Publishing House, 1900); Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/lifelaborsofelde00funk.)
- Several Brethren ministers were arrested for refusing to take loyalty oaths or for preaching against the war. Sappington (ch. 6) documents at least eight such cases.

**For Brady's project this matters concretely.** The bridge generation between James Smallwood (1755) and Henry James Smallwood (1832) — likely two intermediate generations — sits in the Northern Neck-to-Valley migration window. By 1861 the Smallwood patriline appears to be in Augusta / Rockingham but is *not* documented as Brethren-affiliated. The maternal lines (Hess, then later Knicely / Johnson / Wiseman) are documented Brethren. **Henry James Smallwood (b. 1832), age 29 at the war's start, presents a research question, not a known answer:**

- If he was in a Brethren-aligned community by 1861 (via his marriage to Annie Elizabeth Hess, b. FEB 1840 Rockingham VA — also Brethren-likely), he may have claimed religious exemption or paid the $500.
- If he was in an Anglican / Episcopal / Methodist English-stream community, he would have been subject to the Confederate draft and would appear in Confederate service records (the standard set is the **National Archives M324 compiled service records** for Virginia units, accessible via Fold3).
- A third possibility: as a substitute provider, listed only in the Substitute Bond records held at the Library of Virginia.

This is a Phase 2 question for the bridge specialist (`02`) and erasure specialist (`03`) to resolve jointly.

### 4.3 The Burning, 1864

In late September and October 1864, Sheridan's cavalry under Wesley Merritt and George Custer systematically burned barns, mills, granaries, and haystacks across the Valley from Staunton north — the so-called "Burning." Heatwole (1998) reconstructs property-by-property destruction. Brethren and Mennonite farms were not spared as a class, though some individual farms were protected by Union officers who recognized the religious exemption claims.

Damage claims filed by Valley Brethren and Mennonites with the **Southern Claims Commission** (1871–1880) are a uniquely valuable Civil War source for this community — claimants had to prove loyalty to the Union to recover, and the resulting depositions document family circumstances, neighbors, congregational membership, and the war-period record of each claimant in extraordinary detail. The full set is at the National Archives (Records of the Treasury Department, RG 56) and is searchable via FamilySearch. (Source: Mills, Gary B., *Civil War Claims in the South* (Heritage Books, 1994); FamilySearch, Southern Claims Commission collection.) **A Phase 2 search of the Southern Claims Commission for Smallwood, Hess, Knicely, Johnson, Wiseman, and Loker in Augusta and Rockingham is one of the highest-leverage single archival pulls available.**

## 5. The 20th-century arc — Valley out, Maryland-Baltimore-Richmond loop, Valley back

Brady's framing already lays out the geographic puzzle of the modern generations: a Rockingham/Augusta agrarian rural community in 1900 that, within fifty years, scatters across the Valley-to-Baltimore industrial corridor and then partly comes home. The pattern is not unique to this family — it is the standard 20th-century Shenandoah Valley out-migration arc, driven by World War I-era industrial demand in Hagerstown and Baltimore, World War II-era Federal employment and military deployment, and the post-1945 collapse of the small-farm Brethren economy.

### 5.1 Hagerstown 1922 — Charles & Ethel's marriage

Charles Richard Smallwood (b. 1902 Rockingham VA) married Ethel May Knicely (b. 1904 Mount Crawford, Rockingham VA) on **11 DEC 1922 in Hagerstown, MD**. Hagerstown sits in Washington County MD, **70 miles down the Valley from Mount Crawford via the Valley Pike (US-11) / I-81 corridor** — the same wagon-road that brought the German Brethren south in the 1740s, now running them back north for industrial work. The Brethren community in Washington County MD was substantial (Welsh Run, Beaver Creek, Boonsboro, Hagerstown City congregations, all in the **Mid-Atlantic District** of the Church of the Brethren), so a Hagerstown marriage is community-internal, not a break with the family's religious world. Marriage in Maryland rather than Virginia in 1922 may simply reflect Maryland's more relaxed marriage license requirements (no waiting period, lower age of consent) — a common pattern for Valley couples of this period.

### 5.2 The Baltimore endpoint

Both Elijah Gold Knicely (d. 1955 Baltimore MD) and Cora Jane Knicely[?] (d. 1956 Baltimore MD) died in Baltimore — likely in their late 70s, probably living with adult children or in care facilities, and almost certainly **buried back at Middle River Church of the Brethren in New Hope VA**, where Elijah's burial is documented in the GEDCOM. The Baltimore-Hagerstown-Cumberland-Valley urban corridor was the 20th-century out-migration channel for thousands of Valley families. Baltimore in particular drew Brethren and Mennonite young adults to industrial and clerical work from the 1910s through the 1960s, with the **Baltimore Bethany Brethren congregation** (1894) serving as the urban anchor for the diaspora. (Source: Bowman, Carl F., *Brethren Society: The Cultural Transformation of a "Peculiar People"* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1995), ch. 5, on the urban migration.)

### 5.3 Nelson at the Richmond VA Hospital, 1955

**Nelson Roudnal Smallwood (b. 26 MAR 1925 Weyers Cave, Augusta VA — d. 22 DEC 1955 Hunter Holmes McGuire VA Hospital, Richmond VA, age 30)** died at what was then the principal Department of Veterans Affairs medical center in Virginia, named for the Confederate surgeon and post-war medical reformer Hunter Holmes McGuire. The McGuire VA hospital opened in its modern form in 1946 and was — and still is — the regional referral center for Virginia, West Virginia, and parts of North Carolina veterans needing tertiary care. **A 30-year-old man dying at McGuire in 1955 was almost certainly:**

- A WWII veteran (which Nelson would have been — born 1925, eligible for service from 1943 forward) or possibly a Korean War veteran (Korea ran 1950–53, age 25–28 for him), or both
- Receiving treatment for either a service-connected condition (combat injury, exposure-related illness, service-aggravated chronic disease) or a non-service condition for which VA care was the affordable option for a young father of limited means

The death certificate, which Phase 2 should pull from the Virginia Department of Health Office of Vital Records, will give cause of death, attending physician, occupation, and informant — and the attending hospital's status as a VA hospital is presumptive evidence that Nelson had a verified veteran status in the National Personnel Records Center (NPRC) at St. Louis. The corresponding **DD-214 / WWII service record** is the next pull, via NARA's eVetRecs request system. (Source: NARA, "How to Request Military Records," https://www.archives.gov/veterans/military-service-records.) A 1973 fire at NPRC destroyed an estimated 80% of Army personnel records for individuals discharged 1912–1960, but Navy, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard records survived intact, and reconstructed Army records are routinely available from auxiliary sources (final pay vouchers, VA claims files, draft registration cards). The **WWII Draft Registration ("Old Man's Draft" of 1942 covered men 45–64, but the Fourth Registration is online; younger registrants 18–37 are in NARA RG 147)** for Nelson would be at FamilySearch or Ancestry.

### 5.4 Charles Richard's return to Staunton, 1977

Charles Richard Smallwood (b. 1902 Rockingham — d. 1977 Staunton, Augusta VA) ended his life **back in the Valley**, in Staunton — the county seat of Augusta and the largest population center in the original Smallwood territory. Staunton in 1977 had two principal medical facilities: **Western State Hospital** (the historic state psychiatric hospital, founded 1828) and **King's Daughters' Hospital** (the local general hospital, later folded into Augusta Health). The death record will say which. Ethel May Knicely had predeceased him in 1949 in Harrisonburg VA at age 45 — so he survived his wife by 28 years and outlived his son Nelson by 22 years.

The family's center of gravity in 2026 — based on Brady's GEDCOM and grounding context — appears still to be Augusta and Rockingham, with diaspora into Maryland, North Carolina, and (in Brady's branch) west to Arkansas via Brady's father Dennis Eugene "Gene" Smallwood.

## 6. Smallwood family in Augusta / Rockingham today (broad strokes)

This section is intentionally light — Phase 4 will own the living-relatives mapping. Three observations frame what Phase 4 will find:

1. **Smallwood is not a numerous surname in the central Valley.** A surname distribution check in current Virginia voter and property records (publicly searchable via the Virginia Board of Elections and the Augusta and Rockingham county GIS / parcel viewers) shows Smallwood concentrated in the **Loudoun / Frederick / Northern Virginia** corridor (consistent with Jean Carter Smallwood's book line) and a **smaller cluster in the central Valley around Staunton, Waynesboro, and Mount Sidney** (consistent with Brady's line). The two clusters do not overlap geographically and have not for several generations.
2. **The Knicely / Knisely / Kniceley surname remains active in Augusta County**, particularly around Mount Crawford, Bridgewater, Weyers Cave, and Stuarts Draft — and the Brethren congregations at Pleasant Valley, Middle River, and Summit are still operating and are excellent first-stop contacts for living-relative confirmation.
3. **Death notices for Brady's direct ancestors should appear in the** *Staunton News-Leader*, *Staunton Spectator* (historical), and *Harrisonburg Daily News-Record*. Newspapers.com coverage of these papers spans 1880–present (varying by paper); a Phase 2 newspaper-obit pull on every G+1 / G+2 / G+3 ancestor in the GEDCOM will produce community context and, in many cases, surviving-relative names that bridge into Phase 4.
