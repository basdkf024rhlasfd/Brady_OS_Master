## 1. German Baptist Brethren / Church of the Brethren

### Origin and theology

The Brethren movement was founded in **Schwarzenau, in the County of Wittgenstein in Hesse, in 1708**. Eight adults — Alexander Mack and seven companions — submitted to mutual triple immersion in the Eder River, breaking simultaneously with the Lutheran state church and with the existing radical Pietist movement that rejected outward ordinances entirely. They called themselves simply *die Brüder* ("the brethren"). Outsiders attached labels — Tunkers, Dunkers, Dunkards, German Baptist Brethren — that rotated for two centuries before the denomination formally adopted **Church of the Brethren** at the 1908 Annual Conference (Donald F. Durnbaugh, *European Origins of the Brethren*, Brethren Publishing House, 1958; *The Brethren Encyclopedia*, vol. 1, "Beginnings").

The theology synthesized Anabaptist ecclesiology (believer's baptism, congregational discipline, ban) with Radical Pietist interiority (the new birth, the witness of the Spirit, devotional reading of Scripture in community). Five distinguishing markers run through the historical record:

1. **Adult baptism by triple forward immersion** in flowing water — once for the Father, once for the Son, once for the Holy Spirit. The candidate kneels in the stream; the elder presses the head forward into the water three times. This is the single most diagnostic Brethren practice and the one most likely to appear in a congregational record book as a dated entry.
2. **Love feast** (*Liebesmahl*, agape meal) — held twice yearly in most congregations, combining footwashing, a full fellowship meal, the holy kiss, and the bread-and-cup communion. Restricted historically to baptized members in good standing; non-members were seated as observers.
3. **Pacifism / nonresistance.** The Brethren sit alongside Mennonites and Friends as the three "historic peace churches." Bearing arms, taking oaths, going to law against a brother, and (until the early 20th century) holding civil office were grounds for discipline.
4. **Plain dress and plain speech.** Men in collarless coats and untrimmed beards (no mustache, which carried military associations); women in cape dresses and prayer coverings. The plain dress order was relaxed in stages between the 1880s and the 1950s and is now retained primarily among the conservative offshoots (Old German Baptist Brethren, Dunkard Brethren, etc.).
5. **German-language worship** persisted in Pennsylvania and Virginia congregations into the late 19th century. The shift to English was congregation-by-congregation and was largely complete in the Shenandoah Valley by 1900 (Roger E. Sappington, *The Brethren in Virginia*, Committee on Brethren History in Virginia, 1973, ch. 4).

### Distinguishing from Mennonites and Amish

All three are Anabaptist-adjacent, plain-dress, pacifist, and German-speaking — but they are not interchangeable. The Brethren practice **triple forward** immersion; Mennonites pour or sprinkle (with a small minority practicing single immersion). Brethren observe a **full love feast with footwashing**; Mennonites observe footwashing but not the agape meal as a unified rite. Amish are an **18th-century Swiss-Mennonite split** under Jakob Ammann; they retain horse-and-buggy plainness and the *Meidung* (strict shunning) the Brethren never adopted. In Rockingham County specifically, all three communities co-existed by the 1780s, with the Brethren concentrated in the central Valley (Linville Creek, Flat Rock, Beaver Creek congregations) and the Mennonites slightly to the east (Weavers, Trissels). Surname overlap (Hess, Wenger, Miller) is heavy and surname-alone is not a denominational tell.

### Migration into the Shenandoah Valley

Brethren immigration to America began in **1719**, when Peter Becker led the first organized congregation from Krefeld to Germantown, Pennsylvania. The 1729 Mack-led migration brought the rest of the Schwarzenau founders. From Germantown the church spread west and south through Lancaster and Berks counties, then down the Great Wagon Road into the Valley of Virginia. The earliest documented Brethren families in Rockingham/Augusta arrived in the **1740s–1770s**, with the major wave concentrating in the 1750s–1760s. Linville Creek congregation (organized circa 1756, Rockingham) and Flat Rock (Augusta, organized circa 1790 from earlier worship gatherings) are the mother congregations from which most later Valley congregations — including Pleasant Valley, Middle River, and Summit — eventually budded off.

## 2. The three Brethren congregations in Brady's tree

### Pleasant Valley Church of the Brethren — Weyers Cave, VA

Burial: **Ida Florence (Johnson) Smallwood**, b. 1869 Mayes Furnace, Rockingham VA — d. 1948 Mount Sidney, Augusta VA. Wife of Hamilton Osborn Smallwood Sr., mother of Charles Richard Smallwood. She is the only Brethren-cemetery burial on the Johnson-Smallwood side of the tree.

Pleasant Valley congregation sits in the southern end of Augusta County's Brethren cluster, in the Weyers Cave / Mount Sidney corridor. The congregation is part of the **Shenandoah District of the Church of the Brethren** (one of 23 districts, headquartered in Weyers Cave itself). Records flow:

- Active congregational records (membership, baptism, marriage from the present back to roughly 1900) are typically held at the local church office.
- Historical records (council minutes, older membership rolls, deacon books) for Shenandoah District congregations are concentrated at the **Alexander Mack Memorial Library, Bridgewater College, Bridgewater VA** (~12 miles north). The Mack Library holds the official Shenandoah District archive.
- Cemetery interment records are held both at the church and at the Mack Library, with overlap to Find a Grave for the visible markers.

Contact path: Shenandoah District office (Weyers Cave) → local church clerk → Mack Library archivist for anything pre-1950.

### Middle River Church of the Brethren — New Hope, VA

Burials: **Elijah Gold Knicely** (b. 9 FEB 1878 Rockingham VA — d. 22 NOV 1955 Baltimore MD; body returned for burial). His daughter **Ethel May (Knicely) Smallwood** (b. 21 OCT 1904 Mount Crawford, Rockingham VA — d. 21 SEP 1949 Harrisonburg VA) is also recorded at Middle River per the GEDCOM, though some sources place her at a different Augusta cemetery — verify on next site visit.

Middle River sits east of Staunton in the New Hope area, also in Shenandoah District. The congregation traces to the second half of the 19th century as a daughter congregation of one of the older Augusta Brethren mother churches. Same archival pattern as Pleasant Valley: local clerk for active records, Mack Library for historical depth.

### Summit Church of the Brethren — Centerville (near Stuarts Draft), VA

Burials: **George Robert Knicely** (b. 1852 VA — d. 1944 Augusta VA) and his wife **Sarah Amanda (Wiseman) Knicely** (b. 1860 VA — d. 1934). They are the parents of Elijah Gold Knicely above.

Summit congregation is in southern Augusta County in the Stuarts Draft / Centerville cluster — historically a conservative end of the district. Same archival pattern.

### Where to write first

Two letters cover the entire Brethren question:

1. **Alexander Mack Memorial Library, Bridgewater College** — request membership / baptism / marriage / cemetery record copies for the named individuals across Pleasant Valley, Middle River, and Summit congregations; request any council-minute references to discipline, transfer, or the Knicely / Knicely marriage. Elgin is generally responsive to family-history mail requests and holds the *Gospel Messenger* / *Brethren Almanac* obituary indexes that would catch any Knicely / Wiseman / Johnson death notices 1880–1960.

A third inquiry, lower priority, is **Eastern Mennonite University's Menno Simons Historical Library, Harrisonburg VA**, which holds Anabaptist-adjacent material that occasionally catches stray Brethren references the Mack collection missed.

## 3. The Knicely / Kniceley / Knicely surname (H5)

### German origin

The surname is the anglicized form of **Kneisel / Kneisle / Kneisly**, a southwestern German diminutive form (the *-le* / *-li* ending is Swabian / Alemannic) of *Kneis* — itself a vernacular form of *Knie*, "knee" — used historically as a personal nickname or trade name. Variants documented in U.S. records include Kneisel, Kneisle, Kneisly, Knisely, Knicely, Kniceley, Knicely, Knaisly, Kneisli. The variant landing in Brady's tree (Knicely / Kniceley) is the form that stabilized in the central Shenandoah Valley after the Civil War; earlier Pennsylvania records of the same family will appear under Kneisly or Kneisle.

### Migration pattern

The Kneisel / Kneisly cluster fits the dominant Pennsylvania-German immigration profile: **Palatinate / Württemberg origin, arrival at Philadelphia 1727–1755, settlement in Lancaster and Berks counties Pennsylvania, southward migration to the Shenandoah Valley along the Great Wagon Road in the 1750s–1780s**. The Strassburger-Hinke *Pennsylvania German Pioneers* ship-list series (3 vols., 1934, reprinted by Genealogical Publishing) is the canonical entry point; ship lists from 1727–1808 will catch the immigrant-generation Kneisel / Kneisly arrivals at the port of Philadelphia. Brady has not yet pulled the specific arrival; that's a Phase 2 ILL or LVA-trip target.

The **Pennsylvania German Society** publications and the **Palatines to America** German Genealogy Society quarterly are the secondary literature most likely to carry a Kneisel / Kneisly origin essay; both are accessible by membership or by ILL through any university library. The Henry Z Jones *Palatine Families of New York* and *Palatine Families of Ireland* series do not appear to cover this surname directly, but the Annette Burgert *Eighteenth Century Emigrants from German-Speaking Lands to North America* series (2 vols., Pennsylvania German Society, 1983 / 1985) is the highest-resolution source for tying a specific Kneisel emigrant to a specific Palatinate or Württemberg parish.

The Kneisel / Kneisly family is almost certainly part of the Brethren or Mennonite migration from the Lancaster-Berks corridor; the persistence of the surname in the same congregations as the Hess / Wiseman / Loker / Wenger cluster down the Valley is the strongest circumstantial evidence. Confirmation requires either (a) a baptism / membership entry in a Lancaster-area Brethren congregation matching a known Kneisel ancestor before the southward move, or (b) a Henkel Press or other Valley church-history monograph naming the immigrant generation by ship and date.

**What's documented now in the GEDCOM:** the line goes back to **George Robert Knicely (b. 1852 VA)** and his wife **Sarah Amanda Wiseman**, but the GEDCOM does not yet name George Robert's parents. The immigrant generation is at minimum 2–3 generations above George Robert — i.e. a Kneisel born in the 1770s–1790s who moved south from Pennsylvania, whose grandson or great-grandson is George Robert. Filling in those two generations is the load-bearing Knicely research task and almost certainly resolves through a combination of the Mack Library Brethren-in-Virginia files and Lancaster-County Brethren congregational records.

## 4. The Knicely × Knicely "cousin marriage" puzzle (H4)

### What the GEDCOM says, restated carefully

- **Elijah Gold Knicely**: b. 9 FEB 1878 Rockingham VA, parents **George Robert Knicely** (b. 1852) + **Sarah Amanda Wiseman** (b. 1860).
- **Cora Jane [Kniceley]**: b. 17 JUL 1877 Rockingham VA, parents **Adam W. Kniceley** (b. 1846) + **Martha Loker** (b. 1845).
- Adam W. Kniceley + Martha Loker were married **25 DEC 1870** Rockingham VA.
- Elijah Gold Knicely + Cora Jane were married **5 AUG 1880** Rockingham VA.

The "wait — they married in 1880 but were both born 1877–78" reading in the agent brief is a date-field misread. The 1880 date on the F-record for Elijah + Cora Jane is the parents-generation marriage entry mistakenly attached to the children's couple, OR (more likely) it is the date of one of Cora Jane's parents' subsequent vital events. Reconciliation: **assume Elijah and Cora Jane married as adults in the 1890s–1900s, not as toddlers in 1880**, and treat the 1880 GEDCOM date as a transcription error to be corrected against the Rockingham County marriage register on the next pass. The 1870 marriage of Adam W. Kniceley + Martha Loker stands.

### Are Elijah and Cora Jane actually cousins?

The two paternal grandfathers are **George Robert Knicely (b. 1852)** and **Adam W. Kniceley (b. 1846)** — both Knicely / Kniceley by surname, both born in Rockingham County, six years apart, both apparently of the same Brethren community. The most economical explanation is that George Robert and Adam W. were **brothers or first cousins**, which would make Elijah and Cora Jane **first cousins (if their fathers were brothers) or second cousins (if their fathers were first cousins)**.

**Resolution path:**

1. Pull the parents of George Robert Knicely (1852) and Adam W. Kniceley (1846) from the 1850 / 1860 / 1870 Rockingham census on FamilySearch. If both children appear in the same household in 1850 or 1860, they are siblings → Elijah + Cora Jane are first cousins.
2. If they appear in adjacent households with the same surname patriarch, they are likely first cousins themselves → Elijah + Cora Jane are second cousins.
3. Cross-check against Mack Library Brethren congregational membership rolls for the Linville Creek / Beaver Creek / Mill Creek cluster, where the Rockingham Knicelys concentrated.

This is a one-evening census-pass resolution, not a multi-month archive trip.

### Brethren doctrine on consanguinity

The Brethren have historically prohibited marriage within the **Levitical degrees** (Leviticus 18 and 20) — i.e., parent-child, sibling, aunt-nephew, uncle-niece, and (depending on era and congregation) some affinity relationships such as deceased-wife's-sister. **First-cousin marriage was permitted** in most periods, was common in tight rural Brethren clusters because of the small endogamous pool, and would not have triggered church discipline. This puts the Elijah-Cora Jane marriage well within the Brethren norm: socially expected, doctrinally clean, and routinely entered in the council minutes without comment.

The deeper point: in a community that married almost exclusively within itself for four to five generations after arrival in the Valley (Sappington, *Brethren in Virginia*, ch. 7 on endogamy patterns), surname recurrence on both sides of a couple is the rule rather than the exception. Brady's tree is showing a normal Brethren pattern, not an anomaly.

## 5. Adjacent surnames — origin and Brethren signal

### Loker (Martha Loker, b. 1845 Rockingham)

German origin, variant of **Locker / Loecker / Löker**, a Pfälzisch occupational or topographic name. Concentrated in 18th-century Lancaster / York / Berks PA and migrated south with the Brethren / Mennonite stream. The Loker / Locker presence in Rockingham 1840–1900 is consistent with the Brethren cluster — same migration pattern as Knicely.

### Hess (Annie Elizabeth Hess, b. 1840 Rockingham; her father Jacob Hess, b. 1786)

Definitively German — one of the most common Palatinate / Swiss-German surnames in Pennsylvania-German country. Hesses are heavily represented in both Brethren and Mennonite congregations across Lancaster / Berks PA and the Shenandoah Valley. The Jacob Hess (b. 1786) generation falls right in the second-wave PA-to-VA Brethren migration window. **Working assumption:** the Hess line in Brady's tree is a Brethren / Mennonite line that ought to show up in the Mack Library congregational membership rolls.

### Wiseman (Sarah Amanda Wiseman, b. 1860, m. George Robert Knicely)

Ambiguous origin: can be **English Wiseman** (occupational — "wise man," sage) or anglicized **German Weisman / Weismann** ("white man"). The Brethren-cemetery burial of Sarah Amanda at Summit Church of the Brethren is strong evidence that her line was Brethren-affiliated, which biases the German-origin reading. To confirm, pull her parents' generation from the 1850 / 1860 Augusta census; an immigrant grandparent named Weismann would settle the question.

### Anthony (Sarah Ann Anthony, b. 1807, m. Jacob Hess)

Ambiguous. Most American Anthonys descend from English Quaker stock (Friends, not Brethren), but a meaningful minority are anglicized German **Antoni / Antonius / Anthon**. Given the marriage into the Hess family and the Rockingham geography, the German-origin reading is more likely than the Quaker reading here, but this is an open question — pull her parents' census appearance.

### Johnson (Richard James Johnson, b. 1835, m. Frances Elizabeth Manning; daughter Ida Florence Johnson buried Pleasant Valley Brethren)

Almost certainly **English or Scots-Irish** in origin — Johnson is one of the most generic Anglo surnames in 19th-century Virginia and only resolves with parent-generation evidence. The Brethren-cemetery burial of Ida Florence Johnson tells us Ida adopted (or was raised in) the Brethren community, but does not retroactively make the Johnson line German. The most likely scenario is a Scots-Irish Johnson family that married into the Brethren community in the generation of Richard James (1835) or his parents — a common 19th-century pattern in the Valley as the German enclave broadened.

### Manning (Frances Elizabeth Manning, b. 1840)

English. Heavily concentrated in Tidewater and Piedmont Virginia; minimal German-Brethren signal. Same pattern as Johnson — likely an Anglo line that married in.

## 6. The Smallwood patriline — what denomination?

The Brethren context above is the **maternal-side overlay** (Knicely, Wiseman, Hess, plus the Johnson-by-marriage). The Smallwood patriline itself shows **no Brethren cemetery affiliation** in the current GEDCOM. Working hypotheses on the patriline's religion, in priority order:

1. **Methodist (most likely for the Henry James 1832 generation onward).** The Methodist Episcopal Church South was the dominant Protestant denomination in the Shenandoah Valley after the 1845 split, particularly among Anglo families of Tidewater origin who had migrated west. Methodist circuit-rider records and Methodist church cemetery affiliations are the first place to check for Henry James Smallwood (1832) and Hamilton Osborn Smallwood Sr. (1864).
2. **Anglican / Episcopal (most likely for the James 1755 generation in Stafford / King George VA).** The Northern Neck of Virginia in the colonial period was solidly Anglican — Stafford was within **Aquia Episcopal Parish** (Aquia Church, built 1751–1757, still standing), and King George within **Brunswick Parish** and **Hanover Parish**. Vestry books, parish registers (where they survived the Civil War courthouse burnings), and tithables lists are the load-bearing colonial-era records. Stafford's 1862 courthouse burning destroyed most county records, but **Aquia Church parish records survive in fragmentary form** at the Library of Virginia and at the Virginia Historical Society. The James Smallwood (1755) probate at King George 1792 is the highest-leverage single document for confirming the colonial parish affiliation — an Anglican-style will (with bequest language characteristic of the established church) versus a dissenter-style will would settle the question.
3. **Baptist (lower probability).** The Northern Neck saw a significant Baptist surge in the 1770s–1790s (Separate Baptists, Regular Baptists), and the Smallwood line could plausibly have moved in that direction during or after the Revolution. The bequest language in the 1792 King George will is again the diagnostic.

**What the record actually shows for Henry James Smallwood (1832):** the GEDCOM records his death in 1904 Rockingham VA but does not record a burial place. That's a Phase 2 Find a Grave / cemetery-walk task to close. The default working assumption — **Methodist Episcopal Church South affiliation, buried in a county or community cemetery rather than a Brethren one** — should be challenged by the actual cemetery record before it propagates into the whitepaper.

The cultural implication is non-trivial: if Henry James Smallwood (Methodist Anglo) married Annie Elizabeth Hess (Brethren German), this is a **denominational and cultural bridge marriage** in the second half of the 19th century — exactly the period when the German-Anglo line in the Valley was softening. Brady's tree, in other words, is sitting on top of the Valley's classic mid-19th-century cultural integration story: an Anglican-descended Tidewater line marrying into the German-Brethren rural cluster, with the children raised in (or near) the Brethren community and the next generation increasingly assimilating to mainline Protestantism. Verifying this bridge is one of the highest-leverage cultural findings the project can produce.
