## 1. Civil War — Henry James Smallwood (b. 1832) and the Rockingham conscription pressure cooker (H6)

### 1.1 Geography and conscription law

Henry James Smallwood was born in January 1832 in Rockingham County, Virginia, and died in 1904 in Rockingham. [Source: 4 — GEDCOM, last updated 6 MAY 2026]. He was 29 at the firing on Fort Sumter (April 1861) and 30 at the passage of the Confederate Conscription Act (April 16, 1862). [Source: 5 — Confederate Conscription Acts 1862–1864, Wikipedia compilation of primary statute texts]

The first Confederate Conscription Act (April 16, 1862) made all white men between 18 and 35 liable for three years of military service. [Source: 5] The age was extended to 45 in September 1862 and ultimately to 17–50 in February 1864. [Source: 5] Henry James Smallwood was inside the conscription window from the very first day of the draft — there is no version of the Civil War where the Confederate state did not come for him.

Three legal exits existed:

1. **Substitute hire.** A man liable for conscription could pay another man (typically over- or under-age, or a foreign national) to serve in his place. The substitute market in Rockingham/Augusta inflated rapidly — substitutes that cost $300 in 1862 cost $3,000+ in Confederate currency by 1863. The substitute clause was repealed by the Confederate Congress in December 1863. [Source: 5]
2. **$500 commutation fee.** The original 1862 act allowed "members of the Society of Friends, and the association of Dunkards, Nazarens and Mennonites" to escape combat service by paying a tax of $500 each or hiring a substitute. [Source: 5 — Confederate Conscription Acts] **This applied only to documented members of the named pacifist denominations.** A Methodist or Episcopalian Smallwood would not have qualified.
3. **Class exemption / occupation.** Five days after the original act, Congress added exemptions for ministers, teachers, certain skilled tradesmen, overseers of 20+ enslaved persons (the "Twenty Negro Law"), and a handful of other categories. [Source: 5]

### 1.2 The Rockingham Rebellion (March–April 1862) — Brethren in armed resistance

In March 1862, Virginia Governor John Letcher ordered the Rockingham militia incorporated into Confederate regular service. **Several hundred German Baptist Brethren and Mennonite men in Rockingham County refused.** [Source: 5 — *Encyclopedia Virginia*, "Rockingham Rebellion" entry]

The sequence:
- **March 12, 1862** — Letcher's incorporation order. [5]
- **Mid-March 1862** — groups of Mennonites and Dunkards captured trying to flee through the western Virginia mountains. [4 — *Shenandoah at War* secondary]
- **April 2, 1862** — Stonewall Jackson learned of an armed gathering of resisters at Swift Run Gap on the Blue Ridge, the Rockingham/Greene/Albemarle border. [5]
- **April 3–13, 1862** — Jackson dispatched the 33rd Virginia Infantry under Lt. Col. John R. Jones to suppress them. One resister was killed. Twenty-four surrendered. [5]
- **April 14, 1862** — the resistance leader Gillespie ("a tigrous looking fellow," in one staff officer's diary entry) was captured. [5]
- **April 16, 1862** — the Confederate Conscription Act passed Richmond. The $500 commutation and substitute mechanisms were almost certainly written into the act partly in response to Rockingham. [5]

Jackson's eventual disposition was to assign the captured Brethren and Mennonite men as **teamsters** — a noncombatant role consistent with Brethren scruples but still inside the Confederate logistics chain. [5] This was the Confederate state's first concrete acknowledgment that the Rockingham Brethren community could not simply be coerced into combat.

**The Rockingham Rebellion is the most significant single political event for the Brethren community in this branch's geography during the Civil War.** It happened in Henry James Smallwood's county, in his thirtieth year. He was almost certainly aware of it. Whether he was on the resister side, the militia side, or a third-party bystander is the central H6 question.

### 1.3 Was Henry James Smallwood Brethren in 1862?

Going-in answer: **almost certainly not.** The Brethren cemetery affiliations in this family tree (Pleasant Valley Brethren Church for Ida Florence Johnson, Middle River Church of the Brethren for Elijah Gold Knicely, Summit Church of the Brethren for George Robert Knicely and Sarah Amanda Wiseman) are all on the **maternal side of Brady's grandfather Nelson Smallwood**, entering the Smallwood line by marriage when Charles Richard Smallwood (b. 1902) married Ethel May Knicely (b. 1904) in Hagerstown MD in 1922. [Source: 4 — GEDCOM]

Henry James Smallwood himself married Annie Elizabeth Hess (b. FEB 1840 Rockingham VA — d. 1920 Augusta VA). Hess is a German surname common in both Brethren and Lutheran communities of the Shenandoah Valley, but Annie's father Jacob Hess (b. 1786 — d. 1861) does not appear in the GEDCOM with a Brethren cemetery affiliation. [Source: 4] **Until the Phase 2 religious/cultural agent (file 04) confirms or denies Hess Brethren membership, the working assumption is that the Smallwood-Hess household was Methodist or Lutheran or Episcopal, not Brethren.**

If that assumption holds, Henry James Smallwood had three plausible Civil War paths:
- **Confederate service.** Default expectation for a 30-year-old white male in Rockingham VA in 1862. He would appear in the Compiled Service Records of Confederate Soldiers from Virginia (NARA M324, digitized on Fold3) under "Smallwood, Henry" or "Smallwood, H. J." in a Rockingham-raised regiment — most likely the 10th Virginia Infantry, 33rd Virginia Infantry, or the 7th Virginia Cavalry.
- **Substitute or commutation payment.** Possible if the family had cash or land collateral. Would appear in county court records (substitute contracts were filed locally) and in regional newspapers (substitute brokers advertised).
- **Disabled / overage exemption.** Less plausible at age 30 but possible. Would appear in the local enrollment officer's records.

### 1.4 What the record currently says

**There is no Henry James Smallwood Confederate service record in hand as of this writing.** The Library of Virginia's Confederate Rosters Index (LVA Research Guides) holds the relevant union catalog, and Fold3's *US, Civil War Service Records (CMSR) — Confederate — Virginia, 1861–1865* is the digitized backbone. [Source: 5 — LVA Research Guides; Fold3 publication 42] Both are paywalled or appointment-required. **This is the single highest-leverage open question on the Smallwood Civil War record. A thirty-minute Fold3 search closes it.**

### 1.5 The maternal-side Brethren ancestors in scope

For completeness, the GEDCOM ancestors who would have been conscription-age during the war:

- **Adam W. Knicely** (b. 1846 Rockingham VA — d. 1923 Harrisonburg VA, bur. Oak Lawn Cemetery, Bridgewater). Age 16 at first conscription (1862), age 18 at the February 1864 expansion to ages 17–50. **In scope for the 1864 draft.** Brethren burial confirmed (Oak Lawn is the historic Brethren cemetery in Bridgewater). Default expectation: claimed Brethren exemption, paid the $500 fee or hired substitute, or served as teamster. [Source: 4 — GEDCOM]
- **George Robert Knicely** (b. 1852 VA — d. 1944 Augusta VA, bur. Summit Church of the Brethren). Age 10 at first conscription, age 12 at expansion. **Out of scope for the war itself.** Postwar Brethren community member. [4]
- **Jacob Hess** (b. 1786 — d. 1861). **Died before the conscription act and well past military age.** Out of scope. [4]
- **Henry James Smallwood's father** (G+4 SMALLWOOD generation, currently unidentified in GEDCOM beyond James 1755). Likely deceased or overage by 1862. [4]

The **Brethren Historical Library and Archives in Elgin IL**, **Bridgewater College's Alexander Mack Memorial Library**, and the three local congregations (Pleasant Valley, Middle River, Summit) hold council minutes that document conscription exemption requests by name. [Source: 5 — Sappington, *The Brethren in Virginia* (1973); standard cite]. Adam Knicely's name should appear there if he claimed exemption. **This is a Phase 3 mail-lookup item, not a travel-required one — the Brethren Historical Library accepts written reference queries.**

## 2. The Plecker era and the marriage of Nelson Smallwood to Lutie Fannie Clark (H7)

### 2.1 The negative finding on Smallwood-line surnames

Walter Ashby Plecker, director of the Virginia Bureau of Vital Statistics from 1912 to 1946, compiled and circulated a list of "mongrel" surnames in January 1943 to "Local Registrars, Physicians, Health Officers, Nurses, School Superintendents, and Clerks of the Courts" across Virginia. [Source: 5 — *Encyclopedia Virginia*, Plecker entry; Letter from Walter A. Plecker, January 1943, Virginia Indian Archive item 197]

**Cross-checked against the published 1943 list (multiple independent transcriptions):** none of the surnames in the Smallwood patriline or its first-generation in-law cluster appear. Specifically not present:

- **Smallwood** — not on the list anywhere in Virginia. [Source: 5 — Native Heritage Project transcription; Mixed Race Studies transcription]
- **Knicely / Kniceley** — not on the list. [5]
- **Hess** — not on the list. [5]
- **Johnson** — appears once for **Halifax County** only. Brady's Johnson ancestors are in Rockingham (Richard James Johnson b. 1835 Rockingham — d. 1895 Port Republic), not Halifax. Same surname, different geography, not the same flagged group. [5]
- **Manning** — not on the list. [5]

**Augusta County and Rockingham County do not have independent entries on the 1943 Plecker list.** The only adjacent reference is the note "Migrants to Augusta" appended to the Rockbridge County entry — meaning Plecker tracked specific Rockbridge surnames (Cash, Clark, Coleman, Duff, Floyd, Hartless, Hicks, Mason, Mayse, Painters, Pultz, Ramsey, Sorrells, Southerds, Tarry, Tyree, Wood, Johns) who had relocated north into Augusta. [5]

**This is a clean negative finding.** The Smallwood patriline as such was not on Plecker's surname-flag radar. Sister-project Monacan Heritage research file 02 already documents the Clark / Sorrells / Painter / Mason cluster Plecker did target.

### 2.2 The marriage exposure: Nelson Roudnal Smallwood + Lutie Fannie Clark

But the negative finding does not close the door, because **Nelson Roudnal Smallwood (b. 1925 Weyers Cave, Augusta VA) married Lutie Fannie Clark — a documented Plecker-list Rockbridge Clark.** This marriage took place in approximately the mid-1940s based on the Monacan project's running dates (Lutie was Brady's paternal grandmother and Nelson died in 1955).

Under the Racial Integrity Act of 1924 as amended in 1928, a marriage between a "white" person and a "colored" person was a felony. [Source: 5 — Encyclopedia Virginia; sister project file 02-plecker-era.md] County clerks were personally liable for issuing the wrong license. Plecker's correspondence to Rockbridge Clerk A. T. Shields (Library of Virginia accession 1160754, covering 1912–1943) shows that Plecker contacted clerks proactively whenever a flagged-surname applicant came in. [5]

**Three open factual questions on this marriage:**

1. **Where was the license issued?** Almost certainly either Augusta County (groom's residence) or Rockbridge County (bride's residence). Augusta is the more probable venue if the family wanted to avoid Rockbridge's Clark surname flag — a known evasive pattern from this era documented in the Monacan project file 02. **Pull the marriage record from the Augusta County Clerk's Office and the Rockbridge County Circuit Court Genealogy Office, both for 1944–1948 windows, surname Smallwood and Clark.**
2. **Was Lutie's race designation on the license "white," "colored," or did the family use a Pocahontas-Exception self-classification?** This determines whether the family encountered any Plecker-system pushback at all.
3. **Did Plecker's office (or its post-1946 successor under his successor) write to either county clerk about this marriage?** Plecker formally retired in 1946 and died in August 1947. If the marriage occurred 1944–1946, his office was still active. If it occurred 1947 or later, the system was institutionally intact but Plecker himself was gone. **The Rockbridge County (Va.) Clerk's Correspondence collection (LVA accession 1160754) ends at 1943 — meaning post-1943 correspondence on this marriage, if any, would sit elsewhere in Bureau of Vital Statistics Record Group 36 at LVA.**

### 2.3 Why this matters even though the surname is Smallwood

The Clark family in Rockbridge had spent two generations (1876, 1924–1925) fighting these classification battles in court. *James Clark v. J. P. Moore* (1876, LVA accession 1140710) and *Sorrells v. Shields* (1924–1925, LVA accessions 1140744 and 1164679) both used the Clark family tree as central evidence. [Source: 5 — sister project file 02] Lutie Fannie Clark's relatives knew the legal terrain. Her decision to marry Nelson Smallwood — a man whose surname was unambiguously not on Plecker's list and whose home county (Augusta) had no independent Plecker county entry — was likely deliberate. **The Smallwood family knowingly or unknowingly absorbed a Plecker-list family by marriage; the marriage record itself is the document that resolves which.** This is the load-bearing Phase 3 retrieval.

### 2.4 The Smallwood-side Plecker exposure that does NOT appear

Three things are notably *absent*:

- **No Plecker correspondence to the Augusta County Clerk has been catalogued at LVA at the same accession-series level as the Rockbridge correspondence.** This is consistent with the surname-list negative finding: Plecker did not have flagged Augusta surnames to monitor.
- **No Bureau of Vital Statistics altered birth-certificate cases involving the Smallwood, Knicely, Hess, Johnson, or Manning surnames have surfaced in the published Plecker scholarship.** This includes J. David Smith's *The Eugenic Assault on America* and the Virginia Indian Archive collections.
- **No mention of the Smallwood, Knicely, Hess, Johnson, or Manning surnames in *Mongrel Virginians: The Win Tribe* (Estabrook & McDougle, 1926).** That study targeted the Amherst/Rockbridge/Nelson/Bedford Monacan-descended community, not the Rockingham/Augusta Brethren community. [Source: 4 — sister project file 02]

The negative findings collectively support the going-in framing: **Plecker's enforcement zone covered Brady's Smallwood geography administratively, but Plecker's enforcement focus did not include the Smallwood/Knicely/Hess cluster as a target population.** The exposure that exists is the marriage exposure, not the surname exposure.

## 3. Northern Neck slaveholding, 1755–1820 (the doctrine demands this section)

The Genie operating doctrine states: *will not soft-pedal slaveholding ancestors.* Stafford, King George, and Spotsylvania counties were tobacco and wheat plantation country with very high enslaved populations through the colonial and early-republic period. Asking whether Brady's Northern Neck Smallwood ancestors held enslaved people is asking whether they participated in the most ordinary economic arrangement of their time and place.

### 3.1 What the GEDCOM and the secondary record say so far

**James Smallwood (b. ABT 1755 Stafford VA — d. 15 JAN 1792 King George VA)** is the bridge generation. His will was probated in King George County. The executors' bond was dated July 2, 1793, and the executors were **Charles Clark, Thomas Clark, and Catherine Smallwood** (his wife). His will named his wife Catherine, sons Charles Smallwood and William Smallwood, and provided for the remainder of his estate to be divided among his children. [Source: 4 — secondary genealogy compilation citing *Spotsylvania County Virginia Records 1721–1800*; not yet verified against the original probate file]

**Two specific archive pulls would close the slaveholding question:**

1. **King George County Will Book entry, James Smallwood, 1792–1793** — the original will. Wills of this period and place routinely named enslaved persons individually as bequests ("I give to my son Charles one negro woman named ___ and her child ___"). The presence or absence of such bequests is the cleanest single data point.
2. **King George County estate inventory, James Smallwood, 1793–1794** — the appraisers' inventory of the estate, taken after probate. This lists every enslaved person owned at death by name, age, and assessed value. An adult enslaved person in 1793 King George VA was typically valued at £40–£80 sterling (roughly $200–$400 in then-current Virginia currency), and would appear in the inventory between the livestock and the household goods.

**Most colonial Stafford County records were destroyed in the Civil War (1862 courthouse burning).** [Source: 4 — *00-master-sources.md*] Surviving Stafford fragments are at the Library of Virginia. **King George County records, by contrast, largely survived** — King George is the higher-yield archive for the James Smallwood bridge generation specifically. This is a single-day LVA reading-room pull. If the will and inventory are not at LVA, they are at the King George County Clerk's office in King George VA.

### 3.2 What is *highly* likely going-in

A landed white male in King George County, Virginia, owning enough property to require probate and three executors in 1792, is statistically very likely to have owned enslaved persons. The 1790 federal census for King George County recorded a substantial enslaved population — King George was a Tidewater plantation county, not a Shenandoah Valley smallholder county. A Smallwood family with enough standing for the executors to be Clarks (Catherine's family) was almost certainly inside the slaveholding tier rather than below it.

The 1780s–1820s also saw the Virginia manumission window (the 1782 Manumission Act allowed individual slaveholders to free enslaved persons by deed or will; the law was tightened in 1806 to require the freed person to leave the state within a year). [Source: 5 — *Virginia Slaves Freed in Virginia after 1782*, freeafricanamericans.com] **A Phase 3 cross-check against the published list of Virginia manumissions 1782–1806 will confirm whether any Smallwood of King George or Stafford freed enslaved persons in this window.** A manumission would not undo the original slaveholding; it would, however, change the moral and legal posture of the family within the broader Northern Neck slave economy. The default expectation, absent evidence, is that the Smallwoods did not manumit.

### 3.3 Catherine Clark's family

Catherine Clark (b. 1756 Stafford VA — d. 1820 Spotsylvania VA), James Smallwood's wife, came from the Stafford / King George Clark family. Her relatives Charles Clark and Thomas Clark served as executors of James's estate. The PROJECT.md identifies a possible (unproven) connection to the Carter of Corotoman gentry network through this Clark line. [Source: 4 — PROJECT.md]

**If that connection holds, the Clark family on Catherine's side was a substantial slaveholding family.** The Carter of Corotoman estates were among the largest plantation operations in colonial Virginia, with hundreds of enslaved persons. Even a cadet branch of the Clark-Carter network in Stafford or King George would have been firmly inside the slaveholding tier. Catherine's 1820 death in Spotsylvania County means a Spotsylvania probate may also exist — and a Spotsylvania widow's estate of this period would also have included enslaved persons unless previously distributed.

**Two additional archive pulls:**
- **Catherine Smallwood (née Clark) probate, Spotsylvania County, 1820–1822.** Will book and inventory.
- **Charles Clark and Thomas Clark — King George/Stafford 1790s–1820s.** Same logic. Even if James Smallwood's own holdings were modest, his executors' holdings establish the family's economic and racial position.

## 4. Reconstruction-era and post-1900 racial classification anomalies — Smallwood patriline

Census reader agent (file 09) owns the systematic walk through 1860, 1870, 1880, 1900, 1910, 1920, 1930, and 1940 federal censuses for every Smallwood in scope. This file owns the legal/policy interpretation of any anomalies they find.

**Going-in framing:**
- 1860 — Henry James Smallwood would be 28, in Rockingham VA. Census race designation expected: "W" (white).
- 1870 — Henry James, age 38, postwar. Same expectation. **Watch for any household members listed "M" (mulatto) — that designation existed on the 1870 schedule and was sometimes applied to white-passing mixed-ancestry households.**
- 1880 — Henry James, age 48. Same.
- 1900–1940 — Hamilton Osborn Smallwood, Charles Richard Smallwood, then Nelson Roudnal Smallwood lines.

**No anomaly has been surfaced as of this writing.** If file 09 surfaces one — particularly a "M" designation or an "Indian" designation on any pre-1924 record — the legal interpretation goes here. Pre-1924, a Virginia "M" or "Indian" designation was a registrar's discretionary call; post-1924, Plecker's office would have overwritten it. The relevant comparative case is the Cora Almond 1940 census entry where "Ind" was scratched out and replaced with the "2" (colored) code, documented in sister-project file 02. [Source: 5]

**Until file 09 surfaces something, this section sits open and quiet.** That is the correct posture.
