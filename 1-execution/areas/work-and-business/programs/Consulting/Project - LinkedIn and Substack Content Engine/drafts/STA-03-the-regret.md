By the time I was in business school, Dropbox was normal.

Remote desktop was normal. The idea that you could access your files from anywhere — that was just how things worked for me and everyone I knew.

But it hadn't reached Clarksville, Virginia. Not in a way that made sense to a guy who'd been doing paper tax returns for twenty years.

My dad had tax offices in multiple small towns. He'd drive between them during tax season — town to town, meeting clients, doing the work in person, paper files in the car. The kind of schedule that grinds a person down over years, not weeks.

I tried to get him on Dropbox. Sort of. Not hard enough. Not patiently enough. I was in my twenties and I thought the tools were obvious.

They weren't obvious. They were obvious to me because I lived in a different world — even though I'd grown up at the end of the same gravel road.

That's the thing about technology gaps. They don't feel like gaps when you're on the right side. They feel like common sense. And when you're on the wrong side, they feel like noise — one more thing somebody's kid is telling you about that doesn't solve the problem in front of you right now, which is that you've got to drive to South Boston before lunch.

The last real conversation I had with my dad, he told me he was exhausted. Not frustrated. Not angry. Exhausted. The fight was up.

That was 2014.

I don't think Dropbox would have saved King Video. But I think his last couple of years could have been less exhausting. I think driving between three towns with paper files when the technology to eliminate that existed — and I knew about it — is something I'll carry for a long time.

I don't think he needed a better business. I think he needed better tools.

I think about that every time I help someone set up a system that saves them two hours of manual work. Every time I watch someone's face when they realize the AI just did in forty seconds what used to take them all morning.

The tools are here now. The gap between what's available and what actually reaches people is smaller than it's ever been.

I'd like to close it the rest of the way. Before anyone else runs out of gas.
