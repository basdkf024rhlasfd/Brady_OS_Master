## Substack Thought Leadership Essay

# AI Is the Best Thing to Happen to Small Town America in Thirty Years

Last month I built an AI agent for a friend of mine in Danville, Virginia. He runs a real estate operation — not a tech company, not a startup, just a guy who finds properties and does deals. The kind of business that runs on phone calls, spreadsheets, and knowing which contractor picks up on the first ring.

The agent handles a task he used to spend every Monday morning on. Research, comparisons, summaries — the kind of work that's not hard, just slow. Takes him about six hours.

The agent does it in about forty minutes. While he sleeps.

He called me the first Tuesday after it ran and said — and I'm paraphrasing — "what the hell, man, why didn't this exist five years ago?"

It did exist five years ago. Sort of. The pieces were there. But they required a developer, a budget, and the kind of technical infrastructure that assumes you already have an IT department. In other words — it existed for companies in San Francisco and New York and Austin. Not for a real estate guy in Danville.

That's changed. And I don't think people have fully processed what that means.

---

Here's the thesis, and I'll say it plainly: AI could be the biggest renaissance small town America has ever seen.

I know how that sounds. I know the current narrative is doom — jobs disappearing, deepfakes, machines replacing humans, the usual. And some of that is real. I'm not dismissing it.

But the doom narrative is missing something important. For the last 20-30 years, the story of American innovation has been one of concentration. Knowledge work moved to cities. Tech moved to coasts. The tools that let businesses compete at scale were expensive, complex, and required talent that had already moved to where the money was.

Small towns got left behind. Not because the people there were less capable — I grew up with some of the sharpest operators I've ever met — but because the infrastructure of modern business wasn't built for them.

AI changes that equation. Not theoretically. Right now.

All the knowledge ever developed. Every playbook, every process, every competitive advantage that used to require a department — it's sitting right there. Accessible to anyone with an internet connection and a willingness to be frustrated for a couple of weeks while they figure out how to use it.

That's a different thing than what the internet promised in 2005. The internet gave you access to information. AI gives you access to capability.

---

The doomsday people need more nuance. I keep hearing "AI is going to destroy jobs" and "AI is going to replace creative work" and I think — you're not wrong, but you're not looking at the whole picture.

Let me give you a weird example.

I made a song. An AI-generated song for the Danville Humane Society. Just an experiment — can I create something that sounds decent, that serves a real purpose, that helps a local nonprofit?

Turns out, yes. PETA reached out about it. They were interested.

Now — did AI destroy a job there? Was there a jingle writer in Danville who lost a gig? No. There was no jingle writer in Danville. There was no budget for one. The Danville Humane Society was never going to hire a musician to write them a custom song. That capability simply didn't exist in that context.

AI didn't replace something. It created something from nothing.

I think this is the pattern people are missing. In big companies and big cities, sure — AI is going to reshape existing roles. That's real and that's worth taking seriously. But in small towns? In businesses that never had access to those capabilities in the first place? AI is purely additive.

The local HVAC company that's been running on paper invoices and a filing cabinet isn't worried about AI replacing their back office team. They don't have a back office team. AI is the back office team.

The independent insurance agent who's been doing manual comparisons by hand for fifteen years isn't threatened by AI automation. She's liberated by it.

---

I think about my dad a lot when I think about this stuff.

He ran small businesses in Southern Virginia — video stores, tax offices, you name it. An operator through and through. The kind of guy who'd drive an ice cream truck if that's what it took to make payroll.

The tools available now would have changed his life. Not in a dramatic, movie-montage way. In a "you don't have to drive to three towns today because the paperwork can be done remotely" way. In a "here's an AI that can draft your client communications while you focus on the tax returns" way.

Small, practical, immediate improvements. That's what matters. Not the grand vision of artificial general intelligence. Not the philosophical debates about consciousness. Just — can this tool save you three hours this week?

For most small business owners I talk to, the answer is yes. They just don't know it yet.

---

Here's what I think the inflection point looks like.

For the last two years, the AI hype has been dominated by big tech. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft — fighting over foundation models and enterprise contracts. That's fine. That's how the technology gets built.

But the interesting thing — the thing I'm watching — is what happens when the tools get simple enough that you don't need a technical background to use them.

We're there. Or close enough that the distinction doesn't matter.

I built those AI agents for my friend in Danville in a weekend. Not because I'm some genius engineer — I'm not an engineer at all. I'm an operator who learned to build things because the problems I was seeing didn't have off-the-shelf solutions.

The barrier to entry has dropped so fast that the people who are going to benefit most are the ones who've been locked out of technology for decades. Small town tradespeople. Independent shop owners. The guy running a landscaping company who's good at landscaping but drowning in scheduling and invoicing.

These people aren't going to depend on some SaaS company in Silicon Valley to solve their problems. They're going to use AI directly. Build their own tools — or have someone like me build it for them in a weekend.

That's new. That's genuinely new.

---

I want to be careful here because I don't want to sound like I'm selling a fantasy. I've seen enough technology hype cycles to know that "this changes everything" usually means "this changes some things, slowly, with a lot of friction."

So let me be specific about what I'm actually saying.

I'm not saying AI is going to save every small town. Some places have structural problems that no technology can fix.

I'm not saying it's going to be easy. The learning curve is real. I've watched smart people bounce off ChatGPT and say "I don't get it" and walk away. The gap between "this technology exists" and "this technology is useful to me" is still significant.

What I am saying is this: for the first time in my adult life, the direction of technological advantage is pointing back toward small operators. The playing field is leveling — not because anyone decided it should, but because the nature of the technology demands it.

AI doesn't care if you're in San Francisco or Clarksville. It doesn't care if you have an engineering team or a laptop on your kitchen table. It works the same either way.

That's never been true before. Not with software. Not with the internet. Not with mobile. Those technologies all favored scale. AI favors capability — and capability is evenly distributed. It always has been. The tools just weren't.

---

If you run a small business — or you're from a small town and you've been watching the AI conversation from the sidelines thinking "that's not for me" — I'd push back on that.

If you like tinkering — if you're the kind of person who taught yourself QuickBooks or figured out how to build your own website in 2009 — the AI tools available right now will produce the largest ROI you've ever seen in your business.

The frustration window is about two weeks. Maybe three. You'll feel like you're talking to a brick wall. The prompts won't work. The output will be weird. You'll wonder what all the fuss is about.

And then it clicks. The AI finally understands what you're asking. And you realize you've just built something that saves you five hours a week. Ten hours a week. Something that would've cost you $50,000 to build three years ago.

I've seen it happen. Over and over. Not in Silicon Valley. In Danville. In Clarksville. In towns that don't have a Wikipedia page.

The renaissance isn't coming. It's here. The only question is who shows up for it.
