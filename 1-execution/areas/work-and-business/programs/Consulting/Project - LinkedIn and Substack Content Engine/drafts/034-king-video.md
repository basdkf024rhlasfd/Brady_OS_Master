## LinkedIn Story Post

My dad ran a video store called King Video in Clarksville, Virginia. Population: not much.

But it wasn't really a video store. It was an arcade, a tanning salon, a place to rent movies and buy trading cards and hats. He ran an H&R Block franchise out of part of it. Had an ice cream truck. An arcade in the mall. A travel agency at one point.

When you grow up at the end of a gravel road and the nearest career path is a textile mill or Russell Stover — you figure it out. You become the ecosystem because there isn't one.

Then Redbox showed up at the McDonald's. Hollywood Video opened. Netflix did what Netflix does.

Dad pivoted to tax offices. Multiple towns, driving between them during tax season. All paper-based. I remember thinking — man, if he could just get on Dropbox. Remote login. Stuff that felt completely normal to me and my friends but hadn't touched that part of the world yet.

I never fully helped him make that jump. I think about that a lot.

Last real conversation we had, he told me he was so exhausted. The fight was up. That was 2014.

I don't tell this story to make a point about technology adoption curves or digital transformation. I tell it because the gap between what's available and what actually reaches people — that gap has a cost. And sometimes the cost is someone you love running out of gas before the tools get there.

That gap is smaller now than it's ever been. I'd like to keep it that way.

## Substack Origin Essay

# The Last Video Store at the End of the Gravel Road

There's a building in Clarksville, Virginia that used to be a video store. I don't know what's in it now. Probably nothing.

My dad — Gene Smallwood — ran that store. King Video. If you're picturing a Blockbuster, scale it down by about 90% and then add a tanning booth and an arcade cabinet.

King Video was never really about videos. It was about being the guy in a small town who figured out what people needed and then figured out how to provide it. Movies, sure. But also video games. Trading cards. Hats. Tanning. At one point, a travel agency. He had an H&R Block franchise running out of part of the space. He ran an ice cream truck. He had an arcade setup in the mall.

That's what entrepreneurship looks like when there's no ecosystem around you. No startup scene. No coworking space. No angel investors. The career options in that part of Southern Virginia were textile mills, Goodyear, Corning Glass, Russell Stover, Burlington Industries. Good jobs. Hard jobs. Jobs that were already starting to leave.

Dad wasn't an entrepreneur because he read a book about it. He was an entrepreneur because nobody was going to do it for him.

---

I grew up at the end of a gravel road. Went to high school in Dry Fork, Virginia — a place named after a creek, which tells you everything about the ambition of the founding committee.

The video store was my education in business. Not in the MBA sense. In the "watch your dad figure out payroll at the kitchen table" sense. In the "he's driving to three towns today because tax season doesn't care about your gas budget" sense.

Dad had this ability to see what a community needed before the community could articulate it. A tanning salon in a video store sounds absurd until you realize — where else are people going to tan in Clarksville? He wasn't diversifying revenue streams. He was just paying attention.

That instinct is something I think about constantly now. The best business ideas I've seen don't come from market analysis. They come from someone noticing that the people around them are underserved and deciding to do something about it.

---

Here's the part I haven't fully made peace with.

Redbox came to the McDonald's. That was the beginning. Then Hollywood Video opened — an actual chain, with actual corporate money behind it. Then streaming. You know this story. Everybody knows this story. It's the same story in ten thousand small towns.

Dad adapted. He always adapted. The tax offices became the main thing. H&R Block franchise work, spread across multiple towns. He'd drive between them during tax season — town to town, office to office, all paper-based.

And here's what kills me. By the time I was in college, Dropbox was normal. Remote desktop was normal. The idea that you could access your files from anywhere — that was just how things worked for me and everyone I knew.

But it hadn't reached Clarksville yet. Not really. Not in a way that made sense to a guy who'd been doing paper tax returns for twenty years.

I tried to get him on Dropbox. Sort of. Not hard enough. Not patiently enough. I was in my twenties and I thought the tools were obvious. They weren't obvious. They were obvious to me because I lived in a different world — even though I'd grown up at the end of the same gravel road.

That's the thing about technology gaps. They don't feel like gaps when you're on the right side of them. They feel like common sense. And when you're on the wrong side, they feel like noise — one more thing somebody's kid is telling you about that doesn't solve the problem in front of you right now, which is that you've got to drive to South Boston before lunch.

---

The last real conversation I had with my dad, he told me he was exhausted.

Not frustrated. Not angry. Exhausted.

The fight was up. His words.

That was 2014.

I don't think Dropbox would've saved King Video. I don't think any single tool would have. The forces that killed small town video stores — and textile mills, and a hundred other things — were bigger than any one piece of software.

But I do think his last couple of years could have been less exhausting. I think driving between three towns with paper files in the backseat when the technology to eliminate that existed — and I knew about it — is something I'll carry for a long time.

It's not guilt exactly. It's more like — I had a clear view of a problem I could have solved for someone I loved, and I didn't push hard enough. I assumed the tools would get there on their own. They didn't. Not in time.

---

I tell people I work in the gap between what technology can do and what it actually does for real people in real businesses. That's true. But the reason I care about that gap isn't because I read a case study about it.

It's because I watched it cost someone something that mattered.

My dad was an operator. He built things with his hands and his instincts and his willingness to drive an ice cream truck if that's what it took. He didn't need a pitch deck. He needed someone to sit next to him and say — here, let me show you this thing, it'll save you three hours a week.

That's not a sophisticated technology play. It's not a transformation initiative. It's just — closing the gap. One person at a time, if that's what it takes.

I think about King Video every time I help someone set up a system that saves them two hours of manual work. Every time I watch someone's face when they realize the AI just did in forty seconds what used to take them all morning.

It's not the same. Nothing's the same. But the impulse is.

Close the gap. Before the fight is up.
