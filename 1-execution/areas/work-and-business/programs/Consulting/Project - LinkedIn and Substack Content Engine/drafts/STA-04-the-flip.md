The internet gave you access to information. AI gives you access to capability.

That's the difference, and I don't think most people have processed what it means yet.

Two years ago, AI was a novelty. You could ask it trivia questions and get a weird poem. Useful for party tricks, not much else. The output was close enough to make you curious and far enough off to make you skeptical.

Something shifted. Experts are calling it an inflection point, which is a fancy way of saying: the AI finally understands what you're asking.

You can tell. If you haven't noticed yet, it might be because you haven't had a reason to push it. Or because the $20/month subscription felt like a gamble when you weren't sure what you'd get.

Here's what you get: it can write. It can think through a problem. It can plan. It can build things — real, working tools — from a conversation.

I'm not an engineer. I'm an operator who spent 16 years in finance, analytics, and food service. But I've built AI agents that handle research, draft emails, manage calendars, and do competitive analysis. Not because I learned to code. Because I learned to ask.

That's the shift. You don't need a computer science degree. You don't need a startup. You don't need to be in San Francisco. You need a laptop, an internet connection, and a willingness to be frustrated for about two weeks.

The frustration window is real. I'm not going to pretend this is plug-and-play. The first couple of weeks, you'll feel like you're talking to a brick wall. The prompts won't work. The output will be weird. You'll wonder what all the fuss was about.

And then it clicks. And you realize you just built something that saves you five hours a week — something that would've cost $50K to build three years ago.

You don't need to learn to code. You need to learn to ask.

If you like tinkering — if you're the kind of person who taught yourself QuickBooks or figured out how to build your own website in 2009 — this will be the largest ROI you've ever seen.
