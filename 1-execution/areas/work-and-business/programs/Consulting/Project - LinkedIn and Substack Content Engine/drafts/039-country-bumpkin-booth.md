## LinkedIn Story Post

My high school was in Dry Fork, Virginia. I grew up at the end of a gravel road.

When I got to BYU, I was surrounded by kids who'd taken five AP classes, spoken a second language since middle school, traveled internationally, had parents running interesting businesses. I had excellent teachers — genuinely great people who gave everything they had. But the gap was real.

I think it truly was just a resource issue. Maybe a cultural phenomenon too. But mainly resources.

I did fine. Worked hard. Got into Chicago Booth for my MBA. And then I felt it again — that same gap, wider this time. I was the country bumpkin in hiding. Didn't talk about the gravel road much.

But here's what happened as I got to know people: there were plenty of us in that same situation. First generation immigrants who'd scraped their way in. People who'd tried something big and failed. People who were quietly terrified they didn't belong.

The common thread wasn't where we came from. It was that we all felt like we'd shown up to a race that started before we got there.

I'm not saying those resource gaps didn't matter — they absolutely shaped how hard we had to work to get to the same room. But the room itself was more diverse than it looked from the outside.

And now? The tools I didn't have growing up — the ones that separated the well-resourced kids from the rest of us — they're available to anyone with a laptop and an internet connection.

We all have the same resources now. Congratulations.
