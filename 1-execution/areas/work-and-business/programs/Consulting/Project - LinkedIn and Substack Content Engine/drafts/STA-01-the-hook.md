My dad ran video stores in Southern Virginia called King Video.

If you're picturing a Blockbuster, scale it down by 90% and add a tanning booth.

King Video wasn't really about videos. It was about being the guy in a small town who figured out what people needed — movies, arcade games, trading cards, tanning, H&R Block taxes out of the back room. At one point, a travel agency. He drove an ice cream truck. He ran an arcade in the mall.

That's what entrepreneurship looks like when there's no ecosystem. No coworking space. No angel investors. The career options were textile mills, Goodyear, Russell Stover. Dad wasn't an entrepreneur because he read a book about it. He was one because nobody was going to do it for him.

Then Redbox showed up at the McDonald's. Hollywood Video opened. Netflix did what Netflix does.

He didn't lose because he wasn't smart. He lost because he didn't have access — to the capital, the tools, the infrastructure that was concentrating in cities and big companies while small towns got quieter.

That was the story for 20-30 years. Jobs leaving. Innovation concentrating. The gap widening.

I think AI just reversed the direction.

For the first time, all the knowledge ever developed — every playbook, every process, every competitive advantage that used to require a department — is accessible to a guy running a business out of Clarksville, Virginia.

My dad didn't need a pitch deck. He needed someone to sit next to him and say — here, let me show you this thing. It'll save you three hours a week.

That kind of help exists now. For everyone. Everywhere.

I'd like to keep it that way.
