When I was at Walmart, I had five data scientists on one project.

Not the whole analytics org — one project. Five people with advanced degrees, most of whom had already done time in Silicon Valley, managed by a senior leader who'd built products at companies you've definitely heard of.

I had engineers. Product managers. Lawyers — multiple lawyers, just on pricing alone, making sure we could say what we wanted to say and do what we wanted to do.

That's what it takes to compete at scale. And that's exactly why small businesses couldn't.

My dad was running tax offices across multiple towns in Southern Virginia. Driving between them during tax season. Doing the work himself. No team. No data scientists. No lawyers making sure his marketing was compliant. Just one exhausted guy in a car with paper files in the backseat.

The gap between those two worlds wasn't about intelligence. It wasn't about work ethic — my dad would out-hustle anyone I've met in corporate America. It was about resources. Period.

For 20-30 years, that gap only got wider. Knowledge work moved to cities. Tech moved to coasts. The tools that let businesses compete required talent that had already moved to where the money was.

Here's what changed: AI replaces your need to hire a $150K analyst, a $120K marketer, and a $90K assistant. Not perfectly. Not for everything. But for 80% of what a small operator actually needs? Yeah.

The local HVAC company running on paper invoices and a filing cabinet isn't worried about AI replacing their back office team. They don't have a back office team. AI is the back office team.

The independent insurance agent doing manual comparisons by hand for fifteen years isn't threatened by automation. She's liberated by it.

AI gives every small business a team they could never afford. That's not hype. I've built it. I've seen it work.

And for the first time in my adult life, the direction of technological advantage is pointing back toward small operators.
