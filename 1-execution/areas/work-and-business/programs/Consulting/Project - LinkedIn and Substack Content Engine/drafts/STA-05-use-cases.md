My nine-year-old got genuinely angry at an ice cream truck last week.

Not because he couldn't get one. Because of the margins.

"I can get a whole box of those from Walmart for what they charge for one bar." So over spring break, he and his buddy sat down and built an entire go-to-market strategy for an ice cream wagon business. Pricing. Routes. A marketing plan I actually had to review.

The only bottleneck? Adults. I have to get the dry ice.

Meanwhile, his sister has been using AI to study Caitlin Clark's playing style — pulling up analyses of her footwork, her shot mechanics, building custom practice plans. She's nine.

Here's what else happened in the last few months — not hypotheticals, things I actually built:

I made an AI-generated song for the Danville, Virginia humane society. Took about an hour. PETA reached out about it. That's not a job destroyed — that's a capability that didn't exist before in a town that was never going to hire a jingle writer.

I built a suite of AI agents for a buddy back home who does real estate. Knowledge base, lead research, email drafting, calendar management. Stuff that would've cost him $40K/year in SaaS subscriptions — knocked out in a weekend. He told me it saves him about six hours every Monday morning.

My oldest is using AI for college research and applications. She'll be more prepared than I was, and she'll actually know what she's getting into.

I got in trouble at Southside Elementary for selling Jolly Ranchers on the school bus for a dime. My dad drove an ice cream truck. And now my kid is building an ice cream business with AI over spring break. Same instinct — different tools.

The doomsday predictions need more nuance. In big companies, sure — AI will reshape existing roles. That's real. But in small towns? In businesses that never had access to those capabilities in the first place? AI is purely additive.

These aren't hypotheticals. These are weekend projects now.
