## Customer Problem
- **Who specifically:** Brady Smith, solo operator building consulting pipeline, public credibility, and clearer market positioning through published thinking.
- **Trigger moment:** Good ideas, useful observations, and original framing are generated in conversations, notes, and project work, but they do not reliably become published assets.
- **Current workaround:** Post ad hoc on LinkedIn when energy is high. Keep many ideas in private notes. Treat Substack as "something to start later."
- **Cost of status quo:** Weak compounding reputation, lower surface area for inbound opportunities, repeated reinvention of ideas, and ongoing guilt around "I should be publishing."
- **Evidence:** Direct lived experience. Existing OS doctrine, client work, and original framing already generate enough material, but the system for shipping it is inconsistent.

## Competition
- **Direct:** Other consultants, operators, and AI/ops thinkers who publish consistently and become the default voice in the space.
- **Indirect:** Private notes, sporadic posting, overthinking the perfect content system, and doing nothing.
- **Time pressure:** Reputation compounds slowly and punishes delay. Every month of inconsistent publishing is a missed compounding cycle.

## Operating Model

### Minimal Workflow
1. Capture ideas in one place.
2. Select one source idea each week.
3. Draft one primary piece from that source.
4. Adapt it into LinkedIn-native posts.
5. Schedule using native platform tools.
6. Log what shipped and what should be repurposed later.

### Cadence
- LinkedIn: 2 to 3 posts per week
- Substack: 1 post every 2 weeks to start
- Review: one weekly 30-minute review and one 60-minute creation block

### Working Rules
- Do not build a complex automation system before the cadence is real.
- Native LinkedIn scheduling and native Substack scheduling are enough for this phase.
- The source material already exists. The job is extraction and shipping, not endless ideation.
- If a piece is good enough to say privately, it is usually good enough to draft publicly.
- A clean backlog beats a large backlog.
