Source Intelligence

McKinsey: "Merchants Unleashed"

Published January 2026. McKinsey Retail Practice. 7 co-authors including 2 senior partners.

Key Finding

Agentic AI could allow merchants to reclaim 40% of their time from manual reporting and data reconciliation. In their scenario model, up to 60% of a category manager's current tasks could be automated or standardized.

McKinsey describes a future where every buyer has the equivalent of 20 dedicated AI analysts — running pricing scenarios, flagging assortment gaps, generating vendor negotiation scripts, and monitoring on-shelf compliance in real time. Not as a pilot. As the default operating model.

Here's what matters for the pitch: 71% of merchants surveyed say AI tools have had limited or no effect on their business. And 61% say their organization isn't prepared to scale AI across merchandising at all.

That's the market Kroger is sitting in. A market where nearly everyone is failing at this. Which means the first grocer to actually deploy agentic AI in merchandising — not as a chatbot, not as a Google Cloud press release, but as a working system that changes how 2,000+ category managers operate — gets an advantage that compounds every single quarter.

Wyatt Earp's Read

The AI Gap memo tells Greg he's behind Walmart. True. But McKinsey's data says everyone is behind. This isn't a two-horse race — it's a land grab. The pitch should tell Foran: you're not trying to catch Walmart. You're trying to be the first grocer to actually make this work. That's a winnable race. Catching Walmart is not.

McKinsey also identifies the new roles that emerge: category data partners (intelligence integrators who connect data pipelines to AI agents), cross-functional activation leads (who make sure agent recommendations actually get executed in stores), and trust & policy stewards (who set guardrails and reset agents that drift). None of these roles exist at Kroger today. None of them are in the pitch.

Source Intelligence

Yegge: The Factory Is Coming

Steve Yegge — ex-Amazon, ex-Google, 40-year software veteran. Multiple posts Jan–Feb 2026.

Steve Yegge is the most important voice in AI-assisted software development right now, and he's saying something the retail industry hasn't absorbed yet: the era of the individual AI agent is already over. We're entering the era of AI agent colonies.

Gas Town — Jan 1, 2026

Yegge built an open-source orchestrator that manages 20–30 AI coding agents simultaneously, operating as a coordinated factory. In 17 days, it produced 75,000 lines of code across 2,000 commits. He describes agents with persistent identities, ephemeral workflows ("molecules"), durable execution guarantees, and hierarchical supervision — all running on what he calls the MEOW stack (Molecular Expression of Work).

The AI Vampire — Feb 11, 2026

Yegge confirms that 10x productivity from AI is real, and immediately asks the question no one in retail is asking: who captures the value? His frame: if you work 8 hours at 10x, your employer captures 100% and you burn out. If you work 1 hour at 10x, you capture 100% and your company dies. "Build is the New Buy" — he predicts enterprises will vibe-code their own tools and stop paying for SaaS within 18 months.

Future of Coding Agents — Jan 2026

His prediction: a new class of 100x–1000x engineers will emerge in 2026 — people who can orchestrate 50–100 AI agents simultaneously. Individual productivity will match teams of 50+. And his blunt assessment: "I think big companies are going to be screwed."

Wyatt Earp's Read

Yegge is writing about software development, but the principle is universal. If a single engineer can orchestrate 50 agents to produce the output of 50 people, what happens when you apply that to category management? To pricing analysis? To vendor negotiation prep? McKinsey is describing the same vision from the business side. Yegge is showing the machinery that makes it real. The pitch needs to connect these two dots for Foran.

My Verdict: You're All Being Too Nice

Here's what the pitch currently says, essentially: "Greg, Walmart is ahead on AI. You should hire a CDAIO who reports to you, get an AI board advisor, poach 20 mid-level builders, and build a supplier platform."

That's a solid consulting recommendation. It's also the kind of thing McKinsey would charge you $4M to tell you. And it doesn't convey the existential urgency of what's actually happening.

Here's what I'd tell Foran:

Wyatt Earp's Five Escalations

What the pitch should actually say

1

Stop framing AI as a "capability gap." Frame it as an operating model replacement.

The pitch currently treats AI as something Kroger needs to "add" — a new hire here, a board advisor there, some poached engineers. That framing lets Foran treat it as an initiative. It's not an initiative. It's a new way of operating the entire merchandising function. McKinsey's "Natalie" scenario — where 60% of manual tasks are automated and the daily huddle replaces the weekly performance review — that's not a pilot. That's a new OS. The pitch should say: the category manager role as it exists at Kroger today will not exist in 3 years. Either Kroger redesigns it, or the market does it for them.

2

84.51° is the asset. But it's caged.

Everyone on this team knows 84.51° is world-class at data. But it's being used for personalization and retail media — basically an analytics subsidiary. Yegge's factory model says the right move is to turn 84.51° into Kroger's internal AI agent builder. Not a Google Cloud integration. Not a vendor purchase. A team that builds, deploys, and orchestrates fleets of AI agents across merchandising, supply chain, pricing, and vendor management. The talent is partially there. The data is there. The ambition is not. Milen Mahadevan reporting to Yael instead of Greg is the structural proof that Kroger still thinks of data/AI as a technology function, not a business function.

3

The new roles McKinsey identifies? They should be in the pitch.

The pitch talks about hiring engineers and appointing leaders. It doesn't talk about the entirely new job categories that agentic AI creates. Category Data Partners. Cross-Functional Activation Leads. Trust & Policy Stewards. Agent Orchestration Managers. These roles don't exist at Kroger or anywhere else in grocery. Foran could create them. He could define the template that the industry copies. That's the kind of "first mover" language a former Walmart US CEO responds to — he's done it before.

4

"Build is the New Buy" — and Kroger just proved it with Ocado.

The $2.5B Ocado impairment is the single most expensive lesson in American grocery history about what happens when you buy a vendor's vision instead of building your own capability. Yegge's maxim — "Build is the New Buy" — is the perfect frame for the AI section. The pitch already says "capital-light, partnership-driven." But it should go harder: Kroger should never again write a check for a technology platform it doesn't control. Google Cloud chatbot? Fine as a starting point. But the real investment goes to internal teams building proprietary agent systems on Kroger's own data. The Ocado lesson isn't "be careful." It's "own it or don't do it."

5

Give Foran a number. A scary one.

The pitch is all qualitative right now. "Walmart is ahead." "Kroger needs to move." That's not how operators think. Operators think in dollars and headcount. Here's a number: McKinsey says merchants spend 40% of their time on low-value manual tasks. Kroger employs roughly 2,000+ category managers and merchandising analysts. At an average loaded cost of $120K per analyst, 40% of their time is $96M per year in labor allocated to work that AI agents could do better, faster, and 24/7. That's not a theoretical number. That's real money on a real P&L. And it doesn't count the revenue upside from better, faster decisions. Greg Foran has spent his career finding $50M here, $100M there in operational efficiency. This is right in his wheelhouse.
