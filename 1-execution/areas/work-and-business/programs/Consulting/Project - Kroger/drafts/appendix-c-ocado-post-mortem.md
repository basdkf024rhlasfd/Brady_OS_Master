# Appendix C: Ocado Partnership — Post-Mortem

> Synthesis of insider operational knowledge, public financial data, and strategic analysis. Current as of April 2026.

---

## The Original Thesis

Kroger partnered with Ocado in 2018 to build a network of automated Customer Fulfillment Centers (CFCs) — large-scale robotic warehouses designed to fulfill online grocery orders. The bet: centralized fulfillment would give Kroger a technology-driven edge in e-commerce.

The thesis was wrong. Not because the technology didn't work — but because the economics didn't, the execution model didn't, and the market moved in a different direction.

---

## What Actually Happened

**The economics never penciled.** Ocado wanted Kroger to spend millions on infrastructure that would lose money. Meanwhile, Instacart was offering to build comparable micro-fulfillment capacity on their own dime — profitable from day one. The Ocado Zooms (virtual collaboration sessions to align on execution) were structurally doomed — Ocado's incentives were to keep building CFCs, not to evaluate whether they should.

**The opportunity cost was staggering.** While capital was locked into Ocado CFCs, three marketplace partners were at the table with real deals:
- Uber offered significant marketing commitments to support the partnership
- DoorDash explored co-branded Dashmarts
- Instacart proposed deploying hundreds of MFCs, fully funded on their dime

These weren't hypothetical — they were negotiated proposals that stalled because Kroger's internal approval apparatus couldn't execute. The same bottlenecked people were in every conversation. Getting leadership alignment was the persistent blocker.

**The disintermediation trap.** The previous CEO's primary objection to marketplace partnerships was customer disintermediation — the fear that handing delivery to DoorDash or Instacart would erode Kroger's direct customer relationship. This concern was legitimate but ultimately paralyzing. Kroger spent billions trying to hold onto customer value it assumed it owned, while the market moved toward convenience and speed regardless.

---

## The Unwind (2025-2026)

**Ron's early wins (interim CEO):** Before Foran arrived, the interim leadership made several quick, well-received decisions:
- Closed underperforming CFCs
- Disbanded the Transformation Management Office (TMO)
- Closed non-core ventures (Kitchen 1883)

**Foran's pivot (February 2026 onward):** On Kroger's Q4 FY25 earnings call (March 6, 2026), Foran laid out a stores-first fulfillment strategy — using stores as hubs to get inventory closer to customers, reduce last-mile costs, and offer speed. The CFO confirmed plans to reduce e-commerce cost-to-serve by fulfilling from stores and leveraging third-party delivery providers.

**The financial impact:**
- $2.5 billion impairment charge on the Ocado-powered fulfillment network
- Florida CFC closure alone: ~$350M headwind to total sales in 2026
- Third-party delivery partnerships expected to deliver $1.5B+ in sales in 2026

**Remaining CFCs:** As of April 2026, remaining CFCs are being reviewed for performance. Not all dispositions have been decided. The standing assessment: you can't be half-in on Ocado. Full exit is the only viable path. The future is worse with any Ocado in it.

---

## Ocado's Side

Ocado Group's position has deteriorated significantly:
- Shares plunged ~20% on March 4, 2026, after FY26 tech revenue guidance of ~$640M came in far below the ~$775M consensus
- Stock fell a further 12% over the following two weeks (221.50p to 195.45p)
- 52-week range: 166p to 397p
- Sobeys also closed its Calgary CFC — the negative flywheel is confirmed across two continents

CEO Tim Steiner claims confidence in securing new U.S. deals post-Kroger. The market doesn't believe him. The negative flywheel thesis — partner departures deter new partners — is now operationally confirmed.

---

## Lessons

1. **Technology partnerships must be evaluated on economics, not on technological impressiveness.** Ocado's robotics worked. The business model around them didn't.

2. **Opportunity cost kills quietly.** While Kroger invested in CFCs, marketplace partners waited at the table with funded proposals. The cost wasn't just the $2.5B impairment — it was the years of marketplace momentum lost.

3. **The disintermediation fear was real but paralyzing.** The right response was a structured partnership framework with clear ownership and accountability — not blanket avoidance of external partners.

4. **Capital allocation paralysis amplifies bad bets.** During the interim CEO period, capital decisions were put to votes. It took six months to approve a single McKinsey engagement. A broken decision-making apparatus can't course-correct on a $2.5B commitment.

5. **Full exit is the only path.** Half-measures on Ocado (keeping some CFCs, maintaining the partnership in modified form) create ongoing distraction and stranded capital. The clean break — painful as it is — frees capital, attention, and organizational energy for the stores-first model.
