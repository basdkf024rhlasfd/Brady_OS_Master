# §10.2 — Investment Priorities (Year 1 vs. Years 2–3)

## Situation

Kroger has five simultaneous capital demands, all urgent:

1. **Store-based fulfillment.** The Ocado death sentence is store-based fulfillment's birth warrant. MFCs, in-store pick-and-pack infrastructure, last-mile logistics — none of this exists at scale yet.

2. **AI and technology.** Kroger's internal AI tools are firewalled, adoption is single-digit among associates, and KTD has 1,000+ people delivering too little too slowly. Building organizational AI capability (not just LLM chat) requires infrastructure investment, talent hiring, and process redesign simultaneously.

3. **Delivery partnership enablement.** The big pipeline of ideas from Instacart, DoorDash, and Uber exists. Execution is stalled. Enabling these partnerships requires technology integration (marketplace APIs, real-time inventory, order routing) and operational coordination that Kroger's current org structure can't deliver.

4. **Private label manufacturing.** Our Brands is Kroger's highest-margin product line and its strongest moat against Aldi. It has no dedicated manufacturing strategy, no supply chain redundancy, and no capital plan. This is Kroger's best lever against private label growth, and it's under-invested.

5. **Technology infrastructure modernization.** The tech stack is legacy. Cloud migration is incomplete. 84.51° is siloed. Real-time inventory visibility across store and online channels is a strategic dream, not operational reality.

All five are real. All five matter. Kroger has roughly $1.8B–$2.4B in annual Tier 2 capex to allocate across them. The complication is not money — it's deciding what gets priority, when.

## Complication

The temptation is to fund them equally. Committees love parity. "We'll invest in all five because they're all strategic." That's the thinking that produced the TMO mess — spreading money thin across too many initiatives, expecting velocity without accountability.

This gets worse under conditions of organizational stress. When capital allocation becomes a vote, the loudest voice wins. Whoever owns the biggest P&L, has the strongest political sponsor, or can phrase their initiative in the most compelling language gets funded first. Store operations (biggest footprint, clearest ROI) gets capex before technology (invisible, high-risk, long lead time). Digital partnerships get deferred because "we should focus on the core store experience." Private label gets perpetually underfunded because it's not a center of power in the org chart.

The second complication is sequencing risk. If you start private label manufacturing investment before you've stabilized store-based fulfillment, you're managing two transformation fronts simultaneously — and you'll botch both. If you invest in AI capability before you've solved the organizational accountability problem (which KTD demonstrates), you're building a tool nobody will use. If you enable delivery partnerships without completing technology integration, you're outsourcing fulfillment to a platform you can't control.

Foran's task is not to fund equally. It's to sequence ruthlessly.

## Resolution

**Firmly held belief:** Investment priority flows from strategic sequence. You don't fund five things equally; you fund one thing fully, then the next thing, and so on. Sequencing is the difference between transformation and sprawl.

**Loosely held opinions:** The specific funding levels within each tier adapt to market conditions and execution velocity, but the sequencing principle is non-negotiable.

**Year 1 (FY26–FY27): Store-Based Fulfillment is Tier 1 Strategic Capital**

Allocate $600M–$800M to store-based fulfillment infrastructure:
- MFC buildout (3–5 regional facilities): $300M–$400M
- In-store fulfillment technology (pick-pack systems, micro-fulfillment kits): $150M–$200M
- Last-mile logistics and delivery network: $150M–$200M

Why Year 1? Because it's the operational pivot that touches every element of Kroger's business. Stores are already assets. Inventory already exists. The fulfillment infrastructure gap is the single largest blocker to competing with Walmart on speed and convenience. Until store-based fulfillment is operationally sound, everything downstream (AI optimization, partnership enablement) is running against a broken foundation.

The secondary Year 1 allocation ($200M–$300M) goes to AI and data infrastructure capability-building:
- Chief AI Officer hiring and org building
- AI platform consolidation (breaking down KTD's siloes, integrating 84.51° data)
- Demand forecasting and pricing optimization pilots (tied directly to merchandising ROI)

AI is listed second, not because it's less important, but because it's an enabler of everything else. Store-based fulfillment won't reach full velocity without AI-optimized inventory allocation. Private label won't scale without demand forecasting. Delivery partnerships won't work without real-time data integration. So you invest in capability in Year 1 (hiring, infrastructure, pilots), and you let it create value for Year 2–3 initiatives.

**Years 2–3: Delivery Partnerships, Private Label, and Tech Modernization Split Tier 1 Strategic Capital**

Year 2 (FY27–FY28) splits capital between delivery enablement and private label:
- Delivery partnership tech integration (Instacart MFC proposal, DoorDash logistics, Uber Eats inventory): $300M–$400M
- Private label manufacturing and supply chain: $200M–$300M
- Technology infrastructure (cloud migration, 84.51° unification): $200M–$300M

Why this sequence? Because Year 1 will have proven store-based fulfillment works. Delivery partnerships will have clearer ROI math. And private label will have higher confidence that you're not building redundancy into a category where you're exiting.

Year 3 (FY28–FY29) completes the modernization and funds growth:
- Advanced automation (beyond store-based fulfillment): $300M–$400M
- Continued technology stack modernization: $200M–$300M
- Private label manufacturing expansion and margin extraction: $200M–$300M

**Decision Rules Within the Sequence:**

1. **Store-Based Fulfillment Gets the Runway.** Until three MFCs are operational and in-store fulfillment is capturing 25%+ of digital orders in pilot stores, delivery partnerships stay on hold (except for foundational Instacart MFC integration work that Instacart funds).

2. **AI Gets Funded, Not Just Piloted.** Kroger's history is pilot → pause → pilot again. Year 1 AI investment must include multi-year commitments. If the Chief AI Officer needs to hire a team of 40 data engineers and 20 ML practitioners over 18 months, that gets committed upfront. Half measures guarantee failure.

3. **Private Label Moves in Lock-Step with Store Performance.** New manufacturing capacity only gets funded after store-based fulfillment stabilizes. You can't build supply chain for products you can't distribute efficiently.

4. **Delivery Partnerships Are a Team Sport.** Instacart, DoorDash, and Uber will co-invest in their own tech integration if they get confidence that Kroger is serious. The capital question isn't "who builds the whole thing?" It's "what does Kroger need to own?" Instacart MFC investment? Mostly on Instacart. Real-time inventory APIs? Kroger's responsibility. Clearing that separation early (Year 1) saves capital and accelerates Year 2 deployment.

**Capital Reallocation Triggers:**

If store-based fulfillment deployment lags more than two quarters behind plan, pause Year 2 delivery partnership capex and reallocate $200M into store operations. If AI infrastructure hits critical milestones faster than expected (rare, but possible), accelerate technology modernization into Year 2 instead of Year 3. If a major divestiture closes (Harris Teeter, Mariano's, etc.), redirect proceeds into accelerated private label manufacturing rather than shareholder returns.

**Why This Sequencing Works:**

It's not about equal distribution. It's about momentum. Year 1 establishes that Kroger can execute a store-based model — no longer dependent on Ocado's vapor ware. Year 2 proves that execution scales to marketplace partners and brand products. Year 3 compounds the wins with automation and technology backbone.

By Year 3, if execution hits, Kroger's cost-to-serve on fulfillment has dropped 15–20%, private label penetration has grown 3–5 points, and AI is actively optimizing every price, promo, and placement decision. That's transformation. Spreading $1.8B across five initiatives simultaneously produces tinkering, not transformation.

The discipline is saying no. Kroger's capital freedom from Ocado is real. But real freedom means real choices, which means real constraints elsewhere. Foran's job is to make the sequence explicit and to hold the org accountable to it, quarter by quarter.
