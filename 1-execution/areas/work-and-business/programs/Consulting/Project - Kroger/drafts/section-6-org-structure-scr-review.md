## 6.1 Leadership Changes Needed

### Situation

Kroger's senior leadership team was built for a different era — one where the chief merchant administered promotions, the CTO managed systems, and e-commerce was a side project. Six leaders now sit in roles that no longer match the company's strategic needs.

- **Mary Ellen Adcock (Chief Merchant):** Operations background. Developed "Full, Fresh & Friendly" — a store ops program, not a merchandising strategy. Internal assessment: "decent operator, not chief merchant." She is fundamentally an operator who was elevated into a merchant role that requires category instinct, vendor negotiation leverage, and pricing strategy fluency she hasn't demonstrated.

- **Gabriel Arreaga (Supply Chain):** Competent in current scope but untested against the transformation ahead — a stores-first fulfillment model requires supply chain leadership that can think beyond traditional DC optimization.

- **Yael Cosset (CTO / E-Commerce):** E-commerce went back under his purview, but throughput remains poor. Has not materially changed his approach. Not making quick decisions. Not holding the org accountable.

- **Todd Kalmbach (CFO):** Solid financial operator, but capital allocation was paralyzed during the interim period — decisions were put to votes, and it took six months to approve a single McKinsey engagement.

- **Megan Schaefer (Head of Customer Strategy & Growth):** Reassigned from leading the now-disbanded TMO. Internal assessment: "former operator, not really a strategist." This is a role-person mismatch.

### Complication

The problem isn't that these leaders are bad at their jobs. It's that their jobs have changed — and the roles haven't been redesigned to match. A chief merchant who's actually an operator creates downstream dysfunction across every category, every vendor negotiation, every private label decision. A CTO who doesn't drive accountability means a 1,000+ person tech org that ships slowly and defaults to methodology purity (OKR/agile) over results.

These aren't individual performance issues. They're structural mismatches that compound across domains. The merch org dysfunction feeds into e-commerce dysfunction feeds into AI underadoption. You can't fix the org chart one role at a time — the entanglement requires a system-level view.

### Resolution

**Firmly held belief:** Leadership roles must be redesigned around the company's strategic direction, not around the people currently in them. The right structure attracts the right talent.

**Loosely held opinions (adapt as conditions clarify):**
- Adcock should be re-scoped to COO (store operations) — her actual strength. The chief merchant role needs a true merchant with external category experience.
- Cosset requires a forcing function: either demonstrate velocity within 90 days or transition to a technical advisory role while a new e-commerce operator takes the wheel.
- Schaefer's role should be evaluated for elimination or redefinition — "Customer Strategy & Growth" without a clear mandate is organizational camouflage.
- The specific reporting lines and title structures are tactical — they adapt to who Foran hires and how the org responds in the first 100 days.

## 6.2 Roles That Don't Exist Yet

### Situation

Kroger's org chart has no one accountable for the five capabilities that will determine the next decade:

1. **Chief AI Officer** — No executive owns AI strategy, talent, or infrastructure. AI tools exist but are firewalled, siloed, and used as chat interfaces — not as workflow infrastructure.
2. **VP Store-Based Fulfillment** — The pivot from Ocado CFCs to store-based fulfillment has no dedicated leader. This is the single largest operational shift in Kroger's modern history, and it reports to... nobody clear.
3. **Head of Delivery Partnerships** — DoorDash, Instacart, and Uber Eats represent a $1.5B+ sales channel. The "big pipeline of ideas" from these partners has produced little to no execution because the same bottlenecked people on the Kroger side can't convert pipeline to action.
4. **VP Private Label** — Our Brands is Kroger's highest-margin product line and its strongest competitive moat against Aldi. But it has no strategic owner — it's managed as a subset of merchandising, not as a standalone business.
5. **Transformation Office Lead** — The TMO was disbanded (rightly). But the organizational debris from a $2.5B pivot doesn't clean itself up. Someone needs to own the transition — sequencing, dependencies, accountability — without recreating the bureaucratic overhead the TMO became.

### Complication

These aren't nice-to-have roles. Each one represents a gap where strategic intent dies — because nobody wakes up in the morning accountable for it. The marketplace pipeline stalls because no one owns delivery partnerships end-to-end. AI adoption flatlines because no one owns the organizational capability (not just the tools). Store-based fulfillment drifts because the accountable leader doesn't exist yet.

The complication is that creating new roles without redesigning the existing ones produces overlap, turf wars, and the exact committee dynamics that paralyzed capital allocation during the interim period.

### Resolution

**Firmly held belief:** Every strategic priority needs a single accountable owner who wakes up every morning with that as their job. No committees. No shared accountability. One throat to choke.

**Loosely held opinions:** The exact titles, reporting lines, and hiring timelines are tactical. A Chief AI Officer might report to the CEO or the CTO — depends on Cosset's trajectory. The Transformation Office Lead might be internal or external — depends on trust levels in the first 90 days. The specific role definitions adapt. The principle — single ownership — does not.

## 6.3 Culture & Operating Cadence

### Situation

Kroger runs on institutional momentum, not on a deliberate operating cadence. There's no equivalent of Walmart's Legacy model — the weekly rhythm of store visits, Saturday morning meetings, and cascading accountability that turned a regional discounter into the world's largest retailer. Foran knows this cadence because he ran it at Walmart US for three years. He's already instinctively reaching for it — the "Legacy Projects" review is his first attempt to impose structure on what he inherited.

Meanwhile, KTD (Kroger Technology & Digital) runs a different operating system entirely: OKR purism and agile methodology as religion, not as tools. Leadership is "not self-aware about the fact that this approach has failed to produce enough velocity or the right strategic decisions." The business-tech gap is structural — business teams don't know how to participate in technology projects, and technology teams don't know how to prioritize business outcomes over methodology compliance.

### Complication

Foran's instinct to impose structure is right. But he's inheriting an org that just watched the TMO get created, fail, and get disbanded. People are "waiting with bated breath" to see if Legacy Projects is TMO 2.0. The complication: Foran needs to impose cadence without triggering organizational PTSD from the last time someone tried.

And the cadence problem isn't uniform. Store operations can absorb a Walmart-style weekly rhythm — that's what they know. KTD's 1,000+ people need a fundamentally different accountability model that breaks the OKR/agile purity trap without replacing it with something equally rigid. The org needs one operating cadence with domain-specific adaptations, not one size fits all.

### Resolution

**Firmly held belief:** Operating cadence is the difference between a strategy and a plan that sits in a drawer. Weekly rhythms, monthly accountability, quarterly recalibration — this is non-negotiable infrastructure. The cadence itself is a firmly held belief. It's what makes a command center a command center instead of a dashboard nobody looks at.

**Loosely held opinions:** The specific meeting structures, report formats, and escalation triggers are tactical. Whether the weekly review is Saturday morning (Walmart) or Monday morning (Kroger culture) — that's an opinion. Whether KTD gets OKR-style accountability or shifts to a different framework — that's an opinion. The principle — a deliberate operating cadence with teeth — is the belief.

Foran's Legacy Projects review is the right instinct. What he needs is a system underneath it: a governance model that converts his instincts into repeatable infrastructure that doesn't depend on his personal attention to every room. The command center runs the cadence. He reads the signals and makes the decisions.
