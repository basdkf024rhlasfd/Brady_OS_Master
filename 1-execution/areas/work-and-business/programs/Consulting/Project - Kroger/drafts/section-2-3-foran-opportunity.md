# §2.3 The Foran Opportunity: New CEO Window & Strategic Clarity

## Situation

Kroger hired its first external CEO in 143 years on February 9, 2026. Greg Foran, the operator who delivered 20 consecutive quarters of positive comp sales growth at Walmart US (2014–2019), took the job four months after stepping down from Air New Zealand. The market rewarded the pick instantly — KR jumped 7.7% on announcement day, closing at $72.67. Barclays called it the "best case scenario." Investors saw a signal they'd been waiting for: someone from outside the McMullen era, someone with no institutional loyalty to failed strategies, someone who had actually built a playbook at Walmart and proven it works.

But here's what matters: Foran arrived into a governance vacuum. For eleven months under interim CEO Ron Sargent (former Staples CEO), capital decisions were being put to *votes*. McKinsey wasn't approved until month six. The CFCs weren't being evaluated, they were being managed through a committee. The organization knew it needed to move — the Ocado impairment was baked in — but no one person was empowered to move. The interim period created the inverse of clarity: strategic drift disguised as caution.

Foran is now in month two. This is the window.

## Complication

The "first hundred days" is overheated rhetoric, but the underlying insight is real: a new external CEO has roughly 18 months before the organization develops scar tissue around his decisions. After that window closes, the past becomes prologue. Inertia builds. Constituencies harden. People who opposed a decision start defending it because they've been defending it. Changing course gets exponentially harder.

Foran understands this. At Walmart US, he remodeled 500+ stores, raised wages, and reset the employee experience before the organization could convince itself the investment wasn't worth it. He didn't ask for consensus — he asked for execution. Some senior people left. Many stayed and got results. The culture shifted because he acted decisively during the window when the organization was watching to see if he was serious.

At Kroger, the window is arguably wider than Foran's Walmart window because Kroger was leaderless for longer. The vacancy created hunger for clarity, not resistance to it. The Ocado writedown already happened — no one is defending that decision. The CFC closures are already announced — the debate isn't whether to do it, it's how fast to do it. The interim CEO left the table set. Foran just has to decide what meal to serve.

But the complication is structural. Kroger's organization, unlike Walmart's, is heavily unionized. The UFCW has significant voice in labor and capital decisions. Foran's Walmart reputation is "anti-union" — not because he was hostile, but because Walmart's culture defaulted to non-union structure. At Kroger, that dynamic inverts. His wage investments at Walmart are now table stakes. His store operations fixes are existential. And his ability to move quickly on labor, supply chain, and fulfillment strategy will be constrained by existing contracts and relationships.

Additionally, Kroger doesn't have the luxury of Walmart's tech stack. Walmart has spent a decade building artificial intelligence capabilities at scale — demand forecasting, pricing optimization, personalization, supply chain coordination. Kroger has internal AI tools that are heavily firewalled and used as chat interfaces, not transformative workflows. The gap is real. Foran can fix stores and people. He cannot fix technology velocity in 18 months. This is where outside expertise becomes non-negotiable.

## Resolution

The Foran opportunity is singular: he has the credibility to move fast enough to matter. Internally, the organization has been waiting for someone to make decisions. Externally, the market has priced in operational improvement — KR is trading near all-time highs on the expectation that he'll deliver. The margin band he's signaled (2–3 percentage point compression into lower prices and wage investment) is exactly the Walmart US playbook. Investors know how that movie ends.

But the leverage is temporal. The window is now, not later. If Foran spends the next two quarters deliberating on organizational structure, technology partnerships, and merchandising architecture, he loses momentum. If he decides and moves — accepting some execution risk in exchange for clarity and speed — the organization follows. This is not a time for consensus-building or extensive analysis. The analysis is done. The Ocado failure was the data point. The economics of store-based fulfillment vs. centralized warehouses are understood. The only unknown is whether Kroger's organization can move at the speed required to compete with Walmart's omnichannel advantage.

Foran's window extends through Q3 2026. By then, the market will expect to see tangible results: merchandise assortment shifts, technology partnerships formalized, fulfillment cost metrics improving, store condition upgrades underway. If those are visible, the window stays open through 2027. If they're not, the narrative shifts from "new CEO is executing" to "organization is resisting change." That shift is permanent.

This is where outside advisors add nonlinear value. Foran is an operator. He can move the organization. But he doesn't have the retail competitive intelligence, technology scouting, or organizational design expertise to move it toward the *right* target. The organization will follow his direction. The question is whether that direction accounts for what Walmart is actually doing — not what Kroger assumes Walmart is doing. Intel matters. Speed matters. But moving fast in the wrong direction is worse than moving slow in the right direction.

The Foran opportunity is real because the CEO window is real. It closes. The clock is running. And the difference between a 18-month-successful turnaround (profitable e-commerce, lower unit costs, tighter merchandise discipline) and a 18-month-failed repositioning (same structural problems, slower execution, eroded credibility) is whether the organization has the clarity, speed, and external perspective to move together toward the target.

That's the gap. That's where this engagement matters.
