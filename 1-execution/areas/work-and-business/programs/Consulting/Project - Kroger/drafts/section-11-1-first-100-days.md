# Section 11.1: First 100 Days — Quick Wins Across Seven Domains

## Situation
You're two months in. You've taken the Ocado write-down, disbanded the TMO, and signaled that capital gets allocated by decision-makers, not committee votes. The org sees movement. But you haven't yet proven you can move *fast* across multiple domains in parallel — and the window to set tone and momentum closes quickly. The first 100 days aren't about finishing transformations; they're about winning credibility and sealing the decisions that unlock everything else.

## Complication
Seven entangled domains have become disconnected. Merchandising is isolated from e-commerce. E-commerce can't execute because KTD (Kroger Technology & Digital) is still built on OKR theater, not velocity. Supply chain assumes a fulfillment network that no longer makes sense. Retail media has no data strategy underneath it. And your org structure still has people in chairs designed for the old Kroger, not the next one.

The complication: you can't fix all seven at once. But you can't pick just one either—they're dependent. Get the sequencing wrong, and you invest capital in the wrong network, hire for roles that will change, or waste momentum on initiatives that won't land. The first 100 days is where you prove you understand the dependencies and can move on them.

## Resolution: 100-Day Roadmap by Domain

### **Merchandising (Days 1–30)**
**Decision:** Lock pricing strategy and promo calendar for Q2/Q3.

- **Why first:** This is the fastest-moving lever. Pricing decisions don't require infrastructure; they only require conviction and decision rights. Getting this right immediately signals to the merchant org that you're not a consensus builder—you're a decision-maker.
- **What you do:** Meet with your Chief Merchant and the three largest category teams. Review current promotional effectiveness vs. Walmart. Make hard calls on 3–5 big categories where Kroger is vulnerable (likely: dairy, meat, produce). Issue new guidance by day 20.
- **Why it lands:** Sales results show up in weeks. Your merchant team sees accountability. Competitive response is immediate (from Walmart), confirming you picked the right fights. Momentum.

**Owner:** Chief Merchant. You own the decision, not the execution.

---

### **E-Commerce & Fulfillment (Days 30–50)**
**Decision:** Finalize the CFC disposition plan and lock the store-pickup + delivery partnership playbook.

- **Why after merchandising:** You need pricing locked first so you can model unit economics for fulfillment. A CFC doesn't make sense if margin is wrong.
- **What you do:** Directive: all remaining CFCs are reviewed by day 35, disposition decided by day 45. Parallel: final SKU-level analysis on which Instacart/DoorDash pickup combos are profitable. Lock partnership terms (SLAs, take-rates, incentives) with each player by day 50.
- **Why it lands:** You end the $2B+ annual ambiguity of half-Ocado, half-store. Wall Street stops asking "when's the Ocado exit?" and starts modeling cash. Instacart and DoorDash get clear SLAs and can commit marketing spend. Your supply chain team stops designing for a network that doesn't exist.

**Owner:** Chief Ecommerce Officer. You own go/no-go on partners.

---

### **AI & Technology (Days 50–70)**
**Decision:** Name your Chief AI Officer and lock the first-year AI roadmap (3–5 initiatives, not 30).

- **Why after e-commerce:** You now know which fulfillment partners you're using. AI roadmap priorities change based on that (Instacart's algorithm is different from your own MFC's). You also have pricing locked, so you can prioritize personalization and promotional AI that actually moves margin, not just traffic.
- **What you do:** Hire a Chief AI Officer by day 55 (external, not internal—you need someone who's shipped AI at scale). By day 70, they report back with: (1) SKU-level personalization engine, (2) supply chain optimization (via the partner APIs, not building your own network), (3) associate productivity tools (LLMs + agents, bottoming out KTD's current 20% adoption rate). Everything else is debt. Do not fund it.
- **Why it lands:** Your KTD team sees AI is suddenly strategic and laser-focused, not another reorg. Wall Street sees you're hiring to close the Walmart gap. The Chief AI Officer reports directly to you (not buried under KTD), ending the business-tech gap.

**Owner:** Chief AI Officer (new hire). You set the three priorities; they execute.

---

### **Org Structure & Talent (Days 70–90)**
**Decision:** Complete your leadership team, lock new role definitions, announce org structure.

- **Why after AI:** You've made decisions on merchandising, fulfillment, and technology. Now you know which org structure supports those decisions, not the ones you inherited. You're not shuffling chairs; you're designing for execution.
- **What you do:** Finalize your C-suite (Chief Merchant, Chief Ecommerce Officer, Chief AI Officer, Chief Supply Chain Officer). Three people exit (you know who). Six new roles open: Head of Retail Media, Head of Data & Analytics, VP of Automation (supply chain), VP of Organizational Performance (culture/cadence), Head of Alternative Revenue Streams. Announce by day 90.
- **Why it lands:** The org sees clarity on who's in and who's out. People self-select: talent wants to work for a decisive CEO with clear roles. Mediocrity leaves. Your three domain executives (Merch, Ecom, AI) can now build teams underneath them without ambiguity.

**Owner:** Chief People Officer. You own the decisions; they execute the rollout.

---

### **Portfolio & Banners (Days 90–100)**
**Decision:** Announce the banner strategy and kick off one small divestiture.

- **Why last:** You've proven you can move fast across your core operations. Portfolio moves signal strategic clarity without requiring operational execution in these first 100 days. Announce which banners are strategic (Fred Meyer, Harris Teeter, Smith's, Kroger) vs. which are in review (likely 1–2). Kick off a deep-dive on one non-core banner (likely QFC or a regional operator) by day 100.
- **What you do:** Op-ed in investor call: "We're a fortress in four banners, not a sprawl across eight." Pick one banner to analyze for potential sale (not a done deal—just public signal that you're ruthless on capital allocation). Activate the investment banking team by day 100.
- **Why it lands:** You're signaling that every asset has to earn its place. Wall Street sees you've thought about capital structure, not just operations. The workforce knows which banners have long-term funding.

**Owner:** Chief Financial Officer and Chief Operating Officer. You own the banner strategy; they manage stakeholder comms.

---

### **Supply Chain (Days 50–100, parallel to AI)**
**Decision:** Approve the distribution center network redesign and lock supplier relationship principles.

- **Why parallel to AI:** Supply chain moves more slowly than merchandising but faster than portfolio. You can kick this off mid-cycle while other decisions are locking.
- **What you do:** Directive: take all DC network recommendations from the McKinsey engagement and reduce to a 90-day action list (not a 2-year strategic study). Which DCs close? Which get invested in? Which supplier consolidations unlock margin? Decide and announce by day 100.
- **Why it lands:** Your supply chain team stops studying and starts building. Suppliers see Kroger has a plan. Your fulfillment partners (Instacart, DoorDash, your stores) get predictable logistics underneath them.

**Owner:** Chief Supply Chain Officer. You own go/no-go on major DCs; they execute network optimization.

---

### **Retail Media (Days 100, launching in Year 1)**
**Decision:** Fund Kroger Precision Marketing as standalone P&L, appoint VP.

- **Why last:** Retail media is strategic but doesn't require urgent operational changes. Lock the leadership and P&L structure by day 100; execution is Year 1.
- **What you do:** By day 100, you have a named VP of Retail Media with a standalone P&L target ($1B+ by Year 3). Commit $20–30M in FY26 for data infrastructure and advertising tech (either build or buy). This is capital allocation, not operational urgency.
- **Why it lands:** Your CPG suppliers see you're serious about alternative revenue. Wall Street models KPM as a profit lever (not just revenue fluff). Your data team knows it has a home.

**Owner:** VP of Retail Media. You fund; they execute against targets.

## The Command Center Model

**What you decide:** Pricing. Partner choices. C-suite hires. Banner strategy. Capital allocation.

**What the system handles underneath:** Operational execution, stakeholder comms, project management, daily accountability.

You're not running the merchant team or the fulfillment network. You're setting direction, making the big calls, and holding people accountable for execution. That's the difference between a decision-maker and a consensus builder. The first 100 days prove which one you are.
