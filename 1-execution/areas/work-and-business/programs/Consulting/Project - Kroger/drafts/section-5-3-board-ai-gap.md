# §5.3 — Board-Level AI Gap

## Situation

Kroger's board was recently augmented with Ashok Vemuri (CEO of Conduent, formerly president and CEO of IGATE, 14-year career at Infosys) to add tech-focused expertise. That's movement in the right direction. The board now brings fintech, operations, and technology perspective that wasn't there 18 months ago.

But genuine AI infrastructure expertise — the kind that understands agent orchestration, federated learning, agentic guardrails, retail-specific LLM training, deployment scale, and governance at 15,000-engineer scale — is a different credentialing. Vemuri has tech leadership and services company experience. He doesn't have the retail-AI-at-scale playbook.

Walmart's board includes operational AI leaders who've built systems (CTO Suresh Kumar, EVP of Global Technology Platforms Sravana Karnati). Their board can ask the right questions about agent consolidation timelines, model accuracy, developer productivity metrics. Kroger's board can ask whether the company is investing in AI, not whether it's investing in the right AI.

## Complication

Without board-level AI literacy, capital allocation decisions get deferred or diluted. McKinsey can advise on network strategy (they are, on-site as of March 2026). But AI infrastructure investment requires someone on the board who can push back on consultant advice, ask clarifying questions about vendor lock-in, and advocate for building versus buying.

Milen Mahadevan — chief data and AI officer, still leading 84.51° — has allocated significant capital for 2026. But without a board-level advocate who understands AI infrastructure complexity, that capital can get reallocated to other priorities during quarterly reviews. Walmart's board wouldn't tolerate a pause in developer AI investment because they understand the competitive cost of pausing.

This becomes a feedback loop: no board expertise → decisions get slower or misdirected → capital deployment lags → talent pipeline gaps worsen → competitive gap widens.

## Resolution

Recruit or elevate a board AI advisor (not necessarily a full director seat, but a standing advisor role):

**The profile:** Former engineering leader or infrastructure builder from Walmart, Shopify, Amazon, or Google who's built AI systems at scale and understands retail's data dynamics. Examples:
- Someone from Walmart Global Tech who's worked on agent orchestration or developer productivity infrastructure
- A retail e-commerce technology executive who's shipped AI features to millions of users
- A data scientist or ML engineer who's built retail-specific models (demand forecasting, pricing, inventory optimization)

**Mandate:**
1. **Quarterly AI infrastructure briefings to the board** — KPIs: developer AI tool adoption, forecast accuracy gains, infrastructure spending ROI, talent retention in AI/engineering.

2. **Vet major AI infrastructure decisions** — before Kroger spends $50M+ on new platforms, vendors, or models, this advisor assesses: build vs. buy, timeline realism, talent requirements, competitive positioning.

3. **Represent talent perspective** — AI infrastructure engineers want to work where they can see their impact. This advisor flags when Kroger's AI strategy is lagging peers and makes the case for urgency to the board.

4. **Gateway for Walmart talent recruitment** — when Kroger needs to recruit Pfaffenberger-caliber engineers, this advisor becomes the pitch ambassador. "Come lead developer AI at Kroger, and the CEO and board are committed to making this a top-three retail tech platform."

**Timeline:** Identify and onboard by month 2 of Foran's tenure. This person becomes part of the Thursday cabinet. This is not optional.

Board AI literacy doesn't guarantee faster decision-making. But it prevents wrong decisions. It keeps capital allocated to hard problems. And it sends a signal to engineers that Kroger's leadership understands what they're building.
