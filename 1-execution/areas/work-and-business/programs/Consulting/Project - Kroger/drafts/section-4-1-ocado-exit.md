# §4.1 — The Ocado Exit

## Situation

When Kroger committed to Ocado's robotic fulfillment center model in 2020, the partnership made sense at the moment. The bet was clear: centralized automation could outrun third-party delivery margins and protect customer lifetime value. Instead, the opposite happened. Ocado's technology proved slower to deploy and harder to operate than promised. The CFCs burned cash, required continuous capital infusions, and never moved fast enough to stay ahead of the market shift toward store-based fulfillment.

By March 2026, Kroger recognized reality and took a $2.5 billion impairment charge on the Ocado network. The Florida CFC closure alone created a $350 million headwind to 2026 sales. The message was unmistakable: the strategy had failed, and the exit had begun.

The numbers confirm the thesis. Ocado's stock has collapsed from 397p in late 2024 to under 200p today—a loss of 50% of market value. The company reported a £377.6 million loss before tax in FY25, missed tech revenue guidance by 17%, and is now targeting cash flow positivity in H2 2026 only through aggressive cost-cutting (1,000 job reductions, £150 million in savings). Ocado is not a thriving partner. It's a wounded business in free fall, burning cash and losing leverage by the quarter.

## Complication

The temptation now is containment—to keep some CFCs limping along, honor residual commitments, and avoid the narrative of full strategic reversal. This is exactly wrong.

Partial exits create three cascading problems. First, they tie up capital and management attention on legacy infrastructure that has no path to profitability. Every quarter Kroger maintains an Ocado CFC is a quarter of missed investment in store-based fulfillment and marketplace partnerships that are actually working. Second, partial commitment signals internal confusion about direction. If Ocado is truly a bad bet, the organization needs to see full closure, not half measures. Ambiguity paralyzes execution. Third, partial exits preserve legal and contractual exposure. Keeping relationships alive keeps lawsuits alive. Kroger has already been sued by Ocado. The company will continue fighting over whether contractual obligations were met, what constitutes good-faith deployment, and how quickly facilities can be wound down. Every CFC still operating is a point of contention.

Ocado's collapsing financial position creates a narrow window. The company is approaching the threshold where it must choose between survival and conflict. Ocado will likely face additional lawsuits from other partners (Sobeys also closed its Calgary CFC). Bankruptcy may arrive faster than expected. Kroger can either settle on favorable terms while Ocado still has negotiating power, or fight a protracted battle with a company in judicial receivership—which buys leverage but costs time.

## Resolution

The only path is full exit. Every CFC comes offline. Every contract obligation is addressed through settlement, not continued operation. Every final payment is treated as the cost of liberation.

Here's why this is the right move:

**One. Clarity.** "Ocado is over" is a statement that reverberates through the organization. It kills the debate about centralized automation. It stops the internal political fights about whether the CFC network could still be salvaged with better management. It removes ambiguity from the capital allocation process. When the CEO says "we are store-based, period," people move.

**Two. Pace.** Full exit allows Kroger to announce closure schedules, retain remaining talent, and manage the wind-down cleanly. Partial exit drags it out, erodes morale, and creates uncertainty that leaks into operations. Stores running Ocado fulfillment in 2027 will still be underperforming 2025 baselines.

**Three. Leverage against Ocado.** Bankruptcy courts favor debtors, not creditors. Kroger's best negotiating position is now, while Ocado is still solvent enough to negotiate. A full exit demand is cleaner to litigate than a partial one—either the contract lets you exit or it doesn't. Prolonged negotiation over which CFCs stay open plays into Ocado's hands because it forces continued operational engagement.

**Four. Capital freedom.** The $2.5 billion charge is already taken. The write-down is done. What remains is execution cost—decommissioning facilities, severance, contract settlement. Once that's sunk, Kroger's capital is genuinely free to redeploy to store-based fulfillment, MFCs, and marketplace partnerships. Partial exit means partial capital freedom, which is capital no one uses effectively.

**Five. Market signal.** Ocado's remaining prospects depend entirely on new partner commitments. Kroger's full exit accelerates Ocado's market position deterioration, which may paradoxically speed its willingness to settle quickly rather than litigate. A wounded competitor often settles faster than a stable one. Fight now, negotiate harder, exit sooner.

Kroger's leadership should approach Ocado's counterparts with a simple message: "Full exit is happening. We can do this as a negotiated settlement or through litigation. Settlement costs less for both of us, closes faster, and lets you redeploy your own capital to realistic markets. We're open to the first conversation." If Ocado refuses, Kroger's next move is the courtroom—not with anger, but with clarity.

The CFCs themselves become real estate. Some may have salvage value. Others become dark sites. All become someone else's problem on the other side of closure.

Ocado is behind you now. Act like it.
