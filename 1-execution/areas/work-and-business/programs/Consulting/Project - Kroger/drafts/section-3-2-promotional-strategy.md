# Section 3.2: Promotional Strategy — Spend Effectiveness & Rationalization

---

## Situation

Kroger spends more on promotions per transaction than any competitor of comparable format, yet sees weaker traffic lift and loyalty impact. Promotional spending has become structural and invisible — it's baked into the cost base, accepted as "the cost of doing business in grocery." The organization doesn't interrogate it anymore. Promotional calendar is complex and reactive: weekly feature pricing, monthly digital coupon pushes, seasonal events, loyalty tier escalations, supplier co-funds with terms nobody understands. The result is margin leakage without corresponding traffic gains.

The data is damning. When shoppers cherry-pick promotions, they reduce basket size. A customer getting a loss-leader deal on Frito-Lay doesn't suddenly buy pasta sauce at full price — they optimize their shopping trip around the sale. Kroger's promotional mechanics are optimized to bring customers in, not to drive full baskets. Meanwhile, Walmart proves that EDLP + selective promotional events (rollbacks, clearance) drives both traffic and margin. Costco shows that membership + price credibility requires almost no promotional mechanics. Aldi runs primarily on everyday price, with rare promotional events.

Internally, the promotional complexity creates operational drag. Merchants negotiate with suppliers for promotional funding but lack the discipline to say no to low-ROI deals. Category teams manage overlapping promotional mechanics with no enterprise visibility into cumulative impact. The e-commerce platform can't execute complicated promotional mechanics reliably — substitutions, promo calendar crossovers, and "price as promised" failures directly trace to promotional architecture complexity. Simplify the mechanics, and you solve a downstream e-commerce problem.

---

## Complication

The core complication is that promotional spending is treated as a revenue defense mechanism rather than a margin optimization lever. When household count declines, the instinct is to increase promotional intensity — deeper discounts, more frequent deals, higher coupon values. This compounds margin pressure and creates a flywheel where volume is sacrificed to drive traffic. The organization doesn't have visibility into the ROI of promotional spending by channel, category, or supplier arrangement because the mechanics are too complex to measure cleanly.

Second, promotional negotiations with suppliers have deteriorated into unstructured requests for "trade funding." Merchants say, "I need $500K in promotional support for Q3," suppliers say yes, and the funding gets applied to whatever mechanic happens to be easiest. There's no discipline around which promotions should be supplier-funded (brand-building, new products, joint assortment expansion) vs. which should be on Kroger's dime (competitive response, inventory clearance, loyalty acquisition). Suppliers have figured this out — they'll fund anything to get placement. Kroger has lost leverage.

Third, promotional calendar complexity creates customer perception problems. Customers don't know what the everyday price is, so they don't know if a deal is actually a deal. Loyalty tier benefits overlap with weekly promotions, which overlap with digital coupons, which overlap with supplier promotional mechanics. Customers see 30% off, but it's 30% off a jacked-up regular price, or it requires a membership tier, or it's only valid in-store, or substitutions break the deal. Confusion erodes loyalty.

---

## Resolution

**Firmly held belief:** Promotional effectiveness requires ruthless simplicity. You can't optimize what you can't measure. Rationalize the mechanics, and you unlock both margin recovery and data visibility that enables smarter promotional strategy going forward.

**Loosely held opinions:** The exact promotional mechanics — which categories run weekly features, which run monthly deals, which run loyalty-exclusive offers — is tactical and should be grounded in category ROI analysis. The principle — simplicity, measurability, accountability — is not.

### Three-tier promotional rationalization:

**Tier 1: Eliminate low-ROI promotions (immediate, 6-month payback)**
- Audit promotional calendar for the past 24 months. Identify promotions where lift was <10% and/or margin loss exceeded value of incremental volume. Kill them.
- Eliminate overlapping mechanics (e.g., if digital coupons and loyalty offers are both running on the same category the same week, consolidate to one).
- Cancel promotional mechanics that e-commerce can't execute reliably — reduce "price as promised" failures by de-complicating the promotional calendar.
- Estimated impact: 150-200 bps margin recovery from promotional rationalization alone (based on industry benchmarks where over-promotional spend runs 30-50% of promo budget with <5% ROI).

**Tier 2: Restructure supplier promotional funding (6-12 months)**
- Establish a discipline for what Kroger-funds vs. supplier-funds. Supplier funding should be reserved for strategic initiatives: new products, brand-building, joint assortment expansion, competitive response to named competitor in named market.
- Eliminate open-ended "trade funding" requests. Replace with category business plans that specify promotional strategy, expected ROI, and measurement framework.
- Move supplier funding discussions upstream — from merchant to Chief Merchant + supplier relationship team. Professionalize the negotiation.
- Renegotiate supplier contracts to reflect power balance: Kroger has scale, suppliers need placement. Promotional funding should flow to Kroger's strategic priorities, not to merchant convenience.

**Tier 3: Establish promotional governance with measurement (ongoing)**
- Monthly promotional effectiveness review: lift by category, margin impact, customer perception (via loyalty data), traffic lift per mechanic.
- Quarterly supplier promotional ROI audit: which suppliers deliver highest-ROI promotional funding, which are dead weight?
- Annual promotional strategy reset: based on the prior year's effectiveness data, set strategic promotional calendar for the year ahead. Limit variance to competitive response only (ad hoc).
- Assign accountability: Chief Merchant owns promotional strategy and measurement. Category merchants own category-level promotional ROI. Suppliers own supplier-funded promotion ROI.

### Secondary benefits (beyond margin recovery):

- **E-commerce execution clarity:** Simpler promotional mechanics = fewer substitution failures = higher "price as promised" scores = customer loyalty lift.
- **Data credibility:** Clean promotional data enables AI-driven promotional optimization (personalized offers, optimal timing, price elasticity modeling) — capabilities Walmart is already deploying.
- **Merchant decision quality:** Without promotional complexity, merchants can actually think about assortment, pricing, and customer value instead of deal mechanics.
- **Supply chain efficiency:** Supplier relationships can shift from "fund my promo calendar" to "help me build efficient supply chains for your products." Lower trade terms, better service levels.

### Measurement framework:

For each promotional mechanic, track:
- **Incremental volume** (lift vs. baseline, not total volume)
- **Incremental margin** (lift revenue minus promotion cost, divided by baseline margin)
- **Customer perception** (via Boost data: did this promotion drive loyalty growth, or just transaction capture?)
- **E-commerce impact** (failures due to promotional complexity, customer satisfaction on deals)

When promotional ROI falls below a threshold (suggest 50% incremental margin recovery), the mechanic gets eliminated or restructured.

**The point:** Promotional spend is invisible because it's complex. Simplify it, measure it, and it becomes leverage — either to improve margin or to unlock reinvestment in the capabilities Walmart and Costco are building. Right now, Kroger is spending promotional dollars without seeing the upside.
