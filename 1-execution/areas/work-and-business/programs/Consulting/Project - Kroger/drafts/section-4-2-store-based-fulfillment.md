# §4.2 — Store-Based Fulfillment Model

## Situation

Kroger's pivot to store-based fulfillment is not theoretical. It's operational reality. Pick-from-store, micro-fulfillment centers (MFCs), and married fulfillment (some inventory in stores, some in dedicated MFCs) are now the working model across the network. The 2026 guidance reflects this shift: Kroger expects over $1.5 billion in sales from third-party delivery partnerships, with the cost-to-serve dropping as fulfillment shifts from centralized CFCs to distributed store locations.

Walmart has already proven the model works at scale. Two-thirds of Walmart's stores are now supported by some form of automation. About 55% of fulfillment center volume moves through automated facilities. Walmart's five new perishable distribution centers—Shafter, Lancaster, Wellford, Belvidere, and a new Stockton facility coming online in 2026—are designed to support grocery delivery, not replace store-based picking.

Albertsons is three automation facilities in at 22 dedicated distribution centers, with more scheduled. The company has driven e-commerce to represent 7% of grocery revenue. The industry has moved. The question is not whether store-based fulfillment works. The question is how fast Kroger can scale it and optimize it.

## Complication

Store-based fulfillment requires three changes that Kroger is only halfway through executing.

**First, physical design.** Stores are built for customer browsing, not batch picking. Aisles are too narrow, sections are too dense, and picking zones overlap with shopping traffic. Remodeling stores to accommodate picking zones, staging areas for third-party fulfillment, and customer pickup lockers costs money and takes time. Some stores can be retrofitted cheaply. Others will need capital investment. Kroger has started this work, but the scale and pace are not yet clear. How many stores need remodel? What's the cost per store? What's the timeline to get 70% of the network ready for efficient store-based fulfillment?

**Second, inventory positioning.** Store inventory is designed for sell-through velocity, not for reserve stock to fulfill delivery orders. If a store runs out of a SKU, the customer buys it elsewhere—no big deal in a 40,000-SKU assortment. But if that SKU is promised to a delivery order, the store breaks the promise or sacrifices margin by buying it back from a distributor. Kroger's supply chain teams need to know, in real time, what inventory is available for delivery vs. reserved for in-store sale. This is not a new problem, but it's not fully solved yet. Most retailers manage it with educated guesses and manual holds. Best-in-class retailers use AI demand forecasting and dynamic inventory allocation. Kroger is somewhere in the middle.

**Third, labor productivity.** Picking a customer's order from a retail shelf is slower and more error-prone than picking from a dedicated MFC or reserve section. Walmart and Albertsons have both invested in in-store automation—conveyor systems, sorters, pick-to-light technology—to speed the process and reduce errors. Kroger has not. Instead, Kroger is using human shoppers, which works but is labor-intensive and variable. As volume grows, labor costs will balloon unless productivity improves. This is where MFCs fit back in—not as competitor to stores, but as complement to them. An MFC lets Kroger pick, sort, and prepare orders offline, reducing the labor burden on stores and speeding delivery.

## Resolution

Store-based fulfillment at Kroger requires a three-part architecture, sequenced over 24 months:

**Phase 1: Retrofit key stores (Months 1-12).** Identify the 400-600 stores with highest delivery order density. Invest in physical remodeling: dedicated picking zones, staging areas, customer pickup lockers, and labor routing software to guide pickers without disrupting customer shopping. Target store formats: supermarkets in urban and suburban high-density areas. Skip small-format stores initially.

**Phase 2: Deploy marketplace fulfillment MFCs (Months 6-18).** Complement store-based picking with small MFCs (20,000-30,000 sq ft) positioned 5-15 miles from clusters of high-volume stores. MFCs handle the orders that stores cannot easily fulfill due to distance, bulk, or complexity. They also serve as backup capacity during peak demand (holiday, promotional events). Target MFC density: 1 MFC per 10-12 stores in metro areas, fewer in rural markets. Ownership model: Build MFCs as Kroger-owned and operated assets. Do not outsource this. Kroger needs operational control to coordinate with stores and third-party delivery partners.

**Phase 3: Inventory optimization via AI (Months 12-24).** Deploy dynamic inventory allocation software to all retrofitted stores and MFCs. This system predicts demand by channel (in-store, delivery, pickup), allocates stock accordingly in real time, and alerts pickers when items are low. The system talks to stores, logistics, and supply chain. It becomes the nervous system that makes the distributed model work without breaking freshness or losing sales.

**Capacity planning metrics:**
- Store-based fulfillment: target 60% of delivery volume from retrofitted stores by end of 2027
- MFC capacity: 100-150K sq ft of total MFC capacity across the network by end of 2027
- Pickup time: under 1 hour for store-based orders (vs. typical 2-3 hours today)
- Error rate: below 1% (defects, missing items, substitutions)
- Labor productivity: picking rate of 80-120 items per labor hour from stores (vs. 150-200 from MFCs)

This is not revolutionary. Walmart is already here. Amazon is moving here. But Kroger can execute better than both if it combines store density with disciplined MFC placement and real-time inventory coordination. The market is giving Kroger a window—Ocado's exit frees up capital, and the third-party delivery partnership pipeline is ready to absorb volume. The organization needs to move now.

Store-based fulfillment is your cost engine. Get the model right, and your last-mile economics collapse in your favor.
