# §10.3 — Shareholder Return Framework (Buyback vs. Dividend vs. Reinvestment)

## Situation

Kroger's shareholder return machine is running hot. The company has paid dividends for 20 consecutive years with annual increases for the last decade, currently at $1.40/share ($0.35 quarterly, 9% increase in FY26). Share buybacks total $1.4B annually in recent years, with a $5 billion accelerated repurchase program completed in Q3 FY25 and an additional $2 billion authorization added in December 2025. The payout ratio sits at 87% — aggressive, disciplined, and unsustainable if growth stalls.

Here's what this means: Kroger returned roughly $2.9B to shareholders in FY25 (dividends + buybacks) from a company generating approximately $8B in free cash flow after capex. That leaves $5.1B for reinvestment into growth, debt reduction, or additional capex beyond the $3.8B guidance. On paper, the capital structure looks balanced. In practice, it's rigid.

The dividend commitment is the stiffener. A 20-year streak of increases creates investor expectations that are hard to break. Missing a dividend increase or (worse) cutting the dividend triggers a market perception of distress — even if the cut is strategic. The buyback, by contrast, is discretionary. Kroger suspended buybacks during the Ocado uncertainty and ramped them back up aggressively once clarity returned.

## Complication

Here's the tension: Kroger's dividend is now so large that it leaves little room for error on transformation capex without either (a) cutting the dividend, (b) taking on more debt, or (c) slowing transformation investment.

Walmart solved this by growing earnings faster than they return capital. Operating profit growth outpaces dividend growth, and buybacks fill whatever gap remains. Walmart's dividend payout ratio is roughly 40% — half of Kroger's — leaving ample room for reinvestment without raising debt significantly.

Kroger is different. Operating profit growth has been choppy (hindered by Ocado, freight volatility, and private label pressure from Aldi). Dividend growth has been linear and consistent. The math breaks.

There's a second complication: the board's belief in "consistent" shareholder returns has been used to justify committee voting on capex. The logic goes: "We have to return $2.9B to shareholders annually, so capex gets what's left over after debt service." But that's backward. You should return capital only after strategic reinvestment is fully funded. Instead, Kroger has allowed the dividend commitment to anchor the conversation, then squeezed capex around it.

Third complication: buybacks are increasingly controversial. Reducing share count while paying high execution prices on shares is mathematically sound (EPS accretion), but politically fragile. If Kroger cuts capex to maintain buybacks and then stumbles on execution, investors will have justified complaints.

## Resolution

**Firmly held belief:** Shareholder returns are the residual, not the primary, claim on free cash flow. Reinvestment in transformation is non-negotiable. Returns come after transformation is funded.

**Loosely held opinions:** The specific mix between dividend and buyback adapts to conditions, but the ordering principle is immovable.

Here's the framework:

**Tier 1: Transformation Capex (Non-Negotiable)**

Fund Tier 1 and Tier 2 capex as outlined in §10.2 at full guidance ($3.6B–$3.8B annually). This is the before anything else gets capital.

**Tier 2: Debt Reduction (Guardrail)**

Maintain debt-to-EBITDA between 2.0x and 2.3x. If leverage creeps above 2.3x due to EBITDA pressure or integration costs, debt reduction takes priority over shareholder returns. If leverage drops below 2.0x, that's a signal you're returning too much capital and should reinvest or acquire.

Current leverage is approximately 2.3x. This gives Kroger limited room to take on incremental debt for transformation. If transformation requires $4.5B capex in a single year (due to accelerated MFC buildout or a strategic acquisition), debt temporarily rises to 2.4x–2.5x. That's acceptable. Sustained leverage above 2.5x is not.

**Tier 3: Shareholder Returns (Residual)**

After Tiers 1 and 2 are funded, remaining free cash flow splits 60% dividend / 40% buyback:

- **Dividend:** Maintain the current trajectory of annual increases (roughly 9% annually). The 20-year streak is valuable brand equity. But increases should not exceed earnings growth by more than 200 basis points. If EPS grows 8%, the dividend can grow up to 10%. If EPS grows 4%, dividend growth caps at 6%. This decouples dividends from arbitrary annual increases and ties them to earnings reality.

- **Buyback:** Allocate the remaining 40% to opportunistic buybacks. Not mechanical buyback-every-quarter. Opportunistic means: (a) only buy when the stock is trading below intrinsic value, and (b) pause if capex acceleration or debt reduction becomes a priority. Current authorization of $2.9B provides roughly 2 years of buyback capacity at current levels. That's enough runway to remain disciplined.

**Rebalancing Triggers:**

1. **If transformation capex exceeds guidance by >10% in a single year:** Pause buybacks entirely. Maintain the dividend (the 20-year streak is sacred), and redeploy buyback allocation to debt reduction until leverage is back to 2.1x–2.2x.

2. **If EBITDA growth stalls (YoY growth <3%):** Freeze dividend growth, but maintain the absolute dollar amount. This preserves the streak while acknowledging earnings pressure. Redirect all buyback allocation to capex or debt reduction.

3. **If a strategic acquisition or divestiture closes:** Reevaluate leverage immediately. Proceeds from divestitures (banner closures, non-core format exits) should feed transformation capex first, debt reduction second, and shareholder returns third. Acquisitions trigger a 12-month reassessment of leverage target before resuming full buyback authorization.

4. **If AI transformation or delivery partnerships prove more capital-intensive than modeled:** Foran has discretion to cut buybacks by 50% without triggering messaging around distress. Frame it as "accelerating strategic reinvestment." Investors will accept it.

**Why This Framework Works:**

It gives Foran optionality without painting himself into a corner. The dividend is protected — the 20-year streak continues, and investors get the income they expect. But the framework makes clear that dividends grow from earnings, not from inertia. Buybacks remain available as a capital deployment tool, but they're not entitlements. If transformation capital needs exceed plan, buybacks go first.

This is a framework that works whether earnings grow 8% or 4%, whether capex hits $3.8B or $4.5B, and whether leverage stays at 2.3x or needs to go to 2.5x temporarily. The principles are fixed. The numbers are flexible.

**The Competitive Perspective:**

Walmart's dividend payout ratio is 40%. Costco's is roughly 35%. Kroger's at 87% is unsustainably aggressive for a company in transformation. But cutting dividends would trigger a massive market reaction — which is why this framework keeps the streak alive while gradually right-sizing the payout ratio through earnings growth, not dividend cuts.

If Kroger can grow operating profit 8–10% annually over the next three years (realistic with store-based fulfillment, AI optimization, and private label growth), the payout ratio naturally declines to 75%, then 70%, then 65% as the dividend dollar amount grows slowly while earnings grow faster. That's the endgame. No cuts. Just growth that outpaces returns.

**For Foran's First Day:**

He should communicate this framework to the board within 30 days. The message: "We're committed to the dividend streak and competitive shareholder returns. We're also committed to transformation capex that will drive earnings growth faster than returns. The framework above shows how we do both. Capital decisions will be made against this framework, not against votes or parity. If earnings exceed plan, returns increase. If earnings fall short, returns adjust downward. That's how we stay disciplined."

Investors will reward this clarity. Committees will fight it. But clarity wins in the long run.
