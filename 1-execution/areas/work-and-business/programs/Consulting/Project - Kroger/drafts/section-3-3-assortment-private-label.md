# Section 3.3: Assortment & Private Label — Category Management & Our Brands as Strategic Weapon

---

## Situation

Kroger's Our Brands portfolio is a $30 billion business that operates like a cost-reduction tactic, not a strategic advantage. The company has built a multi-tier private label system (Simple Truth for premium, mid-tier store brands, Our Basics for value) that reaches 90%+ of households, but it's organized as a subset of merchandising, not as a standalone business unit with its own product development, supply chain, and brand strategy.

The portfolio is real. Over 13,000 SKUs, 4.9% sales growth (above national brands), and margins running 200-400 bps ahead of branded products. Private Selection and Simple Truth are outpacing the portfolio. The company added 900 new items in fiscal 2024, with 370 fresh items signaling category-specific investment. But the organizational structure fragments ownership: product development scattered across category teams, sourcing owned by supply chain, brand strategy and packaging owned by marketing. Nobody wakes up in the morning accountable for Our Brands as a unified business.

Meanwhile, the competitive moat against Aldi depends entirely on private label. Aldi has two tiers (premium/value) with ruthless simplification — 1,500 SKUs total vs. Kroger's 13,000. Costco's private label (Kirkland) is iconic and generates disproportionate margin. Our Brands should be Kroger's equivalent — the difference between competing on branded commodity prices (where Walmart wins) and competing on differentiated private value (where margin and loyalty both live). It's not being deployed that way.

Category management — the discipline of understanding what should be on shelf, in what configuration, at what price — is underpowered relative to the complexity Kroger faces. Centralized ~5 years ago, the function has the right structure but not the right talent or data tooling. Merchants think like promo administrators, not like category strategists who understand assortment design, shelf design, and how customer buying patterns drive planogram decisions. The result: category performance drifts on inertia rather than strategy.

---

## Complication

The complication is that private label optimization and category management excellence require investment in capabilities that don't exist yet. Our Brands needs product development depth (sourcing new items, quality control, supplier relationship management) that's currently distributed across a dozen category teams with competing priorities. Category management needs data discipline (demand forecasting, competitive pricing intelligence, shelf design analytics, assortment optimization) that's siloed between 84.51° and merchant spreadsheets with no unified view.

Second, the organizational incentive structure works against private label growth. Merchants are evaluated on promotional lift and transaction growth, not on mix shift toward higher-margin private products. In fact, private label growth often comes at the expense of branded product sales, which creates tension with supplier relationships (branded suppliers view private label as competitive threat). If the company wants Our Brands to grow, it needs to restructure incentives so merchants are rewarded for building private label share within category, not for protecting branded supplier shelf space.

Third, category management sophistication in grocery requires a specific skillset that Kroger's current talent doesn't have: understanding of supply chain complexity (how shelf design impacts inventory turns), pricing elasticity (how assortment and price interact), and customer buying patterns (how planogram design drives trip basket). These are learned skills, not intuitive. Building this capability requires hiring or training people who think differently than the current merch organization.

---

## Resolution

**Firmly held belief:** Private label is Kroger's most defensible competitive advantage against both low-price discounters (where branded commodity competition is brutal) and against Walmart (where supply chain is Walmart's moat). Our Brands should be structured as a standalone business with full P&L accountability.

**Loosely held opinions:** Whether that business reports to the Chief Merchant, the Chief Operating Officer, or directly to the CEO is tactical. The principle — singular ownership, clear P&L, dedicated talent, product development strategy — is non-negotiable.

### Path 1: Establish VP Private Label (3-6 month setup)

Create a standalone VP-level role accountable for:
- **Product development strategy:** Which categories expand, which are rationalized, which launch premium tiers.
- **Supplier relationships:** Direct negotiations with private label manufacturers, quality control, cost management.
- **Brand strategy:** Visual identity, packaging, shelf position, brand positioning within category (premium vs. value).
- **Marketing and promotion:** How Our Brands are featured in loyalty, digital, and in-store.

This role has P&L accountability: Our Brands sales growth, margin maintenance, household penetration, repeat purchase rate. The VP reports to the Chief Merchant (merch alignment) or Chief Operating Officer (supply chain alignment) — either works, as long as it's not a dotted line to three different people.

**Organizational impact:** This role consolidates product development, sourcing, and marketing accountability that currently sits across 4-5 different functions. It's a new seat at the table, not a sibling rivalry with existing merchants.

---

### Path 2: Restructure category management around Our Brands growth (6-12 months)

Category merchants move from "manage all products in my category" to "manage category economics with Our Brands as strategic priority."

**Specific changes:**
- Assortment strategy per category: Branded products required to drive traffic, valued brands that show private label vulnerability, and the private label tier penetration target.
- Planogram governance: Merchants design planograms with category management discipline (facings, vertical space, checkout exposure) informed by assortment analytics. This isn't intuitive. Category managers and merchants pair-code planograms like software engineers — one designs, one reviews.
- Incentive shift: Merchant bonuses tied to Our Brands mix (% of category sales), not just total category growth. If a merchant grows category sales 5% but private label is flat, they missed the goal.
- Supplier management: Branded supplier agreements explicitly carve out space/support for Our Brands. Private label shouldn't be a surprise competitive threat to branded partners — it's a known part of the deal.

**Data enablement:** Category managers (different from merchants — broader analytic role) own assortment analytics: demand forecasting, price elasticity, planogram optimization. They feed merchants insights, merchants execute on shelf. This requires 84.51° integration (not a separate system, but unified analytics infrastructure) and new hires with category science backgrounds.

---

### Path 3: Private label product expansion strategy (ongoing)

The company has momentum (13,000 SKUs, 4.9% growth, 900+ new items last year). The strategy going forward should be disciplined:

**Premium tier (Simple Truth, Private Selection):**
- Pursue category-by-category where margin is high and brand loyalty is defensible (organic, natural, specialty).
- Example: Simple Truth organic + natural vs. national organic brands — margin gap is real, customer preference is real, growth is sustainable.
- Investment: higher sourcing complexity, packaging quality, supply chain reliability.

**Mid-tier (store brands):**
- Focus on categories where our cost advantage is clear and customer is price-conscious but quality-conscious.
- Example: Kroger rotisserie chicken vs. competitor rotisserie — same quality expectation, margin difference comes from supply chain efficiency.
- Investment: supply chain quality and consistency, not brand boutique-ness.

**Value tier (Our Basics):**
- Reserve for categories where discounters (Aldi, Dollar General private label) are eating our traffic.
- Not about undercutting price, but about closing the gap so customers don't defect to Aldi for value categories.
- Investment: efficient sourcing, packaging efficiency, inventory turns (limited SKU variety).

**SKU rationalization:** 13,000 private label SKUs is rich. Over time, consolidate within tier: one version of olive oil per tier, not five. Better margins, simpler supply chain, clearer shelf positioning.

---

### Secondary organizational implications:

If a VP Private Label role is created, it cascades into two supporting functions:

**1. Category Management (enhanced):**
- Currently light. Should grow 2-3x in depth and rigor.
- Owners of assortment science, planogram design, demand forecasting.
- Supply both merchants and private label function with category intelligence.

**2. Supply Chain (private label sourcing):**
- Currently part of general procurement. Needs dedicated team for private label sourcing.
- Responsible for supplier diversity, cost management, quality control, innovation (new product sourcing).
- Reports to VP Private Label, aligned with overall supply chain strategy.

---

### Measurement framework:

- **Our Brands mix %:** Percentage of category sales (target: industry-leading per tier)
- **Repeat purchase rate:** Household loyalty to specific Our Brands products
- **Margin contribution:** Absolute margin dollars from Our Brands (should increase as mix grows)
- **Supplier win rate:** When Kroger launches Our Brands competitor to national brand, what % of customers switch or reduce branded purchases?
- **Customer perception:** Brand health scores for Simple Truth, Private Selection (loyalty data)

---

## Why this matters for the broader merch strategy:

Private label success hinges on the broader merchandising architecture: pricing discipline, promotional rationalization, and category management excellence. A VP Private Label without pricing architecture discipline will just build another promotional tactic. Without category management rigor, the product portfolio will remain reactive. This section is simultaneously standalone strategy and integration point with sections 3.1 and 3.2.

**The point:** Our Brands is Kroger's path to margin and loyalty growth that doesn't depend on Walmart's supply chain or Aldi's low-cost model. It's a defensible moat if it's owned as a business, not managed as a cost reduction. Right now, it's being left on the table.
